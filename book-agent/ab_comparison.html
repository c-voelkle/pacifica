"*Hai*, Protector," Takashima replied in clipped, precise English. He stepped back, raised a small red signal pennon, and dropped it.

The breech-loader did not roar with the dull, booming thud of an Eastern smoothbore; it cracked like a giant whip, spitting a flat, vicious tongue of orange flame and a cloud of sharp blue smoke. A heartbeat later, four hundred yards down the glare-white plain, the top of the arsenal’s watchtower dissolved into a geyser of red dust and pulverized sun-dried brick. The heavy bronze mission bell, hung there by Spanish friars a century before, tore loose from its shattered crossbeam and plunged through the roof of the guardhouse with a brazen, discordant clang that echoed off the Santa Catalina mountains.

Before the brick-dust had settled back onto the prickly-pear, Elias touched his rowels to his horse’s flanks. He did not order the Lancers forward; he rode alone up the baked wagon-trace toward the gate, his hands resting loosely on his pommel, the hot wind lifting the skirts of his linen duster.

From the loopholed parapet above the gate, three rifle barrels poked out through the mud bricks, wavering in the heat-shimmer.

"Halt there!" bawled Major Crittenden’s voice from behind the timber parapet. The top of his foraging cap appeared over the adobe crest, white with powdered clay. "Halt, or I’ll give you a volley! You are on property of the United States War Department, Mr. Thorne! There is no truce between Washington and Sacramento for armed brigandage!"

Elias reined in thirty paces from the gate, right in the open beaten zone where a recruit could not miss a barn door. He looked up at the sweating face under the blue cap.

"There is no War Department within fifteen hundred miles of this ditch, Major," Elias called back, his voice carrying clearly across the dead quiet of the desert noon. "General Twiggs has already surrendered the Texas garrisons to the Southern secessionists. Colonel Loring resigned his commission in Santa Fe forty-eight hours ago to ride for Richmond. The pay-wagons from Fort Leavenworth were captured by Missouri bushwhackers on the Arkansas River last Tuesday. Whom are you holding these rifles for?"

Crittenden’s jaw worked behind his graying whiskers. "For the Union, damn your eyes! My oath is to the Constitution!"

"Your oath is to a government that has left seventy men in this oven with four days of salt pork and dry wells outside the wall," Elias said. He drew a folded sheet of heavy vellum from his breast pocket. "If I fire a second shell, Major, it will not be solid shot, and it will not be aimed at the bell-tower. It will be a percussion canister filled with rock-oil from the Santa Paula wells, dropped straight into your hay-corral. In twenty minutes your horses will be roast meat and your family will be choking in the cellar."

He paused, letting the dry rattle of a locust in the mesquite fill the silence between them.

"Or you can march your command out with colors flying and sidearms belted," Elias continued evenly. "I will buy your twelve thousand Springfields and your sixteen howitzers for thirty thousand dollars in stamped San Francisco gold coin, paid over the drumhead right now. Every enlisted man who wishes to go East gets a mule, ten days’ rations, and safe-conduct to the Rio Grande. Every man who wishes to trade that sweaty wool coat for Pacifica blue gets double pay and a bounty warrant in the Gila copper mines. You have three minutes before Lieutenant Takashima reloads."

Elias pulled his silver pocket-watch from his vest and clicked open the lid. He did not look up at the rifles again. He watched the blued-steel second hand sweep around the dial—ten seconds, twenty, forty—while from behind the mud wall came the low, fierce murmur of the regular sergeants arguing with their officers, followed by the sudden, sharp weeping of a woman in the courtyard.

At ninety seconds, the heavy iron crossbar behind the mesquite double-gates groaned and slid back. The timber leaves swung inward on dry, screaming hinges.

Major Crittenden walked out into the glare, his sword sheathed at his hip, his face gray beneath its sunburn. Behind him, four corporals dragged the first of the green-painted ordnance wagons by its tongue-chains.

"You are a thief with a ledger, Thorne," Crittenden said bitterly, stopping five paces from the nose of Elias’s horse and throwing the heavy iron ring of magazine keys into the alkali dust.

"No, Major," Elias replied, snapping his watch shut and nodding to Caleb Vance to bring up the bullion chests. "A thief takes what belongs to another man. I am merely picking up the tools that a bankrupt carpenter dropped in the road."

By the third night after the fall of Tucson, the captured Springfields were already rattling southward in forty mule-drawn wagons toward the dry, jagged hills of the Sonora line, while Elias rode ahead with a single troop of Lancers into the walled courtyard of the Hacienda San Bernadino—two leagues south of the international boundary stones.

Here the desert night was cool and fragrant with crushed lime-leaves, woodsmoke from mesquite braziers, and the pungent aroma of roasting goat-flesh and dark Veracruz cigars. Under the whitewashed colonnade of the patio, where twenty clay *ollas* sweated cold well-water in the lantern-light, Governor Ignacio Pesqueira of Sonora sat in a carved leather campaign chair, his crippled left leg propped upon an ammunition box that still bore the stenciled eagle of the United States Ordnance Department. Across the table from him, fanning away the moths with a silk handkerchief scented with sandalwood, sat Lady Kaiulani—having come up from the Pacifica gunboat *Hilo* at the port of Guaymas by fast mule-litter that very morning.

"Don Ignacio was just reminding me, Protector," Kaiulani said smoothly in Spanish as Elias strode up the tiled steps, stripping the desert gauntlets from his scarred hands, "that President Juárez in Mexico City still recognizes Mr. Lincoln’s minister, and that the French bondholders in Paris are threatening to send warships to Veracruz if Mexico displeases the Atlantic powers."

Pesqueira did not rise, though his dark, intelligent eyes flicked with undisguised respect toward the tall American whose boots were still white with the dust of the captured federal forts. Beside the Governor stood two French-trained Mexican artillery colonels and a pair of silk-hatted British copper-factors from Hermosillo.

"It is a matter of geography, *Señor* Protector," Pesqueira said, gesturing with his cigar toward the dark bulk of the Sierra Madre rising beyond the courtyard walls. "President Juárez is fighting the clerical reactionaries in the south; he has no pesos, and Mr. Seward in Washington has promised us a loan of ten million dollars if we allow Union troops to land at Guaymas and march up through Sonora to retake Arizona from your militia. A scrap of paper from Washington is worth little today, perhaps—but if the French Emperor lands troops at Veracruz this autumn, Mexico cannot afford to have the Yankees as enemies on our northern flank."
