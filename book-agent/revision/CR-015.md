## 1. Summary

The North makes peace after Lincoln loses the 1864 election, and the peace is built so that the Union cannot, will not and need not threaten Pacifica's rear for the following decades. Part III is rebuilt around a single objective, the transpacific empire, pursued mainly through friendship with Japan and China and a small number of surgical strikes, each taken when the owner of the prize is distracted. The Bering railway and the Lake Baikal campaign are removed. Pacifica takes the Inspectorate of the Chinese Maritime Customs from Britain in 1885. In 1894 Regent Kaiulani backs Japan's case for Korean independence and then brokers the armistice, so there is no Liaodong cession and no Triple Intervention. In 1898–99 Japan, with Pacifica holding the sea, takes Russia's Pacific coast.

In North America, a second Union attempt under Grant fails in 1870–71. Pacifica guarantees Confederate independence and binds the Confederacy by rail, debt, customs, coal and careers. The Union turns to non-military anti-slavery pressure, which produces a free-womb amendment in 1874–75 and abolition in 1888–89. By 1895 a free but dependent Confederacy stands in association with Pacifica, and Pacifica is the arbiter of a divided continent. In 1897–1900 the Confederacy splits: Virginia and North Carolina return to the Union, Texas, Louisiana and Arkansas, with Indian Territory, form the Federal Republic of the Gulf allied to Pacifica, and a rump Confederacy remains.

In the interior, the Mormons accede as the confederate state of Deseret in 1862–63, an expression of the religious freedom Pacifica grants to all. The 1865 line runs roughly along the continental divide: Pacifica holds the Great Basin, the Northwest, New Mexico, Arizona, Idaho, western Montana and southwest Wyoming, and leaves Colorado's Front Range and the Great Plains to the Union. Assisted immigration of women and families, with China, Japan and the Philippines as partners, peoples the new territories; over generations, Hapa marriages become as commonplace and symbolic in Pacifica as mulatto and mestizo unions in Brazil.

The Pacifica Power Timeline, 1865–1900, governs the sequence, cadence and guardrails of the period. This CR records the decisions behind it and the adjustments made to agree with it (section J).

## 3. Decisions

### A. The 1864 electoral upset and the peace (Part II §12; resolves part of unresolved decision 15)

**A1. The Union sues for peace after an electoral upset in 1864, and Pacifica must not have a hostile Union in its rear while it expands across the Pacific.** APPROVED

**A2. Why a simple "McClellan wins" is insufficient.** PROPOSED  
McClellan's acceptance letter of 8 September 1864 made Union the condition of any peace (Hist.). Lincoln remains president until 4 March 1865 and committed in his "blind memorandum" of 23 August to win the war first (Hist.). A peace treaty needs two-thirds of the Senate, which state legislatures keep Republican (Hist.). The upset therefore needs three layers: a summer of collapse, an armistice that needs no Senate vote, and lasting structural reasons for Union restraint.

**A3. Sequence, 1864–65.** PROPOSED

**A4. Narrative mirror.** Elias's dissolution election (§12) and Lincoln's defeat fall in the same winter: one democracy rewards continuity, the other punishes exhaustion. PROPOSED

**A5. Negotiating channel.** Sherman, Pacifica's Inspector-General and the advocate of modest terms, has a brother, John Sherman, sitting as Republican senator for Ohio (Hist.), in the body that must ratify the treaty. PROPOSED

**A6. Settlement terms with the Union.** PROPOSED
- **Puget Sound.** The surviving federal enclave from §10 is evacuated by treaty, and the Union leaves the Pacific coast entirely. This is the indispensable term: without it the Union keeps a Pacific naval base, Russia sells Alaska to Washington, and a Union squadron sits on the flank of Part III.
- **The conqueror pays.** Pacifica pays in gold for its Western gains ($40 million as a vendor credit paid in instalments to 1872, which keeps the early budget tight), following the 1848 precedent in which the victorious United States paid Mexico $15 million. It adds a gold loan to the Union Treasury toward resumption of specie payments. The McClellan government can call the outcome a sale; the Eastern financiers of §13 become partners; Elias, dispossessed by creditors in 1837, becomes the creditor of the United States. Seeds the "creditor empire" of Part VI.
- **Take less than you hold.** Pacifica keeps everything draining to the Pacific or into the Great Basin, plus New Mexico, the Missouri headwaters gold country and South Pass; the line is set out in H5. APPROVED (line) It leaves Colorado's Front Range, Denver and the Great Plains to the Union. The Plains remain a moat: a thousand miles with no Union railway to anything Pacifican worth reaching (the Kansas Pacific reached Denver only in 1870, Hist.). Denver becomes the Union's only mountain city, on the border.
- **Ratification of the line.** The Senate cedes Mormon country, Hispano country and mining camps settled from California and Oregon. Losing Utah is close to welcome: the 1856 Republican platform condemned polygamy as one of the "twin relics of barbarism" (Hist.). Colorado, whose volunteers won at Glorieta, stays in the Union, which removes the hardest cession. The price is the Power Timeline's $40 million vendor credit; the feasibility audit tests it against the Montana and Idaho goldfields.
- **Ratification in 1867.** The Senate ratifies the treaty in 1867, alongside Pacifica's purchase of Alaska, so the West is settled before Grant's 1868 campaign and his target stays Richmond. APPROVED
- **Split recognition.** The Senate ratifies the Pacifica treaty (distant, paid for, Oregon's politics favorable). Confederate independence is recognized only by the executive, by McClellan receiving a Confederate envoy. The Senate never ratifies surrender of the claim to the South. The Union accepts losing the far West but never the South, so its grievance points at Richmond, with a moral cause attached: four million people remain enslaved there.

**A7. Why the Union stays out of Pacifica's rear.** PROPOSED  
Because §12 keeps the Union's constitution, army and industry intact, restraint rests on money, geography, politics and distraction rather than collapse.
- *Capacity:* war debt (historically about $2.7 billion) without Southern trade or Western bullion; demobilization (historically the army fell to about 54,000 authorized men by 1866 and the navy was largely laid up even after victory); no Pacific port; no transcontinental line to build.
- *Will:* Democratic government 1865–69; a New York lobby profiting from Pacific transit and Pacifican loans; family ties to Pacifica's large American-born population.
- *Attention:* the unresolved claim on the South (border states, Mississippi navigation, fugitive slaves, freedmen in Union-held Louisiana and Tennessee); the Plains wars, which stay with the Union because the Lakota, Cheyenne and Arapaho live east of the 1865 line (Sand Creek 1864, Red Cloud's War 1866–68, the Bozeman Trail, Hist.; Montana is supplied from the west, so Pacifica has no use for the Bozeman Trail; a darker option is Pacifican rifles reaching the Lakota through Montana traders); Anglo-American quarrels (Fenian raids 1866–71, Hist.; Alabama claims left open because Britain recognized the Confederacy).

**A8. Plot clocks.** PROPOSED
- 1867, Alaska: with no Pacific port and a hostile Senate the Union cannot bid.
- 1868: a revanchist Republican, plausibly Grant, wins on restoring the Union. His target is Richmond. Russia, historically the Union's friend, courts Washington.
- War with Britain comes only after 1900, under the regency (G9); Britain's acceptance of Pacifica's position is fixed first, in the 1899–1900 settlement during the Boer War (J2). Union Anglophobia still points the Union at Canada rather than Pacifica; Elias can encourage this.
- The real long-term threat is a North–South reconciliation. It comes only in part: Virginia and North Carolina return to the Union in 1898–99, while the Gulf Republic moves into Pacifica's orbit (E11).

**A9. Postwar continental course.** The Confederacy slides into dependence on Pacifican credit and protection first. This is the route to the "contested local compacts" of §12 and to the "American military aristocrats" behind Silas in 1887. Developed in section E. APPROVED

**B1. Consolidation and the ocean road (1865–68).** No wars. Long-service naval core, debt refinanced, Northwest integrated. PROPOSED
- State-subsidized transpacific steam line San Francisco–Honolulu–Yokohama–Hong Kong (Pacific Mail opened this route in January 1867, Hist.).
- Midway claimed in 1867 (claimed by the US that year, Hist.).
- Alaska bought in 1867 for the route, not for gold: the great-circle route from San Francisco to Yokohama runs along the Aleutians, and purchase denies the territory to Britain. Russia sells because the Union has no Pacific port and no money (A8).
- Yukon gold in 1867 is removed as premature (Juneau 1880, Klondike 1896, Hist.).
- The Bering project is reduced to Perry Collins's Russian–American telegraph, under construction 1865–67 and abandoned after the Atlantic cable (Hist.).
- *Talent from two armies (1865–67).* Thousands of Confederates emigrated to Brazil and Mexico, and about fifty American officers served the Khedive from 1869 (Hist.). Pacifica offers land, rank and contracts to naval officers, ironclad builders and railway and West Point engineers from both sides, and keeps the Sierra railway's Chinese crews employed on interior lines. Risk: divided loyalties and Union anger. PROPOSED (Power Timeline)
- *Mexico (1865–67).* Arms and credit for Juárez through Sonora as France withdraws (Maximilian is shot in June 1867, Hist.). In return: the purchase of Baja California in 1867, a railway concession to Guaymas with a free port, and a joint Apache convention ending Mexico's scalp bounties (H6). Sonora is not annexed (G9). PROPOSED; price to the feasibility audit
- *The mail line* coals at Honolulu, Midway and Hakodate, so passengers, mail and silks keep moving through San Francisco after Suez opens in November 1869 (Hist.). PROPOSED

**B2. Japan (1868–72).** The 1868 "fleet before Edo" guarantees the transition rather than coercing a collapsing shogunate. PROPOSED

- Enomoto's Hakodate regime falls in 1869 (Hist.). The decisive ironclad *Kōtetsu*, historically the ex-CSS *Stonewall*, is supplied through Pacifica.
- Kuroda Kiyotaka heads the Hokkaido Development Commission and hires foreign advisers from 1871 (Hist.); here they are Pacifican. Founding figure of the later Kuroda line; brings Japan's northern frontier, and Russia, into the story.
- 1870–72: with France beaten by Prussia, Pacifica replaces the French army mission and pre-empts Britain's 1873 naval mission; Japanese officers train in Pacifica and Japanese yards build to Pacifican designs and calibres. Southern Sakhalin, traded to Russia in 1875 (Hist.), is restored to Japan; all of Sakhalin follows in 1899 (D7). The settlement is folded into the Congress of Berlin (June–July 1878), and Britain owes Pacifica its recognition of the Philippine purchase. PROPOSED

**C3. The Customs Inspectorate.** The Inspectorate of the Chinese Maritime Customs is an important prize and must not remain in British hands, especially since Pacifica can be more evenhanded in the role. APPROVED
- *Mechanism.* In 1885 Robert Hart was appointed British minister in Peking and briefly accepted; the succession question, with Li Hongzhang backing the German Gustav Detring, was one reason he stayed (Hist.). Here Hart takes the ministership and Li's candidate is a Pacifican, as China's reward for Keelung and the famine relief, for a small agency fee. The appointment belongs to China; Britain protests but has no veto, and its later demand that the post remain British (historically 1898) comes too late. PROPOSED
- *Evenhandedness.* Multinational staff kept; Chinese promoted into senior grades (largely excluded under Hart), drawing on Educational Mission alumni; published accounts; equal duties for every flag. PROPOSED
- *Power.* The Inspector-General sees all of China's foreign trade, collects the revenue that services foreign loans and runs the lighthouses, later the post. PROPOSED
- *The man.* Edmund Fong Kai-ming (方啟明, "opening light"), born in San Francisco in 1859 to a Taishan merchant family, bilingual and a Pacifican customs officer from 1878. In 1894, when Kaiulani backs Japan, the Inspectorate becomes his personal loyalty crisis and a live test of the Hapa thread (I4); his two names carry the split inside one man. APPROVED

**C4. Opium.** A self-imposed opium ban is not adopted without further justification. Proposed treatment: PROPOSED
- Pacifica cannot win the trade because Britain controls Indian supply, so staying out of it costs little. This is circumstance, not virtue.
- Pacifican merchants carry Turkish and Persian opium privately, as American houses did (Hist.). The state does not stop them.
- Under the 1885 additional article to the Chefoo Convention the customs collected inland opium tax together with duty (Hist.). A Pacifican Inspector-General collects it strictly, making China richer and British opium dearer.

### D. 1894–1899: Kaiulani backs Japan and brokers the peace; the Primorye war

**D1. In the Sino-Japanese War of 1894, Regent Kaiulani backs Japan's case for Korean independence, then brokers the armistice.** APPROVED

**D2. Rationale.** Japan is inside the empire's institutions (the Japanese naval sphere of the proposed spring 1887 settlement under CR-016) and is the coherent military power; the Qing court diverts naval funds from 1888 and will crush its reformers in 1898 (Hist.). Consistent with the master storyline's Japanese pillar, Fleet-General Kuroda and Lady Chiyo. PROPOSED

**D3. Form.** Support short of open war, in Kaiulani's style rather than Elias's: coal, equipment, credit and diplomatic cover; no Pacifican combat. APPROVED

**D4. The armistice and its terms (October–November 1894).** APPROVED unless marked
- *Anchor:* the Donghak rising brings Chinese and Japanese troops into Korea in mid-1894; Japan wins at Pyongyang and the Yalu in September; Cixi wants peace before her sixtieth birthday in November and China seeks mediators, while Japan fears a European intervention (Hist.).
- *Terms:* Korea independent and neutral under the guarantee of China, Japan and Pacifica, with Japanese economic predominance (the Seoul–Busan railway, mines and ports); China gives up suzerainty; a moderate indemnity; the commercial rights Japan won at Shimonoseki; no mainland territory. Pacifica guarantees the terms against any European challenge.
- *Consequences:* no Port Arthur massacre, no Liaodong cession and no Triple Intervention, so Russia loses its pretext. China is not bankrupted and Cixi stays a friend. Queen Min is not murdered and King Gojong does not flee to the Russian legation, since both followed historically from Japan's victory and Russia's rise (Hist.).
- *Money:* a smaller indemnity facility for China, still secured on the customs revenue Pacifica's Inspector-General collects. A Pacifican gold loan to Japan, held with the indemnity in San Francisco, funds Japan's move to gold in 1897, which historically rested on the indemnity kept in London. Japan's currency rests on Pacifican deposits. PROPOSED
- *Risk:* Japan's army and public feel robbed of victory, as after the 1905 peace in history. Their outlet is the Primorye war (D7).
- *Formosa.* Pacifica takes control, for its camphor, the raw material of celluloid and film, and for Keelung coal, at the least cost and friction. APPROVED (control) Form: sovereignty stays Chinese, so no port is taken from China (G9). China secures its indemnity on Formosa's revenues, and a Pacifican administration of the camphor monopoly, the Keelung coal and the island's customs, on the Inspectorate model, collects them. Japan, which has wanted Formosa since 1874, is paid from that revenue instead of receiving the island; the tension with "never take a prize from Japan" has to be played, not ignored. APPROVED (form, 18 Oct)

**D5. Resonance.** In 1881 King Kalākaua proposed a Japanese prince as husband for the historical Princess Kaʻiulani, as part of a union of Asian nations (Hist.). The 1914 Thorne-Kamehameha settlement with a Japanese consort quietly fulfills it. PROPOSED (optional)

**D6. No Triple Intervention.** Without a Liaodong cession, Russia, France and Germany have nothing to reverse in April 1895. The resolution of 17 October (Liaodong returned for a larger indemnity) is withdrawn (§6). Japanese resentment of a victory cut short still feeds the Kuroda faction's later demands for autonomy inside the empire. APPROVED

**D7. The Primorye war (1897–99).** APPROVED
- *Anchor:* Germany seizes Kiaochow in November 1897 and Russia demands an ice-free port. Russia's Far East has no rail link to Europe until about 1903; the Ussuri line joins Vladivostok only to Khabarovsk, in 1897, and Vladivostok has about 29,000 people (Hist.).
- *Trigger:* Russia demands Port Arthur or a Korean harbour, against the 1887 and 1894 guarantees.
- *Conduct:* Japan fights on land in 1898–99. Pacifica holds the sea so that Russia cannot reinforce through Suez, and lends the money; China stays benevolently neutral. There is no Pacifican land army (G9).
- *Settlement:* Japan takes the Ussuri region with Vladivostok and the Razdolnaya valley, and all of Sakhalin; its economic predominance in Korea is confirmed; China recovers the Amur's north bank. Pacifica takes no port in China and declares against partition.
- *Consequences:* Russia loses its Pacific coast, the Chinese Eastern Railway loses its purpose and Kiaochow is isolated. Japan needs Pacifica's guarantee against Russian revenge indefinitely. Japan prevents Trans-Siberian completion under the later direct decision CR-016-R1. Russian revenge remains a possibility requiring a new causal and logistical basis; no completed-route trigger is retained. France stays neutral. Its 1894 alliance with Russia is aimed at Germany, and from September 1898 France is caught in the Fashoda crisis with Britain and in the Dreyfus affair (Hist.). Paris keeps lending to St Petersburg, so Japan is in effect fighting French money, and Pacifica's credit is the answer. APPROVED

### E. The North American course, 1865–1900 (Part II §12 and later sections)

**E1. Target picture for 1895: a divided continent in which Pacifica is the only power with friends on both sides, and the arbiter between them.** APPROVED
- *The Confederacy* is independent in name and associated with Pacifica in fact. Slavery is abolished. It is bound by debt, customs co-administration, rail and careers. No Confederate state is yet a Pacifican member. Accession becomes possible only after the 1887 imperial constitution, when membership no longer carries democratic weight at the center, and the statistics bureau's thresholds decide it (J2). In 1895 the Gulf states sign an economic compact with Pacifica, the first step toward the Gulf Republic of 1899–1900 (E11).
- *The Union* remains intact and the largest power on the continent (plausibly 40–45 million people against about 15 million in the Confederacy). A generation after 1865 its grievance has aged into a legal claim no majority will fight for. In the February 1895 Treasury gold crisis (Hist.: rescued by the Morgan–Belmont syndicate) the gold is Pacifican; the [current financial scenario](../../outputs/pacifica-finances-20261018/financial_reconciliation.md) retains a conditional $65m syndicate with 15% public participation, without approving those figures as canon. Its price is Union recognition of Pacifica's canal rights, neutrality in any Asian war and tariff concessions (PROPOSED). The Union is quarrelling with Britain (Venezuela boundary crisis, 1895, Hist.) and has no Pacific coast.
- *The balance:* Pacifica guarantees Confederate independence. The Union knows an attack on Richmond means war with Pacifica; the guarantee is also Pacifica's leverage in Richmond.
- *Why not the alternatives:* full reunion creates a continental power of 60 million (the partial return of Virginia and North Carolina in 1898–99 is accepted, E11); annexation by 1895 would let some 15 million Southern citizens swamp Pacifica's North American electorate and bring its race politics with them; further break-up of the Union is unrealistic and invites European intervention; an outright Confederate victory would leave the Confederacy stronger and less dependent.
- *Clocks still running in 1895:* the Union naval revival (the "new navy" authorized 1883, Mahan's book on sea power 1890, Hist.) and Cuba's war of independence from February 1895 (Hist.).

**E2. The 1865 armistice line.** The Union keeps Tennessee and the Mississippi from Memphis to Vicksburg. The border is long and porous, with Union freedmen's communities along it; the abolition mechanism of E5 depends on it. APPROVED
- *Black Union soldiers* stay in service as regulars garrisoning the Tennessee border, and freedmen receive homesteads along it. Together they become the engine of the escape networks in E5. APPROVED
- *Indian Territory goes to the Confederacy.* APPROVED (18 Oct) The Five Tribes signed alliances with the Confederacy in 1861, with delegates in the Confederate Congress; the Union held the Cherokee and Creek north from 1863, and Stand Watie was the last Confederate general to surrender, in June 1865 (Hist.). The 1865 armistice freezes that division along the Arkansas River. In the spring 1871 border convention the Union cedes the north as well, the only territory it gives up after Grant's war, so all of Indian Territory except the Public Land Strip is Confederate; the strip stays with the Union and the Plains. The cession hands the Cherokee to Watie's southern party and leaves the old Union party of John Ross with a grievance. The tribes keep slavery until Confederate abolition in 1888–89, and the adoption of their freedmen becomes a fight of the 1890s. The Union's Boomers, who historically pushed into the Unassigned Lands from 1879 (Hist.), now press across a Confederate border from Kansas. The northern cession matters most for the oil: the first commercial well, at Bartlesville in the Cherokee Nation in 1897, and Glenn Pool in the Creek Nation in 1905 (Hist.) lie in the land the Union gives up. PROPOSED (mechanism)
- *The Power Timeline* assumes that Tennessee leaves the Confederacy in 1898–99 and tells the reader to adjust to the armistice map. Under this line Tennessee is already Union, so the Upper South that leaves is Virginia and North Carolina (E11).

**E5. How the Union does the heavy lifting.** After 1870 force has failed, and a second attack means war with Pacifica. An anti-slavery cold war lets the Union keep fighting the Confederacy without fighting Pacifica, and it reunites a party split by reconquest. Its weapons are a free-soil border that turns every mile into an exit, citizenship for refugees, market discrimination against slave-grown cotton at home and in London, moral campaigning in Europe, and the example of free labor next door. Each Union success makes the Confederacy poorer, more frightened and more dependent on Pacifican credit: the Union frees the South's enslaved people and delivers the freed South into Pacifica's association. **E6. The roles are split: the naval station is in lower Mobile Bay, and Galveston is the commercial port.** APPROVED
- *Mobile Bay as naval station:* PROPOSED
  - *Shelter.* The anchorage lies in the protected lower bay near Fort Morgan, not on an exposed barrier island. The deep water is at the entrance; the city, at the head of a shallow bay, is reached by lighter and rail. Hurricane risk was visible to planners in 1871: the 1875 storm destroyed the Texas port of Indianola (Hist.).
  - *Coal.* Mobile is Alabama's port, so Birmingham's coal and iron reach the station by river and rail, fixing Pacifica's oldest weakness at the base itself (E3).
  - *Position.* Slightly closer than Galveston to Havana, the Florida Straits and the approaches to the canal.
  - *Cost.* Union-held Pensacola, with its navy yard, lies about fifty miles east. Elias reads the proximity as deterrence; his critics read it as exposure.
- *Galveston as commercial port:* Texas's outlet and the link to the West, and the Gulf Republic's main port after 1900 (E11). Pacifican engineers build the bar jetties earlier than their historical completion around 1896, and a seawall in the 1890s, which historically was built only in 1902–04, after the storm. APPROVED (seawall) The September 1900 hurricane, which destroyed Galveston and killed at least 6,000 people (Hist.), strikes a protected city in the year the canal opens. PROPOSED
- *Dependency:* Mobile must remain Confederate at the 1865 armistice. Resolved by section F: the Union attack on Mobile Bay fails in February 1865.
- *Excluded:* Pensacola, the Gulf's best natural harbor, was Union-held from 1862 and stays Union under E2.

**E7. Fugitives and Pacifican soil.** By agreement with the Confederacy, Pacifica prevents enslaved people from fleeing to its territory, and enforces the agreement so as not to antagonize its partner. APPROVED
- *Mechanism:* Pacifica's founding law prohibits slavery and its 1846 bill of rights guarantees habeas corpus, so Pacifica cannot return people as property. It acts through border and immigration control instead: persons arriving from the Confederacy without Confederate travel papers are refused entry and handed back at the Texas border, on the Sunset Route, at Galveston and at the Mobile Bay station. Signed with the 1871–72 association treaties. PROPOSED
- *The court test:* a Pacifican court frees a returned fugitive on habeas corpus. The cabinet overrides the ruling under the 1865 enabling law, which allows cabinet legislation departing from specified constitutional provisions. It is an early, quiet use of that power. Kaiulani objects to the policy; Sherman objects to the use of the enabling law rather than to the policy. PROPOSED
- *Consequences:* every escape route points north to the Union, strengthening E5. Abolitionists in the Union and Britain call Pacifica an accomplice. Pacifica's Chinese and Hawaiian citizens draw their own conclusions about whose rights are secure. Mexico and Canada historically refused to return fugitives (Hist.); Pacifica chooses differently. The agreement lapses with abolition in 1888–89. PROPOSED
- *After the lapse:* the 1888 switch to selective immigration (I3) means the lapse does not open Pacifica's door to the freed. Critics, Frederick Douglass among them, call the policy anti-Black racism; a quiet understanding with Gulf planters who fear losing their labor after abolition may be the real motive, and the novel can leave it open. APPROVED (Power Timeline)

**E10. The Union, 1872–1900.** APPROVED unless marked
- *1872–77:* the Greeley affair, a deeper Long Depression, the disputed election of 1876 and the 1877 strike (E4). Pacifica stays out, recruits and publishes. The Union is discredited without a shot.
- *1890:* Wounded Knee, photographed and campaigned on by the prestige weekly (J2).
- *1893–94:* the Panic, Coxey's Army and Pullman. The statistics bureau's wage and unemployment tables, newsreels of Pacifican works and relief, and open recruitment of Union skilled workers let the Union's own voters make the comparison.
- *1894, the Bread March:* with Pacifica's door closed since 1888 and the Union in depression, industrial armies gather in Colorado while its populist governor, Davis Waite, looks away, and armed men try to force the passes; a border post is attacked and men die. Pacifica detains the marchers by the hundreds and tries the armed men fairly, on Pacifican film. Revanchism and silver populism are tied to a bloody fiasco.
- *1895:* the gold rescue and its price (E1).
- *1896:* William C. Whitney, Navy Secretary in 1885–89 and leader of the gold Democrats (Hist.), is nominated and beats McKinley's platform of tariffs and recovering the West, with a supportive House; the Senate follows after the 1898 state elections. His policy: compete with Pacifica, don't fight it; neutrality in the Primorye war; Union capital in Pacifican projects; a recovery from 1897 fed by Klondike and South African gold passing through Pacifican banks.
- *1897:* the Bread March prisoners are returned to the Union in a prisoner deal, against a commerce and arbitration treaty with Pacifica, Whitney's first diplomatic success.

**E11. The Confederacy splits, 1889–1900.** APPROVED (split and Gulf Republic); PROPOSED (dates)
- *Pressures:* abolition removes the Confederacy's founding purpose; the Panic bankrupts cotton and Pacifica's guarantee pays the missed coupons; Whitney's Union offers the Upper South a way home; single six-year presidential terms make the 1897 election the breaking point.
- *Sequence:* 1891, a Deep South nationalist wins the presidency; 1895, the Gulf economic compact; 1897, the election deadlocks between the Gulf, Deep South and Upper South blocs, and Whitney offers the Upper South a customs union; 1898–99, Virginia and North Carolina leave and negotiate reunion with the Union while Pacifica stays neutral; 1899–1900, Texas, Louisiana and Arkansas secede and form the Gulf Republic, taking Indian Territory with them as the state of Sequoyah, the name the Five Tribes historically proposed for their own state in 1905 (Hist.), with a trade treaty and a mutual defence pact with Pacifica, and Pacifican ships at Mobile deter Richmond from marching on Louisiana; from 1900 a rump Confederacy of South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and Florida survives, tied to Lancashire's cotton. - *Adjustment to E2:* Tennessee is Union under the 1865 line, so it is not among the states that leave. Arkansas, which the Power Timeline does not name, joins the Gulf Republic for its rail and river ties to Louisiana and Texas. Alabama, which the Power Timeline places in its Gulf Federation, stays with the rump: its ties run to Georgia and Mississippi, and the Gulf Republic is in effect the old Trans-Mississippi Department (Hist.) with New Orleans. APPROVED
- *The Federal Republic of the Gulf* (República Federal del Golfo, République fédérale du Golfe), "the Gulf Republic" for short: one name in the region's three languages, with no echo of either the Confederacy or the Union. APPROVED Austin is the capital and New Orleans the economic centre. Its constitution follows Pacifica's model: a strong executive, a merit civil service, independent courts and equal civil rights, with a narrow franchise earned by examination that votes only in certain referendums. It has its own citizenship, so there is no free movement into Pacifica before accession, and the Gulf Republic's ports, Galveston above all, are open to Pacifica's fleet under the defence pact.
- *Oil.* The Gulf Republic is born an oil republic: Sequoyah's fields, found earlier with Pacifican survey help, and in its first weeks Spindletop at Beaumont, Texas, in January 1901 (Hist.). Pacifica's fuel-oil programme (J2) supplies the technology and the buyer, so the oil ends with Pacifica's partner rather than in Pacifica itself, and the partnership gains a material core beyond the shared enemy. ### F. The Gulf campaign, 1861–1865 (Part II §§10–11)

**F1. Constraints.** APPROVED
- *Pensacola replaces New Orleans as the Union's Gulf base.* Confederate troops leave Pensacola in early 1862 to concentrate for Shiloh, and the Union occupies it in May. With Ship Island and Key West (Union-held from 1861–62, Hist.) it carries Farragut's operations. Union forces in the Gulf stay small; without a New Orleans base there is no 1864 Red River campaign.
- *The Union's river ironclads cannot reach the Gulf.* Eads's twin-turret river monitors (commissioned 1864; two fought at Mobile Bay historically) are built at St Louis and trapped above Confederate Port Hudson. With Ericsson in Pacifica, the ocean-going Tecumseh-class monitors that led Farragut into Mobile Bay do not exist (canon: no Ericsson-derived production). Armored support must come by sea from an Atlantic fleet that needs it at Charleston and Hampton Roads. This is the material cause of the canonical delay at Mobile.
- *Pacifica's supply route runs through Matamoros.* As a belligerent, Pacifica's ships are fair game on the blockade. Credit and materials go round Cape Horn to the neutral Mexican Rio Grande and overland into Texas, the real blockade-runners' route.

### H. The Great Basin and the interior, 1857–1865 (Part I open decisions; Part II §§8–12)

### I. Peopling the West: women, families and Asian partners (1857–1895 and beyond)

**I0. Decisions.** APPROVED
- The assisted immigration of women, and the other measures that raise population, extend to the new territories, which need them most. China, Japan and the Philippines assist as partners.
- Over a few generations of family migration and integration from Asia, Hapa marriages become as commonplace in Pacifica, and as much a symbol of it, as mulatto and mestizo unions are in Brazil.

**I1. The problem.** PROPOSED
- Mining societies were overwhelmingly male: California in 1850 was over 90% male (Hist.), and the interior territories were several men to one woman in 1870. Without women there are no families, no natural increase and no settled loyalty.
- The interior's Chinese were already numerous and almost all men: in 1870 Chinese made up nearly 30% of Idaho's population (Hist.). The program's first task is to turn sojourners into settlers.

**I2. Instruments.** APPROVED (extension to the new territories); PROPOSED (instruments)
- *Assisted passage for women.* Precedents: the filles du roi of New France (1663–73), Australian assisted female emigration from the 1830s, and Asa Mercer's parties of women brought to Seattle in 1864 and 1866 (Hist.). Here the Mercer parties are a Pacifican program, and the model is extended inland.
- *Family land law.* On the model of Oregon's Donation Land Claim Act of 1850 (Hist.): a larger grant for a married couple than for a single man, with the wife holding her half in her own name. Unlike the historical act, there is no racial bar, so the law rewards marriage, including intermarriage.
- *The confederal marriage floor.* Member states, Deseret included, keep their marriage law but may not forbid marriage on grounds of race or origin. Children born in Pacifica are citizens. Historically California banned marriages between whites and Chinese from 1880 (Hist.); Pacifica never does. The floor forbids only bans by race or origin. The first test case, in the 1870s, is a Mormon–Shoshone or Mormon–Chinese marriage that goes from a Deseret court to the confederal court, putting federalism against the Hapa ideal on stage. APPROVED
- *Carrying capacity:* state irrigation districts in the 1870s, ahead of California's Wright Act of 1887 (Hist.), drawing on Mormon irrigation practice; railways into the interior from the west; homesteads.
- *Religious freedom as recruitment* (H1).

**I3. Asian partners.** APPROVED (partners); PROPOSED (mechanisms)
- *China.* The 1868 treaty (C2) includes a family article: Qing consent to the emigration of wives and families, with brokers licensed jointly by Pacifican consuls and the district associations, which gives a recurring cast of brokers as antagonists. APPROVED Custom kept most wives in the home villages (Hist.), so the first families come from groups that already migrated as families, above all the Hakka, whose families went to Hawaii and Borneo with Basel Mission sponsorship (Hist.). There is no Page Act (1875) and no Exclusion Act (1882). When the Union, the Australian colonies and Canada close their doors in 1880–85 (Hist.), Pacifica stays open to Chinese families, merchants and capital and offers naturalization; a Chinese merchants' bank in San Francisco handles the diaspora's remittances. PROPOSED (Power Timeline) The brokers' trade in women, which flourished in historical San Francisco, is the program's dark side and ties to the Part I broker problem.
- *Japan.* Government-sponsored emigration, historically begun only in 1885 (about 29,000 to Hawaii by 1894, Hist.), starts in the late 1860s. The Wakamatsu Tea and Silk Colony, founded near Coloma in 1869 by refugees from Aizu after the Boshin War and failed by 1871 (Hist.), succeeds with Pacifican support and becomes the model. Arranged "picture" marriages, historically 1908–20 (Hist.), come a generation earlier. Japanese farmers settle the new irrigation districts.
- *The Philippines.* After the Spanish settlement of 1873–76 (B3), Catholic, partly Spanish-speaking Filipino families settle in New Mexico and Arizona, where church, language and Spanish-derived law already fit. A trickle of reformers and families follows after 1876, with larger flows only once the insurgency ebbs in the 1880s; the insurgency remains the pacing constraint. APPROVED
- *Interior hotspots:* the Rock Springs massacre (1885, 28 Chinese miners killed) and the Hells Canyon massacre (1887) (Hist.) fall on Pacifican soil. Neither is prevented; both are punished promptly. The trials are what make the program credible, and they give Pacifica the clean record it needs before the 1890 Wounded Knee campaign (J3). APPROVED
- *1888: from open borders to selective immigration.* Kaiulani replaces the early republic's open door with a programme of skilled migrants and sponsored families. The family programme with China, Japan and the Philippines continues; unskilled drifters, above all from the Union, are refused. Selection lifts every figure the statistics bureau reports. APPROVED (Power Timeline) It narrows the 1868 labor clause (C1), and it is the policy critics set against the lapse of the fugitive agreement (E7).

**I4. Hapa as Pacifica's identity.** APPROVED (direction); PROPOSED (detail)
- *The word is already Pacifican:* Hawaiian *hapa*, from English "half." In 19th-century Hawaii, Chinese men married Hawaiian women in large numbers (Hist.); the Californio society of 1846 was itself largely mestizo. Pacifica begins with two mixing traditions and adds a third.
- *The dynasty embodies it.* Elias and Kaiulani's children are hapa haole; the Japanese consort of the 1914 Thorne-Kamehameha settlement (D5) carries it into Asia.
- *Pace.* By 1895 the first generation of mixed children is coming of age; the norm and the symbol belong to the 1900s–1920s.
- *The Brazilian parallel cuts both ways.* Brazil's celebrated mixture coexisted with a policy of "whitening" through European immigration and a firm color hierarchy; Gilberto Freyre's 1933 image of a "racial democracy" was later widely criticized (Hist.). To be believable, Pacifica's Hapa ideal needs its own tension: nativist politics (the historical Workingmen's Party of 1877), the interior massacres (I3), and the 1871–89 fugitive agreement (E7). The agreement stays an open wound that Kaiulani and Chinese Pacificans name aloud; its lapse in 1889 becomes the moment the Hapa ideal is proclaimed. APPROVED The proclamation comes the year after the switch to selective immigration (I3), and critics will hold the two side by side.

## 6. Alternatives considered and not adopted

- **Permanent division of North America as the whole of Elias's continental policy.** Replaced by A9: division first, then gradual absorption of the Confederacy, to fit §12's compacts and the 1887 succession.
- **A war against China in 1884–85 for Korea, Formosa and the customs.** Replaced by friendship and the Keelung intervention.
- **A self-imposed opium ban as moral policy.** Not adopted; see C4.
- **A cold armed border without a second war.** Replaced by Grant's failed test of 1870–71 (E4).
- **Freedom for enslaved people who reach Pacifican soil,** as Mexico and Canada historically refused to return fugitives. Not adopted by author decision; see E7.
- **All of Arizona, New Mexico, Colorado, Wyoming, Montana and Idaho to Pacifica.** Not adopted in favor of the continental divide (H5): Colorado fought for the Union and would be the hardest cession, and the Plains would hand Pacifica the Union's Indian wars and an open eastern front.
- **A strict continental-divide line in Montana.** Not adopted: the 1860s gold camps lie on the Missouri side; the line follows the mountain front instead (H5).
- **Galveston as the Gulf naval station.** Not adopted: exposed to hurricanes and far from Birmingham coal; it becomes the commercial port instead (E6).
- **A Liaodong compromise after a Triple Intervention** (resolution of 17 October). Withdrawn in favour of the Power Timeline's brokered armistice of 1894 (D4, D6).
- **Pacifica takes Formosa and the Pescadores by cession.** Not adopted: it would take a port from China and a prize from Japan (G9); control comes through revenue administration instead (D4).
- **The Long Walk followed by a return in 1865** (Power Timeline). Not adopted by author decision of 18 October: the Long Walk is avoided by treaty (H6).
- **Breckinridge as Confederate president, 1874–75.** Not adopted: the eligibility fight over Kentucky takes the plot too far off course (E4).
- **A neutral commonwealth of Sequoyah in Indian Territory under a three-power guarantee.** Not adopted by author decision of 18 October: Indian Territory goes to the Confederacy, so its oil ends with Pacifica's partner in the Gulf Republic (E2, E11).
- **Kaiulani backs China in 1894.** Case: protect the Chinese investment, contain the Japanese faction inside the empire, a larger long-term prize, and a choice that suits a Hawaiian who knows what it means to be the weaker partner. Consequences: an early Anglo-Japanese alignment (historically 1902) against Pacifica, a Japanese fifth column inside the empire, a rebuild of the Kuroda line and Lady Chiyo, and a bet on a declining dynasty. Retained as a road not taken, argued in Kaiulani's council.
