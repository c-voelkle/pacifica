## Source paragraphs

**CR-022 integration:** [Silas, Abigail Merritt and Donner Pass](CR-022.md), revision 3. Silas is Elias's eldest son, born in 1851 to Abigail Merritt, the pastor's daughter; Kiyoshi becomes the second son, reversing CR-016's seniority. At Donner Silas fires at Soberanes and Caleb dies in his place; the lie holds. Silas confesses to Victoria in 1912 and dies that winter. Where CR-016's summary above calls Kiyoshi the eldest son, CR-022 governs.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** The pastor whose nose Elias broke takes in the widow and her son. His daughter Abigail, two years older than the boy, keeps the house while Mrs Thorne sinks under the loss; she feeds him, mends for him, reads with him and scolds him. He finds in her the mother that grief has taken from him.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** Years later Abigail Merritt says her dying father told her to marry the boy he had taken in. Only she heard it. The story is persuasive, flattering and impossible for the Chronicler to verify; its source is her own papers, kept with the Thorne family Bible and the letters Elias's mother wrote to a son they never reached. Whether the pastor admired Elias, forgave him, or was remembered according to his daughter's needs remains uncertain.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** In 1849 the Rev. Silas Merritt brings a Methodist mission to Sacramento, with his daughter Abigail, unmarried at twenty-seven. She seeks out the famous young man she once mothered; she alone calls him Eli. Her father dies in the cholera of 1850, telling her to marry him. They become lovers, but in 1851 Elias sails for Honolulu as the princes' guest, drawn by a larger world than Sacramento's settlers. Abigail finds that she is pregnant and does not tell him. Only Caleb knows. She marries Henry Pruett, a San Antonio man who shared the Mexican captivity, and goes home to Texas, where Silas is born late in 1851, legitimate in law as Pruett's son.

**[PROPOSED]** A Pacifican California column establishes control of Arizona and New Mexico in 1862; Richmond renounces its Arizona claim in return for credit and the future railway connection. **[PROPOSED — CR-022]** Before the renunciation the column drives Baylor's Texans from Mesilla in a brief clash both governments prefer to forget. Among the Texan dead is Captain Henry Pruett of San Antonio. Idaho's mining districts turn toward the Northwest in 1863, western Montana's camps toward Salt Lake and Walla Walla in 1863–1864. Washoe is retaken in 1863–1864. Washington still admits Nevada in October 1864 to claim its electoral votes, then loses it in the peace. Exact forces, routes, accession instruments and the new members' representation remain **[OPEN]**; civilian and Native authority require their own agreements.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** Early in 1863 Elias goes to Texas in person to settle the Arizona renunciation and the Matamoros supply terms. Caleb insists that they carry Pruett's watch to his widow together. Abigail opens the door; behind her stands an eleven-year-old with Elias's build. Elias learns he has a son in the week the boy learns who his father is, from the men whose soldiers killed the father who raised him. She will not come to California and takes money only for the boy. She asks for the name, and Elias refuses it.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** The examinations reach the officer corps in mid-career. Many of the old army's fighting officers fail them. Silas fails the mathematics and is capped below general rank; Elias refuses to intervene. The American officers' grievance against the examination state has his face.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** One armed standoff at Donner Pass in October 1887 tests the pact. The Polytechnic corps comes up to take over the Sierra telegraph under its charter; Silas's veterans hold the summit as the inland command's line. Caleb brokers a parley in the telegraph hut inside the snowshed. Before it, Abigail has told her son that Soberanes must not come down from that pass. When Soberanes says that Elias's last instruction to him was that the wire must not be divided, Silas draws and fires at him. Caleb steps across and takes the bullet. Dying in Silas's arms, he says: "Don't give it." Silas's officers wait for the order to advance, and he does not give it. Soberanes dictates the first dispatch: shot from outside the shed. The inquest finds a person unknown. Every man in the room is bound by the lie, and Silas is praised for the restraint of a man who fired. There is no campaign, general mutiny or civil war. Caleb's sacrifice remains the working implementation of the managed-succession direction.

Victoria governs through bargaining. Elias’s enabling-law delegation lapses at the beginning of the regency, reassuring the factions while limiting her instruments. **[APPROVED — CR-022]** The 1888 restriction is presented as Silas's price, but it is what Victoria wanted, and she holds the Donner report. He takes the blame. In the Bread March of 1894 his border command detains the marchers, and he insists that they be fed and tried, not shot. Pacifica consolidates through 1887–1889, with foreign military adventures paused around the succession. The compact begins a relationship whose later Concord will depend on Japanese consent and on different understandings of who has gained command.

**[APPROVED — CR-022]** Silas's nativists raise a school-segregation crisis in 1906. His former adjutant Harlan Tate now runs Sacramento; his board segregates Japanese pupils and revokes the licence of the mission school Silas's mother endowed. Silas, declining into drink, lends the crisis his name and is privately ashamed of it. Roosevelt courts Tokyo and sends the Great White Fleet around Cape Horn to Yokohama, while his immigration restrictions make the courtship unreliable. A more confident Japan bargains hard in the 1907 compact renewal, securing Kiyoshi as Japanese Fleet-General, a joint war council, family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans.

In 1905–1906 Julian spends a year in Tokyo attached to the legation and navy, meeting Saionji as he becomes prime minister. Victoria's language education gives the nineteen-year-old access beyond interpreters and formal audiences. Coming of age in 1907 gives him no crown: the constitution leaves its establishment to an Act of Settlement, and Victoria delays while the eldest son lives. **[APPROVED — CR-022]** In 1909 Silas's younger daughter marries Lieutenant Commander Tanabe Makoto of Kiyoshi's staff in the chapel of her grandmother's mission school; the Fleet-General comes to Sacramento, and the two sons denied their father's name meet for the only time. In the autumn of 1912 Silas comes to Thalassia and confesses Donner to Victoria, who has known for twenty-five years; he dies at Sacramento that winter. In 1913 an examined officer receives the inland command. The Act of Settlement waits another year, because the founder's second son now claims to be his eldest.

**[PROPOSED — CR-022]** At his accession the regency's sealed papers pass to Julian. Looking for the truth of his mother's death, he finds instead the truth of Donner, kept by the woman who raised him. He tells no one that he knows.

**[PROPOSED — OPEN-018-18]** Julian approves a falsified report to protect the approaching treaty. Vance signs it, then tells Victoria the truth. She confronts her adopted son over the attack and the lie, renouncing her remaining political role in 1921 at about eighty-three. **[PROPOSED — CR-022]** He knows that she kept the Donner lie for twenty-five years. Whether he says so is **[OPEN-022-12]**. She withholds a public denunciation to preserve the settlement, becoming complicit in the silence she condemns. Vance retains command, but neither relationship recovers its former trust.

The Senate votes the rescue loan but refuses the treaty: Pacifica would pay sixty-five percent of common costs, Japan's mass threatens dilution, and county boards want leverage against the Commissioners. **[APPROVED — CR-022]** Harlan Tate, Sacramento's senator and Silas's old adjutant, leads the refusal, adding that foreign coupon-clippers already draw nearly $300 million a year from Pacifican industry. Julian claims provisional application under the treaty prerogative pending consent, though the Senate has already refused. This is illegal in substance. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The Hawaiian and Philippine delegations to Ka Lōkahi declare for the Concord. The declaration gives Julian no legal authority, but it shows who holds the order together. The same year Ka Lōkahi takes the Marianas, Carolines and Palau into its trust, **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** with Tutuila, and the navy keeps Guam's harbour on lease. Japan's Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council's conditional advice and the Diet votes its quota; its side follows Japanese law and can lapse if Pacifica never obtains consent.

Victoria supports the Concord and opposes its illegal imposition. In 1928 she wins two-thirds Senate consent and an Act of Indemnity. **[PROPOSED — CR-022]** She calls on Tate once at Sacramento; neither mentions Donner. His bloc splits and he abstains. Julian accepts a five-year first term instead of ten, quota review at renewal, Pacifica's delegates chosen half by each chamber and a Polytechnic audit reported to the Senate. The constitutional case at Monterey is mooted before judgment. The legal cure gives the friendship institutions that can outlast it.
