Hirohito becomes Emperor Shōwa in December 1926. Japan enters the spring prosperous, its Treasury full, but its international banks and trading houses still carry the wartime industrial debts, renewed year after year, and the losses of the earthquake. The dollars Japan earned in the war are almost gone; the debts it ran up in the war are still there. When the bad loans come to light, confidence collapses: foreign correspondents discover that several banks have lent to the same borrowers against the same collateral. Owners lose their capital, depositors queue for cash, and foreign lenders refuse to renew most of their short money. London's acceptance houses are the first to pull out, and Tokyo remembers it. Takahashi's three-week moratorium buys time to examine the banks, rebuild them and find a way to pay abroad.

**[MODEL — CR-024]** About $400m of losses wipe out the affected banks' owners, and Japan puts in $400m of new capital; the Bank of Japan lends $295m. Three-quarters of $2bn in short foreign funding is not renewed. Outside Pacifican hands, London acceptance houses and British overseas banks hold about $400m of it, Union banks $250m, continental banks $100m and Chinese and Indian banks in Shanghai and Hong Kong $50m. Keeping six weeks of imports in reserve requires about $497m from abroad.
