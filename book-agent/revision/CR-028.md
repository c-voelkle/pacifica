## Source paragraphs

**CR-028 integration:** [The bargain of 1847–1848](CR-028.md). Washington accepts California's independence in three steps: the suspension of April 1847, Mexico's recognition at Querétaro in December 1847, and Polk's acceptance of Trist's treaty and the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention in February 1848. California gives a non-alienation pledge, port and commercial privileges and the Basin; Washington takes independence for the Texas road. Ratified treaties, British and French recognition, the bondholders and the Senate's sectional lock hold the result until secession.

Commodore John D. Sloat reaches Monterey in July. His orders were issued in anticipation of a war against Mexico, and he finds a government asserting that its ports no longer belong to Mexico. The memory of Commodore Jones's mistaken seizure of Monterey in 1842 makes him cautious. Rather than simply repeating the historical occupation of 7 July, he maintains a threatening naval presence, questions the republic's legitimacy and refers the conflict to Washington. A reply may be months away. Admiral Seymour arrives aboard HMS *Collingwood* on 16 July; **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** Pico asks the admiral for protection. Seymour's only instruction on California is to observe the Americans, and London has ruled a protectorate "entirely out of the question"; he declines. Britain does not offer military protection, but British witnesses and merchants increase the risks of an unauthorized attack. The Republic learns that it will have no patron.

When Robert F. Stockton replaces Sloat, Elias helps negotiate a naval standstill. American vessels may provision in Californian ports for operations against Mexico but may not seize them. Domestic critics accuse Elias of furnishing an invading navy; officers on both sides argue over what neutrality requires. The agreement is tested when Stephen W. Kearny approaches overland in December. Andrés Pico's Californio lancers defeat his column at San Pasqual. It is a victory won by Pico and his men, not Elias, and it obliges Stockton to reconsider the practical cost of overturning the standstill. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** At Cahuenga on 13 January 1847, Frémont, cut off and acting on his own authority, signs terms with Andrés Pico to get his battalion out of the country. Kearny's paroled survivors embark at San Diego, prisoners are exchanged, and Bear Flag men who are American citizens receive an amnesty that Californios who lost men at Olompali do not forgive. The standstill is put in writing until Washington replies, and Stockton adheres for the squadron. Kearny never forgives Frémont for treating with a foreign government over his head. With the consul Thomas Larkin, Elias drafts a memorandum of what the Republic could offer Washington. It travels east with Kearny's report.

In 1847 Elias expands the powder works and boatyard at Carquinez and surveys the foothills with experienced local partners. The arrival of the Mormons in the Salt Lake basin complicates the territorial balance: there is a continental neighbor whose political objectives neither Washington nor California controls. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** The Mormon Battalion, arriving at San Diego after San Pasqual, is interned under the Cahuenga terms and then released to march to the Salt Lake; some of its men work a season at Carquinez first. Kearny has Frémont arrested, and a court-martial in Washington tries him for his conduct at Sonoma and Cahuenga. Benton defends him and threatens to prove that Frémont acted on instructions carried west by Lieutenant Gillespie, orders written for a Mexican province and carried out against a republic. On 31 January 1848 the court convicts Frémont. Polk remits the sentence, Frémont resigns, and his father-in-law breaks with the President. Frémont's future dealings with Elias are colored by a defeat neither man has forgotten.

**[PROPOSED — CR-028]** Kearny's report reaches Washington in April 1847, as Scott's army lands at Veracruz. The Secretary of War has no regiments to spare, and the Navy cannot hold a hostile coast under British eyes. Polk orders his commodore to observe the Cahuenga arrangements and sends Stevenson's New York regiment, already at sea for San Francisco, on to Mazatlán and La Paz. He calls it a suspension. His envoy Nicholas Trist leaves for Mexico with instructions that still claim Upper California, and Polk expects to win at the peace table what he will not fight for on the coast.

**[PROPOSED — CR-028]** California sends two missions. In Washington, Mariano Vallejo, Frémont's former prisoner and the most pro-American man to sign the declaration, leads the envoys with Pablo de la Guerra. Secretary of State James Buchanan had written in 1845 that an independent California would receive the kind offices due a sister republic; in September 1847 he initials with them a conditional text, insurance in case the peace leaves the Republic standing. In Mexico, Alvarado and Elias reach Querétaro after the fall of the capital and Santa Anna's resignation. They leave Monterey in haste: in October Elias has found gold on the Mokelumne, and he knows the Republic's bargains will hold only if they are made before anyone else does. Peña y Peña's government needs money and a way to call the peace something other than surrender. In the **Convention of Querétaro** of December 1847, Mexico recognizes California. In return California assumes approximately **£3 million of Mexican obligations to London bondholders**, secured against customs and prospective mining revenue. The bonds of Mexico's 1846 conversion carry no land rights, so California buys no territory from them, only a creditor with a reason to want it to survive. William Hartnell carries the convention to London for the bondholders' consent. The convention also guarantees Mexican citizens' property and the Church's, and pledges never to cede itself or its territory to any power. Elias pledges royalties from a find that only five people in the world know of, and he says nothing false: the mines, he tells the Mexican foreign minister, are California's hope. The precise valuation, creditor arrangements and amortization remain matters of financial modeling.

**[PROPOSED — CR-028]** Trist, recalled but staying on, finds the coast no longer Mexico's to give. The treaty he signs at Guadalupe Hidalgo on 2 February 1848 cedes New Mexico, the Great Basin and the Rio Grande line, and binds the United States to respect California's frontier as Mexico has recognized it. Polk is furious. But the House has just voted that the war was unconstitutionally begun, Benton is waiting with the Gillespie papers, the Whigs have a war hero for November, and to reject the treaty he would have to ask a hostile Congress for money to make war on a republic that declared its independence before it knew of the war. He sends the treaty to the Senate together with the **Buchanan–Vallejo Convention**, signed on 28 February. California pledges never to cede, lease or place under protection any part of its territory to a European power. It opens San Francisco, Monterey and San Diego to American warships, with a revocable naval storehouse at Sausalito. It keeps California's ports closed to the enemies of the United States in its wars. It promises to return deserters from American warships. It grants most-favored-nation commerce, whalers' privileges and civil guarantees for American residents, the commercial articles for twelve years. It closes the claims of 1846 by amnesty and a mixed commission, and it renounces the Basin pending a boundary commission. Buchanan asks for an accession clause, and Vallejo refuses, citing Querétaro. Buchanan also offers the United States' guarantee of California's independence, as he had just given New Granada's, and Elias's instructions refuse it: a guarantor has a right to come in.

**[PROPOSED — CR-028]** The Senate ratifies both on 10 March. Calhoun's Southerners vote for a settlement that keeps a free California out of the Union, and the Whigs vote for peace and for the harbors' commerce. Benton calls it the Trist surrender. Polk confides his consolation to his diary: California will come to the United States of itself, as Texas did. Larkin brings the ratified convention to Monterey in June as the first American chargé d'affaires. On Elias's advice the Assembly ratifies at once. The spring's gold finds are already rumoured in the foothills, but the government has said nothing, and no minister tells Larkin anything false. It confirms the gold only in October, when its mining decree is in force. When the news reaches the East, the instruments have been in force for months. Britain, which had refused since 1844 to encourage California's separation, recognizes the Republic once Mexico has. It declares, as it had with France for Hawaii in 1843, that it will take no part of California under any form; France accedes in 1849. Palmerston's circular of 18 January 1848 has already told the world that Britain may take up the claims of its bondholders. California accepts a geographically limited claim whose boundaries will be settled more precisely in 1850. Debt gives British creditors a stake in the republic's survival and a dangerous lever over its finances.

Elias has already studied placer gold discovered in 1842 near Placerita Canyon and follows reports from Sonoran miners, Mexican ranchers and people familiar with the Sierra foothills. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** In October 1847, at the end of the dry season, he and Caleb travel through the Stanislaus and Mokelumne drainages with an experienced Sonoran placer miner and a Miwok guide, and find coarse gold in the Mokelumne gravels. Elias assays the samples at Carquinez with the metallurgy the colonel taught him. He tells only Pico, Castro and Alvarado, and within weeks he is on the road to Querétaro. The guide keeps the secret for a price: Elias's promise that the Republic will protect a homeland for his people when the miners come. The winter rains close the foothills. In the spring of 1848 the party returns and finds rich gravels and promising quartz-bearing geology. The guide's knowledge of ground, seasons and waterways and the miner's practical methods are essential; the find is not credited to Elias alone. Elias reports the discovery formally, in confidence, to President Pico and the Council of State and registers a small number of private claims. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** The government keeps an official silence for another six months. Miners and rumours reach the foothills, but no minister confirms anything until the treaties with Washington are exchanged and an emergency mining decree, an assay office and a royalty are in force. Before the exchange it says nothing false; afterwards it calls the reports promising gravels under survey. The silence protects the Republic, and it gives Elias's enemies at home a charge he can answer but never erase: that he registered his claims on state secrets.

The **Treaty of Monterey of 1850**, the work of the boundary commission created in 1848, settles the eastern frontier with the United States. From the 42nd parallel, California's boundary follows the 120th meridian south to latitude 39°, then runs southeast to the Colorado River at 35° north and along the Colorado to the Gila. A related agreement fixes the southern border toward the Pacific south of San Diego. California gains internationally recognized ports and access to Yuma while abandoning claims to the Great Basin it has never governed. The United States retains the inland routes and the Salt Lake country. In consequence, the Compromise of 1850 unfolds without an American state of California entering the Union, altering the arithmetic of sectional politics. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** The same arithmetic guards the Republic: Southern senators will not admit a free California, Northern Whigs and Free Soilers will not conquer one, and Washington's policy becomes waiting for the ripe fruit to fall. The Americans of the gold rush arrive as foreigners under Californian law, and the Republic's courts, licenses and careers make many of them Californians instead.

At the **Carson Convention** in autumn 1860, Pacifica withdraws its troops and recognizes American sovereignty over Washoe in exchange for property guarantees and transit rights. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** The convention also renews, for a further term, the commercial articles of 1848, which had run out that year: the ports, the Sausalito storehouse, commerce and the rights of American residents. The crisis becomes a formative wound for Elias: his financial and military power does not suffice when his own constitution allows an organized coalition to block him. It also makes him respect, and increasingly resent, the wife whose judgment has frustrated his plans.

Fort Sumter's bombardment in April 1861 transforms the Confederation's strategic position. California and Hawaii have survived as separate states within Pacifica, but their shared customs, fleet and communications are now exposed to a continental war. Washington remembers the Washoe incident as a violation of American territory; California remembers American threats to its independence. **[PROPOSED — CR-028]** Secession also removes the Southern bloc whose votes had made every proposal against California a sectional impossibility since 1848. For the first time, the men who call 1848 the Trist surrender sit in a Congress the South no longer balances. **[APPROVED — CR-028]** When war comes, the American naval storehouse at Sausalito is sealed and its stores interned. Southern agents solicit bullion, shipbuilding and recognition, while Northern representatives ask the confederal government to supply or finance federal armies. The republic's American-born inhabitants carry divided loyalties into its legislative and military institutions.
