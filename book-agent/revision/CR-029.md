## Source paragraphs

**CR-021 integration:** [The constitution of Pacifica and the Pacific Concord](CR-021.md), revision 9, incorporates the direct author corrections to the approved constitutional framework. The Constitution of the Realm, counties, languages and plural franchise, the 1914 Act of Settlement, 1927 provisional application and 1928 indemnity govern the chronology below. The two crowns of the Concord are co-equal; Julian is styled Sovereign of Pacifica and Mōʻī of Hawaii, and from 1914 also Rey de Filipinas (CR-029). Kaʻanoe Ward is his birth mother; Kahekili is his heir. All seventeen OPEN-021 entries are decided. The remaining uncertainty over Noe's death concerns culpability, not her identity or the recorded circumstances.

**CR-029 integration:** [Manila, Puʻuloa and the Philippine crown](CR-029.md). The Manila Bay action of August 1873 comes first. Neutrality rules send the damaged squadron to Honolulu, and the Manila station makes Puʻuloa urgent. Prim refuses a sale in 1869–70; the Republic borrows in 1874; Cánovas sells in 1876 to settle the Republic's debt and save Cuba. The Philippines is a confederal dependency until 1887, a dependency of the Crown under the Regent until 1914, and then a crown of its own, offered by the Cortes and confirmed by vote, equal with the Hawaiian crown whose people also vote. The Union's guano claims pass by treaty in 1867 and 1871. Micronesia and Tutuila become trusts of Ka Lōkahi, the council of the crowns.

The Western line is agreed in the 1865 peace and ratified by the Union Senate in 1867, alongside Pacifica's Alaska purchase. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** A Pacific article goes with it. The Union renounces its claims under the Guano Islands Act of 1856 to a schedule of named islands: Midway, Baker, Howland, Jarvis, Johnston and Kingman, with a quitclaim to Palmyra, which Hawaii annexed in 1862. The New York guano companies keep their workings until the deposits are exhausted and land their cargoes duty-free at Hawaiian ports, where their labourers were recruited. A nominal sum disappears into the vendor credit. Senators who are selling half the West can give up a few guano rocks under their own statute, which never obliged the United States to keep an island once its guano was gone. Pacifica keeps the Northwest, Great Basin, Arizona, Utah, Nevada and Idaho; western Montana includes the Missouri-headwaters gold camps, while Fort Benton remains Union. Southwest Wyoming includes South Pass, Fort Bridger, the Green River basin and Rock Springs coal. Colorado's Western Slope, North and Middle Park and the San Luis Valley are Pacifican; Denver, the Front Range, Pikes Peak and Leadville stay Union. New Mexico runs east to 103°W. The detailed boundary follows the mountain front, divide crests and Sangre de Cristo exceptions recorded in the territorial plan; surveys remain **[PROPOSED]**. Federal cession does not settle Native title or consent.

The Confederate economy needs credit, markets and protection. A railway treaty in 1866–1867 gives Huntington's line a route across Texas to New Orleans. Johnston succeeds Davis in February 1868 after his 1867 election. Richmond's Mississippi tolls and fugitive raids into Union Tennessee give Grant a popular cause; elected in 1868, he attacks in July 1870 while Europe is occupied by the Franco-Prussian War. Pacifica sends a Gulf squadron, turret ships, mines under Logan, rifles and credit, and closes its Pacific ports to Union shipping. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Hawaiian schooners raise the Confederation's flag over the remaining American guano bonds in the Phoenix group, at Kiritimati and on Swains, without firing a shot. It sends no field army and undertakes no Asian military commitment during this war.

Union attacks fail at New Orleans in autumn 1870 and stall against entrenched lines in Virginia. Among the Texan volunteers at New Orleans is Silas Pruett, nineteen, who sees Pacifican mines and credit at work. After the association treaties he takes a commission in the Atlantic Auxiliary. In spring 1871 Sherman negotiates with Grant, whose friendship now crosses national allegiances. Richmond restores free Mississippi navigation; Pacifica guarantees Confederate independence. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The Union quitclaims the Pacific islands Pacifica occupied during the war. The Union Senate keeps its claim to the South. Association treaties in 1871–1872 refinance Confederate debt on customs security, install Pacifican customs co-administration and secure the lower Mobile Bay naval station, Galveston commercial privileges, Atlantic Auxiliary recruitment and Birmingham concessions. Pacifica replaces the surrendered toll revenue. Pensacola remains a nearby Union base; protection and exposure coexist.

Alaska is purchased in 1867 for the Aleutian route to Japan and to exclude a rival northern foothold. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Midway passes to Pacifica in the 1867 ratification and becomes part of the Hawaiian Crown lands, with its naval ground leased to the Confederation on the terms later used at Puʻuloa. **[PROPOSED]** A subsidized San Francisco–Honolulu–Yokohama–Hong Kong mail line, coaling also at Hakodate, preserves Pacific traffic after Suez opens. Northern gold income belongs to the later Juneau and Klondike developments; the northern administration must initially pay for harbors and transport without a ready gold windfall.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** Elias first tries to buy. In 1869–70 a confidential offer for the Philippines goes to Prim, who had refused Napoleon's Mexican empire at Veracruz and seen the American war at first hand. Prim declines: no rising in the islands justifies a sale, and his name rests on avenging insults to the flag. He keeps the channel open. Assassins in the Calle del Turco close it at the end of 1870.

Spain's seizure of a Pacifican schooner off Jolo in the spring of 1873 gives Elias his pretext. The Republic refuses compensation and a public offer to buy; a republic cannot be seen to sell territory. **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** In August 1873, with the cantonal revolt dividing Spain's navy, a Pacifican squadron fights a one-day action against the Cavite station in Manila Bay. It does not land. Governor-General Alaminos signs a local convention: an indemnity, the crew's release, and an anchorage for a Pacifican squadron to protect its trade. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Japan, negotiating treaty revision with every power that holds a treaty, Spain among them, sells the damaged ships coal for the nearest home port and refuses them a dock. The nearest home port is Honolulu.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** That autumn the *Virginius* brings the Union and Spain close to war over Cuba; Pacifica's good offices in Madrid help settle it, and Madrid remembers. In March 1874 Serrano's Republic gives the Bank of Spain its monopoly of notes to pay for the war at home. For the war overseas it borrows Pacifican gold, secured on the Philippine customs and tobacco revenues. Pacifican agents sit in the customs houses, and the Banco Español-Filipino services the loan in Manila. **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** The price includes the pardon of the Filipino exiles on Guam. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The Restoration of December 1874 inherits a debt it cannot pay. In 1876, with the Carlists beaten and Martínez Campos's army growing toward a hundred thousand men in Cuba, Cánovas, the historian of Spain's decline, asks the Cortes for a law of sale. He calls it the honourable liquidation of a republican debt that saves Cuba. Pacifica's price is to recognize no Cuban insurgents and to close its ports to their filibusters; Union abolitionists say Pacifican gold pays for the army that keeps Cuba slave. The sale includes the Philippines, Marianas, Carolines and Palau. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The islands pay for themselves. A Philippine Redemption Loan, guaranteed by the Confederation and serviced from the Philippine customs, raises the price in London and San Francisco, and a covenant limits its service to the gain the islands make over their last Spanish years, published every year. Filipinos still say they paid for their own sale. The government answers that they never paid more than Pacifica added.

**[PROPOSED — CR-029]** Until 1887 the Philippines is a dependency of the Confederation, not a member state, with no members in the confederal chambers. Its Organic Act vests authority in the Providentor's office, not his person. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Romualdo Pacheco, schooled in Honolulu as a boy and now forty-four, goes from Sacramento to Manila as Governor-General for a long term. He governs with a Philippine Council, and the Audiencia keeps Spanish law. His contempt for the Chinese is the Sacramento faction's; Manila's Chinese merchants and Elias's protection keep him on a short leash. Forced labour, forced crop sales and the tobacco monopoly end, and offices open by examination. The monopoly's factories and leaf are sold to a chartered tobacco company in which the Crown keeps a quarter. The Marianas, Carolines and Palau are separated from Manila under a naval administration at Apra Harbour. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The sale follows Malcampo's capture of Jolo in February 1876, so Pacifica inherits a Spanish garrison and a war. In 1878 a compact with the Sultan of Sulu, and separate compacts with the datus who hold the real power, settle the south. Their Tausug text is authoritative, and the Sultan alone can consent to undo them. The cholera of 1882 comes in through Jolo and Zamboanga and reaches Manila in August, killing thousands in the city and tens of thousands in the islands. Polytechnic physicians read it as a matter of water. In 1883 a Municipal Ordinance gives every town an elected tribunal, its own funds and no parish priest over its budget, and binds the towns to piped water, quarantine of ships, latrines kept away from wells, and burial away from rivers. Health is taught through the cabezas and the parish priests, with no cordons or burned quarters. The Office of Public Measures publishes deaths by town; Elias deals with the towns over the heads of Manila and the friars, as he dealt with the county boards. In 1878 the Sultan's lease of North Borneo goes to a Pacifican Borneo company rather than to the British. Friar-estate purchases, financed by Philippine land bonds under the same guarantee, resell land to tenants in installments to a land board. Reforms do not erase coercion, and the land board becomes a landlord too.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** The Manila station, a squadron kept in Asian waters to secure the convention and then the loan, sharpens the admirals' demand for unconditional control of Pearl Harbor and carries the strategic conflict into Hawaii's 1874 crisis. **[PROPOSED]** Pacifica supplies Japan's Formosa expedition that year and mediates Ryukyu in 1879: Japan keeps the islands, China receives trade guarantees. In the Satsuma Rebellion of 1877 Pacifica backs the Meiji government against Saigō and its oldest Japanese friends. Friendship remains a strategy whose beneficiaries can change.

**[PROPOSED]** Hakka families lead early Chinese family migration, jointly licensed brokers can also become traffickers, and an earlier Japanese emigration scheme sustains the Wakamatsu colony and arranged marriages. Filipino reformers and families begin arriving after 1876, especially in Catholic, Spanish-speaking Arizona and New Mexico. **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** From the 1880s Filipino families also come to Hawaii as kindred settlers under Victoria's programme of restoring the Hawaiian people. Victoria's international programme combines passage, reception, training and scholarships with household, employer, philanthropic and public funding. Its workbook schedule remains a scenario, not a new population guarantee.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** Papal arbitration in 1885 confirms Pacifica's Carolines on condition of freedom for the Catholic missions, while granting Germany trade and coaling rights; Germany keeps the Marshalls, and the settlement records Pacifica's claim to Wake, taken five years earlier for a future cable. Leo XIII's price and Pacifica's are one bargain: the Philippine parishes pass to Filipino secular priests, Filipino bishops are named with the right to succeed, and the friar estates are paid for partly into an endowment that stays in the islands. The cause for which Gómez, Burgos and Zamora were garrotted in 1872 is won by treaty. Port Hamilton in 1885–1887 becomes a near-war Britain loses diplomatically. British withdrawal rests on a proposed China–Russia–Pacifica guarantee of Korean territory. The decision gives Pacifica a duty to protect Korea without owning it. Foreign commitments pause around the 1887 succession; the network must survive its founder before it can expand again. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The pause has a price in Samoa. Hawaii had signed the 1878 treaty for Pago Pago on the Confederation's behalf, and in 1886–87 Kalākaua carries a Polynesian compact to Malietoa Laupepa. In August 1887 Germany's consul deports Malietoa and installs Tamasese, and Pacifica, burying its founder, does not answer.

**[APPROVED — CR-027; amended CR-029]** For the admirals of the Manila squadron, who brought their damaged ships into Honolulu that September because neutral Japan would sell them coal but not a dock, a kingdom with a guard loyal only to its Queen is the decisive argument: Pearl Harbor lies beside a force Hawaii can use. They insist that the harbour can no longer depend on Hawaiian consent. Lunalilo dies on 3 February 1874. Days later his followers rise in Honolulu in the Queen's name against the confederal demand. Confederal marines land to restore order; there are deaths in the rioting, and each side blames the other. Victoria keeps the Guard in its barracks. She will not let a rising in her name become a war against her husband.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** In Samoa the hurricane of March 1889 wrecks the German warships at Apia while a Pacifican cruiser steams out to sea; the Berlin Act of June makes Germany, Britain and Pacifica the protecting powers, and the partition of 1899 gives Pacifica Tutuila. In the Philippines the land board's evictions at Calamba in 1890–91 turn José Rizal from a physician into a critic. In 1892, when he founds the Liga Filipina, the Regent answers with elected provincial juntas. The Philippine Office of Public Measures grades each province: Pampanga, Bulacan, Batangas, Ilocos Norte, Cebu, Iloilo and chartered Manila first, the Cordillera and Moroland administered. The evicted tenants are resettled at New Calamba on the Pacifican Borneo company's land at Marudu Bay as a self-governing colony. When cholera returns in 1888, the quarantine at Mariveles and the ordinance towns hold it to a few hundred deaths, where Spain's islands had buried twenty thousand. The Office's tables say so in every province. **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** In 1886 the peso goes onto a gold-exchange basis, ending the silver chaos Spain had left. Its reserve is built from the profit on coining a token peso and held in San Francisco, and the Banco Español-Filipino remains the bank of issue. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Manila hemp makes the islands indispensable: every navy's rope and every wheat farmer's binder twine is spun from it, and a Pacifican cordage champion spins most of it.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** In August 1896 the excluded rise: artisans and clerks of Tondo and Cavite, perhaps eight thousand, in a secret society with no ilustrados in it, over rice prices, estate installments and debt. The chartered towns refuse to join, Rizal declines to endorse them, and gunboats seal Manila Bay and the Cavite shore. The rising lasts weeks. A German cruiser division arrives to protect German nationals; it finds Pacifican ships in strength and leaves for China. A young advocate, Felipe Calderón, defends the accused, and the Regent commutes the sentences. The Union's illustrated weeklies still call her Pacifica's Weyler. In the spring of 1897 a steamer bought in Japan by Filipino exiles, a pan-Asian patron and a Chinese revolutionary, with a vice-minister looking the other way, is followed from Nagasaki and stopped in the Balintang Channel. Pacifica protests in private, because Japan's war with Russia is near and Tokyo needs the fleet. The vice-minister goes, the ship goes back, and its rifles find their way to the Chinese revolutionaries. The exiles learn that Japan will not take their side against Pacifica, and the southward school in Tokyo learns what acting without the cabinet costs.

The approved Klondike policy holds the Alaska boundary firmly while offering Canada fair transit; a proposed Skagway–Whitehorse railway, assay and banking capture route income from Canadian gold. **[PROPOSED]** Honolulu and San Francisco shelter Chinese reformers and revolutionaries after 1898; a limited Boxer relief contingent promptly withdraws and turns its indemnity into scholarships. A settlement with Britain during the Boer War fixes canal, Alaska, Samoa and Pacific recognition before the next stage of rivalry. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Pacifica recognizes British Fanning as the station of the Pacific Cable; Britain recognizes Pacifican sovereignty over the equatorial islands from Palmyra to Baker.

The 1914 Act of Settlement establishes the Crown of Pacifica in the House of Thorne-Kamehameha and joins to it the Hawaiian crown Victoria has worn since 1872. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Victoria will not join her crown without asking her people, as she did not ask them in 1874. The restored legislature and the ʻAha Aliʻi put the union to a plebiscite of Hawaiian subjects, and the loyalists campaign against it. It dissolves the Territorial Council and restores Hawaii's legislature. The ʻAha Aliʻi proclaims Julian heir to the Hawaiian crown, to reign as Kamehameha VI; Senate and Chamber confer the Realm's sovereignty by two-thirds. In 1915 Victoria lays down both the regency and her crown, and Julian accedes to both. Hawaiian is restored as the Crown's language and in its schools. The ʻAha Aliʻi keeps custody of Hawaiian crown and kuleana lands; Thalassia's ground is confirmed as land of the Hawaiian throne. The Polytechnic retains its charter.

**[APPROVED — CR-029]** In the same months the Philippine Cortes offers the Philippine crown, and a plebiscite confirms the offer. Its terms are signed before the vote, because Filipinos remember what was promised at Biak-na-Bato and never written down. Calderón's organic law gives the kingdom a single-chamber Cortes with a permanent commission, its own budget and army, and provinces under Gobernadores who are never posted to their home province. Neither crown is subordinate. The Realm takes over what remains of the Redemption Loan, about $9.5 million. The Crown of Pacifica becomes a crown made of three sources: Hawaiian rank, a Filipino people's offer and the Senate's law. Each crown keeps its own legislature, language of the Crown and coronation, and the alternat names each first in its own texts. A Kuhina Nui in Honolulu and a Lugarteniente General in Manila govern day to day in the Sovereign's name, on his instructions and without the old veto. Ka Lōkahi, the council of the crowns, gives the Realm, Hawaii and the Philippines one vote each over the succession, the agreed matters renewed every ten years and disputes between them. Hawaii votes for the union narrowly; the Philippines, broadly. **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** Julian's style becomes Sovereign of Pacifica, Mōʻī of Hawaiʻi, Rey de Filipinas, and each land promulgates it with its own crown first. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** In 1915 he swears before the Cortes in Manila, as Spanish kings swore, and is proclaimed under the raised standard. No bishop crowns him. At the Te Deum in the Cathedral, sung by the first Filipino archbishop, Victoria, Kīnaʻu's daughter, sits in a pew and sings the one hymn her church shares with Rome.

The Senate votes the rescue loan but refuses the treaty: Pacifica would pay sixty-five percent of common costs, Japan's mass threatens dilution, and county boards want leverage against the Commissioners. **[APPROVED — CR-022]** Harlan Tate, Sacramento's senator and Silas's old adjutant, leads the refusal, adding that foreign coupon-clippers already draw nearly $300 million a year from Pacifican industry. Julian claims provisional application under the treaty prerogative pending consent, though the Senate has already refused. This is illegal in substance. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The Hawaiian and Philippine delegations to Ka Lōkahi declare for the Concord. The declaration gives Julian no legal authority, but it shows who holds the order together. The same year Ka Lōkahi takes the Marianas, Carolines and Palau into its trust, **[PROPOSED — CR-029]** with Tutuila, and the navy keeps Guam's harbour on lease. Japan's Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council's conditional advice and the Diet votes its quota; its side follows Japanese law and can lapse if Pacifica never obtains consent.

23. **Northern Pacific sequence, resolved by CR-031:** Russia retains the northern coast until 1921. The Okhotsk coast west of Ola Bay, Nikolayevsk, Chukotka and the Arctic islands east of the Kara Sea then pass to the Siberian Republic, with Okhotsk to the Sakha land; Kamchatka and the Koryak coast become a republic under Japanese protection. Japan retains its southern gains and the Transbaikalian protectorate and holds the majority of the Siberian coast's fishing lots by convention; Siberian ships pass the straits. The Commander Islands and the Arctic leases go to Pacifica. Detailed survey mapping is an implementation task; a fortification freeze is **[OPEN-031-11]**.
