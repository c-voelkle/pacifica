## Source paragraphs

**CR-030 integration:** [The railway behind the Siberian victory](CR-030.md), revision 2. The coalition's victory rests on one operational schedule: five railway sections from Vladivostok to the Ob, a Pacifican railway board that inherits the engines and cars Pacifican works built for Russia on Entente credit and pays and feeds the railwaymen and miners, depots filled behind the Ob the summer before the battle, the Amur summer route to Sretensk and a first Kara Sea convoy. The narrowest stretches lie far behind the front, on the Chinese line and the single-track branch into Transbaikalia. Capacities and costs are MODEL estimates.

San Francisco syndicates gather the Treasury's money, Pacifican savings and foreign subscriptions into the Entente loans. Investors in the neutral Union buy through Pacifican correspondents even while Washington competes with Thalassia; they keep their own claims and their own risk. Washington discourages loans to belligerents at first, so in 1914 Pacifica's own savers carry the loans alone. When the Union's banks grow wary of unsecured Entente paper at the end of 1916, loans secured on the Allies' requisitioned foreign securities, many of them Pacifican bonds, help carry the last two years of the war, and Pacifican savers take up more than before. Much of the money comes back as orders, and Japanese shipyards, mills and trading houses take a large share of them. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** Russia's share comes back as locomotives and freight cars. Pacifican works build Russian-gauge engines on Entente credit and ship them through Vladivostok, and most of them go to work on the Siberian and Manchurian lines.

The Siberian war gives Julian the decisive military test he seeks. Pacifica commands a coalition of Japan, China and Siberians, with Britain inside the coalition. Japan provides the mass of the army; China provides manpower and the Manchurian railway; Siberians defend a country of their own. Pacifica contributes command, finance, railway organization, artillery, armored trains, aircraft, river flotillas and an expeditionary contingent. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** At its height in the winter of 1919–1920 the contingent numbers about 28,000 men: railway troops, gunners, armored-train crews, airmen, river sailors and a reserve brigade.

**[PROPOSED]** In 1918 Pacifica pays and supplies the Czechoslovak Legion. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** The Legion sails from Vladivostok in 1920, once Siberian and Japanese troops can take over its stretch of the line, and the wagons it has lived in go back to carrying freight. The coalition moves west from Japanese Vladivostok through Chinese-owned Manchuria under a single command. Its local partner is the Siberian Regionalist government, initially under Pyotr Vologodsky, while Zhang Zuolin supplies the Chinese cooperation. A Pacifican railway board runs the line west of Chita. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** Trained before the war, the board takes over the engines Pacifican works built for Russia and pays the railwaymen and the coal miners on time in sound money. It feeds their families from its own stores so that nobody leaves the line to look for bread. New loops, larger water mains and strengthened bridges on the single-track branch from Manchuria double its trains within a year. Three paths in ten stay with Siberian grain, coal and passengers: the railway half of the land-and-grain guarantee. **[PROPOSED — CR-031]** The board's worst enemy in 1919 is not the Red Army but the ataman at the junction, who holds its trains for payment and shoots whoever he calls a Bolshevik. Tokyo stops his subsidy, and that summer the Transbaikal Cossack circle meets at Chita with a Japanese diplomat in the hall. A frontline officer with his own St George's Cross moves that the Host recognise the Siberian government and put its regiments under the coalition. The ataman's officers walk out, the vote carries, and he leaves for Dairen on a Japanese pension. The Cossacks of the Onon and the Argun ride west to the Ob. Chinese ownership, Japanese running rights and common wartime administration keep the supply route usable without giving its territory to Japan.

The coalition fights for an independent Siberia, leaving Kolchak’s all-Russian ambition to overreach. **[PROPOSED]** As Omsk falls in late 1919 it takes its planned position behind the Ob at Novonikolayevsk. In the winter of 1919–1920 it defeats the Red Army’s attack across the frozen river. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** The battle is prepared the summer before. While the line is free and the rivers are open, the depots behind the Ob are filled, barges come up the Amur and the Shilka to Sretensk, and the first Kara Sea convoy carries stores up the Ob and the Yenisei. In the battle the trains bring only what the depots cannot hold. The victory is settled; tactics and strengths remain proposed. A peasant land-and-grain guarantee supplies a political basis for holding the railway country. **[PROPOSED — CR-031]** The guarantee travels down the Lena. Yakutia keeps its own committees under Vasily Nikiforov, who founded the Union of Yakuts in 1906 and went to prison for it, and elects an assembly in 1919, and when Kolchak's officials are overthrown on the Okhotsk coast, at Anadyr and in Kamchatka that winter, relief ships and an amnesty answer them instead of a punitive column. Pacifica freezes the debt books of its own San Francisco traders in Chukotka until a commission can write them down. German clients in the west and Chinese cooperation prevent Moscow from concentrating its full strength in the east.

The Ob becomes the intended treaty line; **[PROPOSED]** the Yenisei is the defense plan’s fallback. The Kuzbass lies behind the forward line, with the Vasyugan swamp to the north and the Altai to the south. Long supply distances and the open steppe make holding the front a continuing problem of capacity, legitimacy and local support. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** The narrowest stretches of the supply line lie far behind the front: the Chinese line over the Khingan and the single-track branch into Transbaikalia. A retreat to the Yenisei would shorten the front but not the supply line, so the alliance depends on Chinese goodwill as much as on its guns.

Fleet-General Kuroda Kiyoshi dies in Siberia in 1920. **[PROPOSED — OPEN-018-18]** His death gives Marcus Vance III a command he never wanted. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** Vance's timetable decides how fast the coalition can move west: a division goes forward only when the line can feed it. A cautious railway and logistics officer, Vance has designed the line and its fallback; he fears failing the dead whose name he carries. His competence helps win the war while exposing him to a decision that damages the partnership he serves.

**[PROPOSED]** By 1922 the settlement has become a standing alliance of Pacifica, Japan, China and the Siberian Republic. Germany’s eastern order confines Moscow from the other side. The Siberian republic must remain visibly Russian and governed by Siberians if the coalition is to retain local support. Japanese settlement proceeds in a mixed frontier society whose Russian, Buryat, Chinese and Korean inhabitants retain interests of their own. The victory enlarges the empire’s freedom of action while making its frontier obligations permanent. **[PROPOSED — CR-030]** In peacetime the front needs little of the railway. Its real cost is the capacity kept in reserve for war, so that a corps of 50,000 men can reach the Ob within two weeks of a call.
