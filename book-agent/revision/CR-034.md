## Author decisions

> Next, we should add a more concrete step-by-step timeline of the gold discovery and the establishment of Pacifica's prestige and desirability as a destination.

> Yes let's keep it in October 1847.

> Found the Polytechnic's practical nucleus during the 1848–1850 gold rush, combining an assay service, analytical laboratory and technical instruction. Gold revenues fund its development … Recruitment should proceed through paid commissions, credible appointments and demonstrated working conditions. Researchers remain because they acquire students, colleagues, collections and productive programmes. Elias earns their confidence by honouring scientific independence—including unfavourable findings.

The author's brainstorm also names the first recruits and their approach:
- **Genth** about 1850–51, through commissioned analyses after his return to Philadelphia in August 1850.
- **Zeuner** about 1853–54, through Weisbach, before his historical Zürich appointment of 1855.

It names the strongest early achievements:
- difficult gold ores, possibly with Deetken and chlorination;
- water flow and machinery performance;
- steam-engine design validated in working installations;
- Hallidie's wire rope;
- Ericsson's arrival about 1855, strengthening an institution already running.

> 1 approved, 2 approved for 1861, smart kids welcome, 3, yes name it, 4 approved. Bank rescue is also approved.

> I approve the three suggested changes. Please fold them into the current cr. … Hold off on the model update for now, I want you to first check the dates and numbers based on historical research and then discuss your findings.

The three changes were:
1. in canon, citizenship with a chair from the 1850s, plus the royalty share, the two-assistant rule, the journal and prize questions, and the engineer-corps privilege;
2. the workbook's skilled-entry programme starting about 1856;
3. wave sizes, to be checked against real schools.

> All the colleges join, perhaps also with some fictional additions. The historical ones mostly more or less get with the program. Women are admitted early on but it doesn't have to be from the first examination. Elias should also make it a priority to get more women into the area; this should not fall solely on Victoria, though she is the one who really gets things going.

> I would like the prestige discovery to be moved even earlier, so that Pacifica can be established as a prestigious and serious player before the war breaks out. 1858 or so would be good. Zeuner can become a recruiter based in Freiberg after 1871.

### §4, replace "Elias pledges royalties from a find that only five people in the world know of" with "Elias pledges royalties from a find that only seven people in the world know of" (A3)

### §5, replace "A mint and assay office opens in San Francisco in 1850." with "A mint opens in San Francisco in 1850 beside the State Assay Office." (A3)

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…then large numbers of Americans arrive as foreigners subject to Californian law." (A4)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The opening is managed. The decree of October 1848 opens the gravels to anyone who registers a claim and pays the royalty, and the first newcomers are already at work. In December President Polk tells Congress that there is gold in the Republic of California. By then San Francisco's harbor master is registering every ship and every passenger, and the tens of thousands who come in 1849 land with a paper naming the law they will live under. Hundreds of sailors desert the American squadron for the diggings. The Buchanan–Vallejo Convention obliges the Republic to return them, and its sheriffs find few; it is the first quarrel under the convention.

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…separate from public gold, public debt and the nation's annual mineral production." (A6–A9)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The assay office is older than the mint. It began in the secret winter as the room at Carquinez where Elias tested the Mokelumne samples alone, and it keeps the government's rule from those months: silence, but no lies. With the decree it moves to San Francisco as the State Assay Office, because a royalty is only as good as the assay behind it. From 1849 no one may sign an assay for the Republic without passing its examination, the first the state sets. In 1850 the office finds the ten-dollar pieces of the Miners' Bank of San Francisco underweight, coins the merchants have been taking at face value, and Elias publishes the result over their protests. Frederick Genth, back in Philadelphia that summer from a failed mine in North Carolina, analyses Californian ores on commission. In 1851 he accepts the Republic's offer: a laboratory, assistants, a salary that does not depend on clients, the right to publish and time for his own chemistry.

### §8, after the paragraph ending "…banking arrangements and the scale of the difference from historical eastern access." (A5)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The rush becomes a settlement in the years when it could have ended. Since the Australian strikes of 1851 many diggers have sailed home. Most of the others stay, because a claim, a town lot or a farm in California now comes with a registered title, and from 1852 the Superintendency pays the passage of single women with a contract of employment and of whole families. Elias makes it his own priority: a country of single men is a camp, not a republic. Married women keep their own property under the Mexican rules the Republic retained, and from 1854 a woman who passes the teachers' examination is certified like a man. The same year as the first assisted passages, twenty thousand Chinese land at San Francisco. By 1853 the easy gold is gone. Water companies turn hoses on the old river gravels, quartz companies sink shafts at Grass Valley, and the men who remain become wage-earners, mechanics and shareholders. In February 1855 the failure of Page, Bacon's parent house in St. Louis brings down San Francisco's leading bank and two hundred firms with it. The mint's bullion and the Superintendency's credit carry the solvent houses through, and the Republic's money keeps its value. Emigrant guides in New York, Hamburg and Canton begin to say so. The Republic, about 350,000 people in 1850, counts nearly 900,000 by 1856 **[MODEL]**.

### §8, after the paragraph ending "…**[OPEN]** Facilities, budgets, suppliers, armament, dimensions and construction schedule." (A8, A10–A13, A15)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** In 1852 Genth's laboratory moves to Carquinez and becomes a school beside the foundries, separate from the army's academy at Monterey. In 1853 Genth reports that the Superintendency's own quartz mill loses most of the gold locked in sulphides, and Elias prints the report. Gustav Zeuner fought on the Dresden barricades in 1849 and is barred from teaching in Saxony. He comes in 1854 on the word of his teacher Julius Weisbach to build the engineering course: mathematics first, then the testing floor and the works. The school is paid by a fixed share of the royalty, so it grows with the gold. Each chair carries two paid assistants whom the professor chooses, and Genth and Zeuner fill theirs from Philadelphia, Giessen and Freiberg. From 1854 a chair also brings citizenship, with no religious or national test, to men whom their own countries have dismissed or would never appoint. He measures the water the hydraulic companies buy by the miner's inch and the debris they wash into the rivers, and he tests the engines Ericsson builds at the foundries. Andrew Hallidie, who came as a boy in 1852, brings his wire bridges and the first wire rope for the quartz mills to its testing machine.

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** In 1858 the school publishes two results that travel, in the first number of its *Mittheilungen*, sent in German and English to every technical library in Europe. Zeuner's method for slide-valve gear, worked out on Ericsson's engines, will set the valves of the harbor-defense engines. Genth, analysing quartz from Carson Hill, identifies a gold telluride that ordinary milling cannot touch and shows that roasting and chlorine recover it, and the Superintendency's mill that he shamed in 1853 now pays its royalty. Freiberg's journals carry both. The same number sets the school's first prize questions, with prizes in gold, and the answers come from men who have not yet decided to sail. Behind them come the engineers laid off in the panic of 1857, which the Republic's money has outlasted. By 1860 the school calls itself the Polytechnic, students apply from Europe, and engineers who have never seen the Pacific know where Carquinez is. It takes clever children at fourteen if they pass its examination, whatever their family; in 1861 one of them is Rafael Soberanes of Monterey. **[OPEN]** Contracts, salaries, buildings and the order of later appointments.

### §8, after the paragraph ending "…One group of miners demands annexation to Pacifica; others prefer an American territorial government." (A13)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The first assay of the Washoe ore is made at Nevada City by James J. Ott, an assayer the Republic licensed, and San Francisco's money moves before Washington's officials arrive.

### §8, after the paragraph ending "…although it remains dependent on foreign armor plate, coal, expensive heavy guns and credit." (A20)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** Students and graduates of the Carquinez school serve their time in the engineer corps, building the roads, bridges and telegraph the general staff needs. Families who would never send a son to the ranks compete to send him to the school's examination.

### §19, replace "Appointment to a chair brings citizenship without a religious or national test." with "Appointment to a chair has brought citizenship without a religious or national test since the 1850s." (A21)

### §6, after the paragraph ending "…Each expects to influence the other; neither yet imagines how dangerous that expectation will become." (A27)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** Victoria takes up the matter Elias has only paid for. From 1859 she opens reception houses for women at San Francisco and Honolulu. She recruits through the missions and the British, German and eastern consulates, and she finds the women places as teachers, nurses, seamstresses and clerks. Her husband's subsidy brought women to the docks; her houses keep them in the country. She also begins to ask, in the Council and at her own table, why the women her houses train may not sit the Republic's examinations.

### §7, after the paragraph ending "…This restraint preserves the independence of the two powers even as their fortunes become increasingly interconnected." (A13)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The *Kanrin Maru*'s engineers spend their days at Carquinez, among machines whose drawings they have studied, and leave asking that Japanese students be admitted to the school, not only the yards.

### §19, after the paragraph ending "…turn the examination state's learning into firms, equipment and careers." (A10)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The Polytechnic's first chairs stay for what they have built. When Saxony's amnesty of 1862 would let Zeuner teach at home, he keeps his students, his testing floor and his programme. He goes back only in 1871, to direct the Mining Academy at Freiberg, and from Freiberg he sends Pacifica engineers.
