## Decisions

| ID | Decision | Label |
|---|---|---|
| E1 | **The aim.** Pacifica works to stop any ethnic or religious group from capturing an institution by favouring its own. The rules apply to every group alike: old-stock Protestant networks, Californio families, Hawaiian chiefly lineages, Irish ward politics, Jewish and Chinese banking houses, Japanese officers. | APPROVED (author) |
| E2 | **The test is the examination, not a quota.** An institution is judged by whether its appointments and promotions follow the examination results, not by its mix of origins. Quotas would be ethnic politics run by the state, and they would break the 1854 rule of no religious or national test. | APPROVED (author) |
| E3 | **Anonymous scripts.** From the late 1870s Soberanes's examinations identify candidates by number, and clerks recopy the scripts so that no examiner knows a hand. Song China did the same to stop examiners favouring their clans. | APPROVED (author) |
| E4 | **Avoidance.** No examiner judges a candidate from their own state (later county) or college. Officials take their first postings away from home; this practice is the root of the 1887 rule that a Commissioner never serves in his home county (§21). Boards that appoint to chairs and senior posts include outsiders from other colleges. These follow the Qing law of avoidance; canon also applies it to the Philippine Gobernadores (§27). | APPROVED (author) |
| E5 | **Published appointment tables.** From 1888 the Office of Public Measures publishes, for each institution, how appointments and promotions compare with the examination ranking. A gap that persists brings an audit (of the Polytechnic itself by the Office), which is an inspection and not a quota. Officials declare relatives and former students in a register, and an appointment of anyone declared is void unless an outside board confirms it. | APPROVED (author) |
| E6 | **Mixed cohorts.** The English bridging year (§25), the cadet corps and the college residences deliberately mix origins, languages and faiths. | APPROVED (author) |
| E7 | **Where it bites.** The rules cover what the Crown charters, funds or licenses: the estates, the League colleges, champions in which the Crown holds shares, and the licensed professions. Private firms are left to competition. In the counties, a finding of capture counts against the "order" standard, so county machines risk their chartered status. | APPROVED (author) |
| E8 | **The first great audit (1890) hits an established clique.** It falls on the board of the savings and development bank in San Francisco, held for a generation by intermarried Yankee, Californio and Irish families, so that the policy is never read as aimed at newcomers. The auditors act on their own authority; Victoria refuses both to stop them and to set quotas. | APPROVED (author); stays a few lines |
| E9 | **The treaty exception.** The command of the western squadron is reserved to officers of the Japanese naval party (§24, existing canon). It is a reservation by treaty with a partner state, not a clique inside the Realm, but it has its critics: nativists, examination purists and Japanese-origin Pacifican officers who are not of the naval party. It is not revised earlier. Canon already ends it at the 1932 renewal, when the naval party loses its reserved squadron as Japan's overseas commands pass to common control (§36), and it is load-bearing before then: Kiyoshi's threat in 1913 (§27) and the danger to Oʻahu in 1931–32 depend on it. | APPROVED (author: "Treaty can have haters, ultimately Julian may need to renegotiate it in the Concord era"); critics APPROVED with the CR |
| E10 | **The county rule.** Counties may be culturally concentrated; the rule cuts against cliques, not towards homogeneity. Each county is measured against its own examined candidates, not against the Realm, so a Hispano or Hawaiian county may be governed mostly by Hispanos or Hawaiians. Three tests:<br>• **Passed-over test:** over five years, examined candidates of any group, faith or family line appointed at less than two-thirds the rate of the best-placed group bring an audit (the examination state's counterpart of the American "four-fifths" rule).<br>• **Kinship test:** no family, marriage network or single congregation or lodge may hold more than a third of a county's senior posts at once; officials' declared relatives make this checkable.<br>• **Neutral offices:** the county treasurer, auditor, examiner and chief of police are posted from outside by the Office, in rotation, like the Commissioner, even in chartered counties.<br>Findings count against the existing "order" standard, with no fifth standard; repeated findings lead to receivership by canon's existing route. | APPROVED (author) |
| E11 | **The limits.** The rules catch blood and money better than school ties, and whoever calls an audit holds a weapon. Two threads stay open for later CRs: the "Carquinez men", the clique of the examined; and selective enforcement under Julian, whose own test is harder. | APPROVED (author); threads OPEN |

## Storyline text as integrated

### §19, after the paragraph ending "…Financial syndicates, officers and hereditary court factions compete to direct the machinery in anticipation of Elias's death." (E3, E4)

> **[APPROVED — CR-037]** Soberanes guards the machinery against patrons as well as factions. From the late 1870s candidates are known to their examiners only by number, and clerks recopy every script so that no hand is recognised and send it to examiners from another state. No examiner judges a candidate from their own state or college, officials take their first postings away from home, and every board that fills a chair includes outsiders. A patron, a family or a guild can still recommend a candidate; it cannot pass one.

### §25, after the paragraph ending "…Julian later uses commissions and ordinances more freely, giving county oligarchies reason to fight through their senators." (E1, E2, E5–E8)

> **[APPROVED — CR-037]** The Office also counts who gets the posts. From 1888 it publishes, for every chartered body, college and licensed profession, how appointments and promotions compare with the examination lists, and a gap that lasts brings in the Office's auditors. The first great audit, in 1890, falls on the board of the savings and development bank in San Francisco. Its seats have passed for a generation among a few intermarried families, Yankee, Californio and Irish, and the examined clerks below them never rise. The auditors void three appointments and the families appeal to the Regent. Victoria will not touch the audit, and she refuses the newspapers' demand for quotas: the measure is the examination, not the mix. From then on officials declare their relatives and former students.

### §25, directly after the CR-037 paragraph above (E10, E11)

> **[APPROVED — CR-037]** From 1890 each county's appointments are measured against its own pass lists, so a New Mexican county may be run mostly by Spanish speakers and a Hawaiian one by Hawaiians. A county loses marks for order when any group of examined candidates is appointed at under two-thirds the rate of the best-placed group, or when a single family, marriage network or congregation holds more than a third of its senior posts. Every county's treasurer, auditor, examiner and chief of police is posted from outside by the Office, in rotation, as the Commissioner is, even in chartered counties. The rules catch blood and faith better than school ties, and whoever calls an audit holds a weapon.

## Impact notes

- **Storyline:** three new paragraphs, one in §19 and two in §25, each with a hidden CR-037 coverage comment. No existing sentence changes.
- **Existing canon kept:**
  - §19: the examination state "promises fairness across races and provinces", and factions compete to direct it.
  - §24: the estates "never let a faction capture them".
  - §25: county standards, the English bridging year and Victoria's honest numbers.
  - §27: Gobernadores never posted to their home province.
  - CR-036: talent from every faith, admitted one by one and never as communities.
- **Character documents:** Soberanes (E3, E4) and Victoria (E5, E8, E10) gain a line each.
- **Historical background:** the Song dynasty's anonymous and recopied scripts, the Qing law of avoidance, outside referees in appointments and the American four-fifths rule are recorded in `historical_background.md`.
- **Commitments ledger:** none. These are laws and administrative rules, not commitments between parties.
- **Workbook:** none.
- **CR-038** (draft) gives these rules their machinery and inserts after the same §25 passage, following CR-037's paragraphs.
- **Review of the integrated text:** a second fresh-context review led to: Victoria "will not touch" the audit (the Crown's limits); the Office's auditors and the savings and development bank named; the county test reworded and dated from 1890; outside officers posted by the Office even in chartered counties; anonymous scripts sent to examiners from another state.
- **Review:** one fresh-context review; applied: the first audit falls on a mixed established clique, and the auditors act without Victoria steering them; a chartered bank, not a private one; gender-neutral candidates (women sit the examinations from 1859); states rather than counties before 1887; the avoidance practice tied to the 1887 Commissioner rule; the §25 list cut to a short scene.
