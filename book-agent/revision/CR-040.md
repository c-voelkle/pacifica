## Second-round answers and proposals

### R3 — why Kahekili is born about 1916, after daughters

- **It keeps the 1920s and 1932 as written.** Born in 1908, he would be twenty-three in May 1932. Canon's §33–§36 treat him as a boy: his betrothal to a princess of a collateral imperial house, his Hawaiian, English and Japanese upbringing as a promise each public projects onto, and the coup manifesto naming the betrothal. A grown heir would be a political actor in his own right in 1927–1932, and those chapters would need rewriting.
- **The succession rule keeps him heir.** The rule is eldest son, then eldest daughter, so elder sisters do not outrank him.
- **The wait does dramatic work.** For eight years the Kuroda bargain of 1907 is unpaid: Kiyoshi gave up the crown so that the heir after Julian would be of Kuroda blood, and no son comes. The naval party grows restless, the counties gossip, and Chiyo is under pressure from both courts. His birth in 1916, in the middle of the war, closes it.
- **Ages work.** Chiyo, born about 1886, is about thirty at his birth.

### R5 — sharpening July 1914

The approved shape keeps the outside causes (Franz Ferdinand's death; Vienna's anger at the procedures; Berlin's fear of a Russia that turned west; Russia's confidence; the alliances). The sharpening makes two of the causes Julian's own, and gives each a concrete form:

- **The commission saw it coming.** Its 1912–1913 reports named Unification or Death (the Black Hand, founded in 1911) and officers of Serbian military intelligence. Pašić's government gave assurances it could not enforce against its own army. Julian accepted them rather than embarrass the settlement he was proud of. In July Vienna has the commission's own reports in its hands.
- **The oil is a contract, and he keeps contracts.** Pacifica supplies fuel oil to the German navy under contracts Ballin brokered in 1912. Victoria and the Five urge him to say in Berlin that deliveries stop if Vienna refuses the inquiry. He refuses, for a reason that is his virtue: Pacifica's credit rests on never breaking a contract for politics. He breaks it on 25 July, when it no longer matters, and the contract is broken anyway.
- **The man who sets him aside.** On 5 July Jagow is away, and Pacifica's envoy is received by the under-secretary, Arthur Zimmermann, the same day Hoyos is. Zimmermann hears him out and gives him nothing. In January 1917, as Foreign Secretary, he sends the offer to Washington (canon §30) that breaks Germany's 1909 undertaking. Checked: on 5 July Hoyos met Zimmermann because Jagow, Moltke and Tirpitz were on leave; the Emperor's assurance came at lunch at Potsdam that day, confirmed by Bethmann Hollweg on the 6th ([Hoyos Mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoyos_Mission)).

The July passage in Stage 2 is revised to match.

### R8 — the charges

Each man is tried for what he did, on evidence that exists in this timeline: the decree of 5 September 1918, the published Cheka lists, and the orders below. **VERIFY** each order's text and date before integration.
- **Lenin:** the Council of People's Commissars that decreed the Red Terror sat under him. His own orders of August 1918 (Penza) direct the public hanging of peasants as an example.
- **Trotsky:** his 1918 orders to take the families of former officers as hostages against desertion, and the reprisals his commanders carried out.
- **Stalin:** the executions at Tsaritsyn in 1918, and the underground's killings in Petrograd in 1920. Under K1 revision 5 he is never tried; the charges remain the fallback.

Lenin and Trotsky are tried together in Moscow and executed in late 1920; Stalin is tried separately and executed in 1921 (A's dates).

### R12 — Stalin in Petrograd

*Superseded by K1 (APPROVED): Stalin dies at Kronstadt. The text below is kept as provenance.*

Stalin knows Petrograd: in 1919 he was sent there to organise its defence against Yudenich. When Moscow falls he goes north to make Petrograd and the Kronstadt fleet the last base. Petrograd falls to Yudenich by land in March–April 1920, but Kronstadt, on its island in the ice, holds out. Kiyoshi's negotiated surrender of the fortress in May (K1) takes it from Stalin. He stays in the city underground, his cells killing officials and informers, until the Russian security service, working with coalition intelligence, takes him later in 1920.

### R13 — Ka Lōkahi

The council of the crowns forms in 1907 and reviews its agreed matters in 1917 and 1927. **PROPOSED mechanism:** at the 1927 review the three delegations agree that Japan joins. The Concord makes Japan the coequal second party opposite Julian's three crowns, and the council's Hawaiian name passes to the larger union: Ka Lōkahi has grown. The three internal votes continue on the crowns' side, and the Hayama Council and five-year executive term remain distinct.

The 1927 illegality still holds. The council's vote binds the Hawaiian and Philippine crowns, but the Realm's delegation votes without the Senate's consent, so the provisional application is unlawful for the Realm until 1928. ### K1 — Kiyoshi's death: Kronstadt, May 1920 (revision 5)

The author on the first K1 draft (28 October 2026):

> I like the premise of Kiyoshi's death but it's too pacifist for his character and also quite disappointing. I want him to die a beautiful warrior's death in the heights of brutal glory worthy of a true noble son of a Samurai bloodline. What I mean is he should die killing Stalin or something on that level.

Kept from revision 4: Kronstadt in May 1920 and the battleship *Petropavlovsk*. The negotiated surrender and the mine are dropped.

**Why it fits.**
- **Why Kiyoshi is in the Baltic.** In this war Siberia is the rear and the decision is in European Russia. After the 1919 peace opens the Baltic, the coalition sends a squadron alongside the British force already there. Its ships come from Japan's Mediterranean squadron (canon §30), with Pacifican cruisers. Kiyoshi takes command as the coalition's senior admiral, and leaves the Siberian line to Vance.
- **Why Stalin is at Kronstadt.** In June 1919 he directed the recapture of the Krasnaya Gorka fort from the sea, so he knows these waters and this fleet. He told Lenin that the naval experts had called it contrary to naval science, and that he deplored that science ([telegram of 16 June 1919](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/stalin/works/1919/06/16.htm)). When Moscow falls he goes north to make Petrograd and Kronstadt the last base (R12).
- **What drives the clock.** The hostages are the R6 thread paying off: the last of the Pacifican railway advisers taken in 1918, with the families of fleet officers who went over to the Whites. They make an assault urgent and a bombardment impossible.

**Revision 6.** The author asked for something cleverer than a charge or a parley (28 October 2026):

> This is good but still too basic. I want you to think of something really clever. How does Kiyoshi use subterfuge and / or phronesis to gain the upper hand in a surprising way and then cut Stalin down after he lashes out like a madman?

Kiyoshi's insight is that Stalin holds the ship by fear and by one belief: that he shares his men's fate. Kiyoshi takes that belief away in front of them, and keeps faith with every rule of the parley while he does it.

**Storyline text (APPROVED — CR-040 K1):**

> Kronstadt is the last Red fortress. When Petrograd falls by land in the spring of 1920, Stalin crosses the ice to the island with the Petrograd Cheka and the fleet's commissars. He makes the battleship *Petropavlovsk* his headquarters and keeps the hostages aboard her: the families of officers who went over to the Whites, and the last of the Pacifican railway advisers taken in 1918. When the ice goes out at the end of April and the coalition's Baltic squadron comes up the channel, he sends word that the hostages will be shot if a single ship enters the roads.
>
> Kuroda Kiyoshi does not enter the roads. The sailors' families live in Petrograd, which the coalition now holds, and he lets their letters cross under a white flag every morning. The commissars read every one, and cannot stop them without losing the crew. In the second week a reply comes back in clumsy Japanese characters. It is from a senior boatswain's mate of the *Petropavlovsk*, one of the men Kiyoshi took off the captured cruiser in 1905, who learned a little Japanese from the interpreters in the camp at Matsuyama. He writes where the hostages are kept, how many Chekists there are, and what the crew thinks of them.
>
> Stalin demands that the admiral come aboard alone to bargain. Kiyoshi accepts, in full dress with his sword, and asks for only one thing: an interpreter who speaks Japanese, so that nothing is lost. Stalin trusts no coalition interpreter and will not let one aboard. He sends for the only man in the ship who speaks Japanese. To make the admiral see who holds the ship, he packs the wardroom with his Chekists, and while they stand behind him the boatswain's mates quietly take over the guard below.
>
> Kiyoshi makes the offer plainly and has it translated in full: a neutral ship to any port Stalin names, for him alone, in exchange for the hostages. Stalin refuses with contempt, for his men to hear. Kiyoshi bows and turns to them. Since their commissar will not take the terms for himself, he says, he offers them instead: any man who lays down his rifle goes ashore to his family in Petrograd tonight. Stalin sees his guards look at one another, and then at him; a sailor appears in the doorway to say the hostage deck is in the crew's hands. He understands that the admiral never came to bargain with him, and he loses his head. He shoots the interpreter, then fires twice into Kiyoshi.
>
> Kiyoshi was raised in Satsuma, whose school of the sword teaches that the blade stays sheathed until it is needed and that there is no second stroke. He draws and cuts in one motion, and Stalin is dead before he falls. The guards throw down their rifles; the crew holds the doors; the shots are the signal for the cutters waiting in the dark. Kiyoshi lives long enough to see the hostages brought up alive and the red flag hauled down, and dies on the quarterdeck in the morning light. The fleet surrenders without a gun fired into the town. The ensign that goes up is the St Andrew's flag of the navy he fought in 1905, and the boatswain's mate who brought him aboard is buried with the honours of both navies.

**Why each move holds up.**
- **The letters.** Petrograd is in coalition hands, so the crew's families are Kiyoshi's to protect. Stopping the letters would turn the crew against the commissars, and allowing them opens a channel.
- **The informant.** Japan held over 70,000 Russian prisoners in 1904–1905 under the Hague rules, with camp interpreters who met weekly and a Russian committee that thanked Japan for their treatment ([JACAR](https://www.jacar.go.jp/wp/newsletter_en/newsletter_047e/newsletter_etc3_047e/)). A sailor from Kiyoshi's 1905 prize is plausible, and so is his goodwill.
- **The crew.** The *Petropavlovsk*'s crew turned on the commissars historically: in February–March 1921 it passed the Kronstadt resolution demanding the abolition of the party's political departments and armed detachments ([Berkman](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/berkman/1922/kronstadt-rebellion/ch2.htm)). Here the resentment comes a year early.
- **The interpreter.** Stalin's distrust chooses the interpreter for Kiyoshi. The parley itself is the diversion: the Chekists are in the wardroom because Stalin wants an audience.
- **The offer.** It is genuine, so there is no perfidy, and Kiyoshi breaks no rule of the parley. Stalin fires first. The offer forces him into a choice that loses either way: accept and abandon his men, or refuse and watch the terms pass to them.
- **The cut.** Jigen-ryū is Satsuma's own school: attack only, no defensive techniques, and the sword not drawn unnecessarily ([Jigen-ryū Hyoho](https://www.jigen-ryu.com/?p=2037)). One draw-cut at the distance of a wardroom table is realistic, even after two pistol hits; the Nagant revolver's round is light. The tradition's saying that there is no need for a second stroke is widely quoted but not on the school's own page (**VERIFY**).

### R9 — the Romanovs

The author, 28 October 2026:

> I would add it but with the slight change that the Romanovs were killed by the local authorities to prevent them from being rescued. Julian may have had his private reasons for not wanting that rescue to happen, but he doesn't need to say it. The rescue doesn't have a chance to happen. By the time they reach Yekaterinburg, they have already been killed.

**Decided (APPROVED).**
- **No rescue, no refusal scene.** No rescue is planned or refused on the page. Julian's private reasons for not wanting one are never stated; the storyline neither says nor hints that he would have refused. The decision simply never comes to him.
- **Killed to prevent a rescue.** On the night of 16–17 July 1918, as the coalition and the Legion close on the Urals, the Ural Soviet's men shoot Nicholas, Alexandra, their five children, their doctor and three servants in the cellar of the Ipatiev House, so that no one can free them. The next night the Romanovs held at Alapayevsk are thrown alive down a mineshaft.
- **Too late.** The Legion takes Yekaterinburg on 25 July (historical date), eight days later.

**Proposed for the storyline text (Stage 3).**
- **Found, not hidden.** Pacifican investigators come in with the Legion: Polytechnic chemists and surveyors, with the Office of Public Measures. The bodies were moved from the first mine to a pit on the Koptyaki road after a lorry stuck. By questioning the villagers, following the lorry's tracks and surveying the soil, they find the grave within weeks. Historically it stayed hidden until 1979–1991. In the autumn of 1918 the Office publishes the cellar, the grave and the evidence beside the Cheka's own lists (R6).
- **The trial (Stage 4).** The murder of the family is one of the state crimes charged against Lenin (R8). The Ural Soviet carried it out; who decided is the question the court must answer. Trotsky answers it in the dock with what his diary recorded in our world: that Sverdlov told him "We decided it here", and that Lenin did not want to leave the Whites "a live banner to rally around". He names Lenin to save himself on that count, and it does not save him.
- **Burial.** When Petrograd falls in 1920, Wrangel's government buries the family in the Peter and Paul Cathedral (historically, 1998). Who from Thalassia attends is **OPEN**.
- **Monarchists.** The surviving Romanovs, the Dowager Empress and Grand Duke Kirill, give monarchists a figurehead. That feeds the open question of whether federal Russia ends as a monarchy or a republic.

Sources: [Shooting of the Romanov family](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/11778341) (Trotsky's diary, the Koptyaki road burial, Alapayevsk on 18 July); [Murder of the Romanov family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murder_of_the_Romanov_family) (Yekaterinburg taken 25 July 1918).
