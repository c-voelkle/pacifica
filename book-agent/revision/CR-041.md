## 0. Summary

The Russian war of 1918–1920 is re-planned around the enemy's one year without an army. Pacifica seizes the Siberian line in one week in May 1918, holds the Volga through 1918 instead of losing Kazan, builds the Ural gate and a Russian army through the winter, and strangles Moscow's hinterland so that the Red Army of 1919 is formidable on paper and starving in fact. Moscow still falls in March 1920, to Russians. Nothing in the approved 1920 sequence (Tula, the Oka, Moscow, Petrograd, the Vyatka line, Kronstadt) moves; what changes is 1918–19, the signatory, and the mechanism of the 1919 bargain.

The signatory is a sequence, not a man: Komuch at Samara (June–September 1918), the Ufa Directory (from 23 September 1918, which survives because the Omsk coup fails), and Wrangel with Krivoshein from October 1919, who inherit a signature rather than dictating a price. The Siberian treaty is signed in July 1918, before any Russian general gets a rifle, and ratified publicly on 10 September 1918. It is tri-partite: the Russian signatory, Siberia, and Alash.

The 1919 bargain changes mechanism. Wrangel cannot demand Siberia, which is signed; he asks Julian not to stand in the way when Siberia's own assembly chooses the federation, and Julian promises to deliver the vote. In 1921 the Tomsk Duma votes for the federal compact under the guarantor's eye, credit and newsreels, and nobody puts the question to Alash. The promise is kept to the letter and sold in spirit; the people betrayed are the ones not asked.

## 2. The operation (Plan C)

### 2.1 The two timetables, reconciled

The enemy's window without an army is 1918. Everything that can be seized while the regime has sixty thousand men and no road is seized in 1918: the line, the coal, the Baikal tunnels, the Urals, the works, the Kazan gold, the Volga crossings, the signatory. The Russian army that takes Moscow has to be made over a winter, so Moscow falls in the second winter. One summer to seize, one winter to strangle, the Russians take Moscow in the second winter.

### 2.2 February–May 1918: speed mode, four constraints

Nobody declares war. The Entente has not recognised the Council of People's Commissars and its ambassadors have withdrawn to Vologda; a declaration on "Russia" would recognise the Bolsheviks as Russia's government and make the Siberian government and the Directory the enemy. After Brest-Litovsk (3 March) Russia is a neutral; the Entente's legal form everywhere is protection of stores, nationals and the Czechoslovak transit at the request of local Russian authorities, and Pacifica uses it. The recognition of a state of war comes in September 1918 and is not a declaration on Russia: when the Cheka takes the railway advisers hostage, Thalassia declares the Council of People's Commissars a conspiracy in arms against the Russian people and its lawful government, now the Directory, and the Office publishes it beside the Terror decree. "The Russian war" is the Chronist's name.

The four constraints that fix D-day: the Legion must be spread along the line (otherwise it is the hostage, not the striking force); the thaw; the Pacific crossing; and the Entente's hope of turning Trotsky, which dies with the Aisne. All four release in the last ten days of May.

The line: coal from Cheremkhovo, Anzhero-Sudzhensk and the Kuzbass, Suchan and Chelyabinsk; Pacifican oil-fired engines as reserve. ### 2.4 Five movements

**1. The Week (26 May–10 June 1918).** The Legion seizes the stations on its historical dates; the railway brigade takes the workshops at Omsk, Krasnoyarsk, Chita and Irkutsk; the Japanese 12th Division takes the tunnels and Irkutsk; the Transbaikal Cossacks open the line to Chita; one Pacifican division lands at Vladivostok (Urajio) and the second comes up through Harbin. By 10 June the line is coalition and Legion from the Pacific to the Volga; the Yenisei–Baikal section is cleared by 11 July. No battle larger than the Legion's fight for Chelyabinsk.

**2. The Volga summer (June–September 1918).** Kappel and the Legion go up the Volga: Simbirsk 22 July, Kazan 7 August with the Imperial gold reserve (650 million rubles). The Pacifican corps takes Yekaterinburg (25 July) and Chelyabinsk and builds the gate; it sends west a river flotilla (eight motor gunboats shipped by rail, 4.7-inch guns) and a brigade. The Izhevsk–Votkinsk workers' rising of 8 August (~15,000 at peak, *hist.*) is relieved from Yekaterinburg in September instead of being crushed in November. The Siberian treaty is signed in late July (§4.2); the gold goes into a Reserve Bank escrow at Chelyabinsk against it. **Second Kazan, 5–12 September:** Kappel 4,000, Czechs 3,000, Pacifican brigade 6,000 and the flotilla against the 5th Army (~12,000) and Raskolnikov's flotilla with the Sokol-class destroyers brought through the Mariinsk canals (*hist.*); the turbine gunboats outgun the destroyers in the shallows at the Kama mouth and the Red landing is shot off the bank. **Kazan held.** The Volga is the line through the winter.

**3. The gate (September 1918–February 1919).** The Directory forms at Ufa on 23 September and inherits the treaty. The Omsk coup of 18 November fails (N2). Pepelyaev's corps (~45,000) takes Perm on 24 December against the collapsing 3rd Army (~36,000; 18,000 lost, *hist.*). In January 1919 the Pacifican 1st Division with Siberian cavalry takes the Perm–Vyatka line to Glazov and cuts the Vologda–Vyatka line at the Kotelnich bridge by a cavalry raid; Vyatka town stays Red as the 3rd Army's last base. Through the winter Vance fills the depots and the Directory, with Boldyrev commanding and Pacifican instructors in every regiment, builds a Russian army at Chelyabinsk and Omsk on the Kazan gold: about 120,000 at the front by April. Moscow does not see the Siberian harvest of 1918.

**4. The enemy comes (March–June 1919).** The Red Eastern Front, about 110,000–140,000 (*hist.* April 1919), attacks the Volga and the Kama. **The Volga battle, 25 May–15 June 1919:** the Directory's Volga Army (50,000, Boldyrev; Kappel's corps in reserve), Pacifican corps artillery and air, the flotilla at spring high water, and Vance on the Volga bridge at Zelenodolsk (N4). Frunze's groups cannot cross under the guns; Kappel's counterstroke at Alatyr on the southern group's flank. Nothing changes hands, which is the victory. From July the Perm wing is supplied from the north by Dvina barges to Kotlas and rail Kotlas–Vyatka river–Glazov (20,000 tons a summer).

**5. Winter (November 1919–March 1920).** Denikin stalls at Orel in October; Wrangel takes the southern command (S2). Three columns: Kappel's eastern army (60,000) crosses the frozen Volga in December and takes Nizhny Novgorod and the Sormovo works, then Arzamas and Murom toward Ryazan; the Perm wing (one Pacifican division, 20,000) goes down the frozen Vyatka; Kutepov (30,000) and Wrangel's cavalry (15,000) hold Orel and take Tula in February (approved). The Oka, Serpukhov, Trotsky (N9); Moscow in March; Petrograd by land in March–April; the Vyatka line and Lenin (N9); Kronstadt in May (K1). All as approved.

## 3. Instruments

### 3.1 The money as a weapon
The Reserve Bank's paymasters release credit against results at Novorossiysk, Samara and Omsk and nowhere else. The land act, the Siberian treaty and the Directory's survival are the conditions. The man who feeds and pays commands.

### 3.2 Command
A council command would wait. The field goes to the Japanese commander east of the Yenisei and to the Russian commanders west of the Urals; Vance holds the line and the depots; Julian holds the money. Julian sits at Irkutsk with the war council and does not command an army.

### 3.3 The three seas
- **Black Sea (Wrangel's supply):** from October 1919 one convoy a month, San Francisco–Panama–Gibraltar–Constantinople–Novorossiysk (~35 days), six to eight transports, 50,000–60,000 tons: rifles, guns, shell, oil and petrol, lorries, the paymasters. Escort one Pacifican cruiser and two destroyers.
- **White Sea (the Perm wing):** two sailings a summer to Archangel from 1919; Dvina barges to Kotlas, rail to Glazov; ~20,000 tons.
- **Baltic (Kiyoshi's squadron):** *Izumo* (flag; Tsushima veteran, Japan's Mediterranean flagship from June 1917), *Nisshin*, *Akashi*, twelve Japanese destroyers (Kaba and Momo classes), two Pacifican turbine cruisers (oil-fired, 6-inch), six Pacifican destroyers, four minesweepers, a seaplane tender with eight aircraft, an oiler, a hospital ship, eight motor torpedo boats carried on deck, two transports with a Pacifican marine battalion and a Japanese naval landing force (~2,000). About 35 ships. Japanese ships retained in European waters by Tokyo's decision in spring 1919 (historically *Izumo* reached Japan on 2 July 1919); Pacificans through Panama; rendezvous Copenhagen September 1919; winter at Reval and Libau beside Cowan's British force. Autumn 1919: lands Yudenich's rifles, shells and forty armoured cars at Reval and Narva-Jõesuu; Estonia, paid in Pacifican credit, does not make the Tartu peace. Winter: nothing moves east of Reval; the eastern Gulf is ice December–April; Kronstadt opens about the fifth of May (*hist.* average), so K1's "end of April" is the first open water. Spring 1920: aircraft spot for Yudenich against Krasnaya Gorka; the squadron enters the roads at the end of April (K1); the motor torpedo boats are the cutters in the dark.

### 3.4 The two train actions
**Serpukhov, 26–27 February 1920.** Trotsky's train (formed 7 August 1918; two armoured locomotives, armoured turret wagons, flatbeds with motor cars including the Tsar's Rolls-Royce, telegraph and radio, printing presses, a wagon with a collapsible aircraft; a guard of about a hundred Latvians in red leather and a staff of some three hundred, *hist.*) goes south on 25 February to hold the Oka at Serpukhov, where the Moscow–Kursk bridge crosses and Kutepov's advance guard is on the south bank. Wrangel's cavalry cuts the line behind it at Lopasnya and blows the culvert. On the 26th the Volunteer armoured train *Ofitser* (*hist.*: captured from the Reds in summer 1918) comes up the south embankment; the two trains fight across the river at two kilometres for an hour; a shell disables Trotsky's lead locomotive; the escort, backing north, finds the culvert gone and the cavalry on the embankment. Kutepov's infantry crosses the ice above and below the bridge in the night; after a six-hour siege the train surrenders at first light on the 27th. Trotsky taken alive (approved). A Russian action with no Pacifican hand in it.

**Kotelnich, April 1920.** Lenin leaves Moscow by the northern road through Yaroslavl to Vologda, intending to reach the 3rd Army at Vyatka (approved). The Pacifican armoured train *Māui* (built in the Omsk works in 1918: two oil-fired locomotives, eight armoured cars, two 4.7-inch naval guns, a dozen machine guns, a wireless car, a company of the railway brigade) runs west from Glazov through Vyatka's suburbs under fire to the Kotelnich bridge, where the river is a kilometre wide, with two squadrons of Russian cavalry on the frozen banks. The engineers lift a rail behind the last siding west of the bridge and replace it with one cut through. Lenin's escort train attacks the bridge and is wrecked in a quarter of an hour; Lenin's train backs to the siding and derails on the cut rail; the cavalry closes. After a night in which the Pacifican commander declines to come aboard, the government surrenders to a railway engineer at a river bridge and is handed to the Russian cavalry for trial (approved). Red armoured trains ran in heavy–light pairs with about fifteen miles' range between water stops (*hist.*); water towers are objectives in both actions.

### 4.2 The Siberian treaty, July 1918 (tri-partite)

Signed at Samara and Omsk in late July 1918 by Komuch for Russia, the Provisional Siberian Government, and Alash Orda, under Pacifican guarantee, printed by the Office and deposited in Peking and Tokyo (S3). It builds on two real documents: Komuch's declaration recognising Alash autonomy pending the Constituent Assembly, and the Sapozhnikov–Bukeikhanov draft agreement of 29 July–3 August 1918 between the Siberian government and Alash Orda (army, courts, kibitka tax, mixed institutions under joint commissars in Akmolinsk, Semipalatinsk and Turgai, land status quo). Pacifica's additions: the mutual-recognition clause Alash dropped at Omsk is restored; Komuch's suspensive veto over Alash is replaced by the guarantor's arbitration; Siberia and Alash are recognised as two autonomies in union, with "a federal power of autonomous Siberia and autonomous Alash, or an all-Russian federal power created with their participation" as the agreed horizon (the draft's own words); land law enforcement (N3) and the Alash land status quo are conditions of credit; the Emba concession to Pacifica is signed with the Western Alash Orda at the same time (N14). The Ministry of Agriculture and Colonisation's dissent (Yarmosh: the political relationship is left undefined and will become "acute and dangerous") is recorded; the Chronist quotes it in 1921.

Ratified publicly by Julian on 10 September 1918, two days before Bukeikhanov's Ufa speech for a Russia "federated, democratic and unified" (*hist.*, 12 September 1918). The Directory's decree of 4 November 1918 abolishing regional governments (which historically named Alash Orda) is not issued; the Directory instead confirms the treaty as a condition of Pacifican credit.

### 4.3 The 1919 bargain and the 1921 vote (N6)

In autumn 1919 Wrangel's price is the unity of Russia, Siberia included (approved R10). He cannot ask for Siberia, which is signed; he asks that the coalition not stand in the way when Siberia's own assembly chooses the federation, and that Pacifica's credit, the Office's films and the railway board's payrolls lean the way Omsk already leans. Julian gives his word, which is the trade. Victoria tells him once, privately, what she will say openly in 1921 (S3).

In 1921, at Harbin, the Siberian Regional Duma at Tomsk, elected under the guarantor's eye and financed by the guarantor's bank, votes to enter the federal compact as a land of Russia from the Ob to Baikal, governed from Irkutsk (approved R11). The vote is free and it is not free. Nobody puts the question to Alash: the Alash–Siberia union of 1918 is dissolved in the same act and Alash becomes a land of Russia, keeping its courts and its kibitka tax and losing its army and its equal standing; the Yarmosh gap is closed in Russia's favour. Yakutia keeps its land statute (CR-031) and loses the state it was a land of. The regionalist minority who wanted a Siberian state, and Kazakhs from the Semipalatinsk edge, go into the Altai: the partisans of 1921 (approved R10) now include them. Pacifica's guarantee of Siberia's frontier becomes a guarantee of the federal compact (approved, MODEL).

Victoria's charge (approved chain: the promise of 1918, the trade of 1919, the gas, the lie) gains its second count: he asked one people and not the other. Her approved line, "a federation imposed by a victor is not consent," now describes a vote nobody forced and nobody freed. Julian's approved answer, "Siberia's own regionalists were federalists from the start," is literally true (Sapozhnikov at Ufa). The storyline gives no verdict.

### 4.4 Oil (N14)
Emba: Pacifican concession from 1918, survives the 1921 transfer. Grozny and Maikop: concessions from Wrangel's government against the reconstruction credit. Baku: joint Anglo-Pacifican concession with Azerbaijan, Pacifica operating, Britain strategic. London's price for its share is one of the things it collects in 1927.

## Appendix B — The Napoleon correspondence (non-canonical, author's instrument)

*Three letters written on 29 October 2026 as a planning exercise: Napoleon at St Helena receives Julian's canon to October 1917 and his Russia plan; Julian's staff answers; Napoleon's plan. Kept for reference. Not canon; no character in the novel has read them.*

*Note on the premise: Napoleon knows only what the historical Napoleon knew plus what Julian's letter tells him. Julian's reply was written before Plan C; it describes the Ob-line plan Napoleon then rejects. Several of its sentences ("I will keep the promise", "I am not looking for a Russian who can take Moscow", "I will not forgive the debt for a flag") are deliberately contradicted by the approved canon of 1919–21.*

### B.1 Napoleon's first letter (the questions)

**Longwood, St Helena**

Monsieur,

Your letter reached me by a road I do not understand, and I have decided not to try. Fifty years on this rock would not be enough to explain Austerlitz to a man who had never seen a cannon, and you ask me to understand railways, oil, wireless cables and a fleet that burns no coal from twelve pages. Very well. I have read your pages three times. I accept the premise that you are who you say you are and that the world has gone as you describe. I do not accept that you have told me enough to advise you, and I will not give advice on a map I cannot see. Send me maps. Large ones. I have never in my life been able to think without a map on the floor.

You address the one man in Europe who marched to Moscow and came home with nothing. You could hardly have chosen worse, or better. I will tell you what I learned, as questions, because that is what the lesson was.

**I. The object.** You write of a "Russia campaign" and of a Siberia governed on your own model behind a line on the Ob. I had an object in 1812 too: to force Alexander back into my system of commerce. I took his second city and he did not write to me. What I had not asked myself was what I would do if the man I meant to compel declined to be compelled. So: what is the condition of peace you intend to impose, on whom will you impose it, and what do you do if he refuses it from a town three hundred leagues further east? If your answer is "hold the Ob," tell me why the men in Petrograd would respect a river. I respected none. A frontier is a treaty, not a line of water, and a treaty needs a signatory. Who signs for Russia?

**II. The enemy.** You describe the men now in power with contempt, as a government of sailors and agitators. I was a government of sailors and agitators. I know exactly what a revolution can put into the field in eighteen months, and I know that the one army an invader manufactures for his enemy is the army of national defence. In 1812 the peasants burned their own villages rather than feed me. Tell me, then: in the Russia of 1917, whom does the peasant fear more, the commissar who takes his grain, or the landlord who will come back behind your columns? The answer decides the war. Everything else is arithmetic.

**III. The Germans.** You are at war with Germany. Your letter says the new masters of Petrograd mean to make peace with Berlin. Then understand what you are proposing: you would open a second war while the first is unfinished, against a power that is about to become the client of your enemy. I never willingly fought two wars; Spain was forced on me and it was the ulcer that killed me. Either Russia's new government is Germany's instrument, in which case your Russian war is your German war and you must say so to London before London says something worse about you, or it is not, in which case explain to me why you choose this moment. Can you reach a peace with Germany first? What does your treasury say about carrying both?

**IV. The ground.** From your eastern ports to Moscow you give me a distance I can scarcely credit, along a single iron road through a country of which I know only that it is larger than everything I ever ruled. My line from the Niemen to Moscow was two hundred and fifty leagues of good road in summer, and it killed my army before the Russians did. So:

1. How many trains can that road carry in a day, and how many men does one train feed for one day?
2. Who holds the road? Not your garrisons, the country. If a band of fifty men with axes can stop your army for a week, your army is a hostage.
3. Where else can you enter? Your partners hold the Baltic approaches and your allies the White Sea. You once ran convoys through the ice to the Ob and the Yenisei. Can a second line of operation come from the north or west, so that the enemy must face two directions?
4. What of winter? I do not ask whether it is cold. I ask what your plan says happens in the four months when it is.

**V. Your partners.** You count on Japan, China and Siberians. I count on nothing I cannot command. In every coalition I ever fought I found the seam between the allies and put my army through it. Who commands? Is the Japanese army under your general or beside him? What does Tokyo want in Siberia that you do not? What does Peking demand for the use of its line over the mountains, and what happens the day it demands more? And the Siberians: are they your allies, your subjects or your excuse?

Here I will say something plainly, since the next paragraph of your letter will reveal whether you have understood me. I raised a Polish army on the promise of Poland. Eighty thousand men followed me because they believed I would give them a country. I never gave it, because I kept the Austrian and Russian cards in my hand and a country for the Poles would have cost me both. They were the best troops I had, and I spent them. If you intend to promise the Siberians a state, tell me now whether you mean to keep the promise when a Russian general offers you Moscow for the price of it. Because one will. I would have.

**VI. The Russians on your side.** Who are they, and where? A Russia cannot be governed from the baggage train of a foreigner; I put my brother on the throne of Spain and the Spaniards spent five years teaching me that. If there is a Russian who can take Moscow with Russian soldiers, your coalition's purpose is to make him possible, not to replace him, and the whole shape of the campaign changes. Is there such a man? What does he want, and is it the same thing you want? Does he pay his debts?

**VII. The instruments.** I read of guns that fire six hundred times a minute, of wire that stops horses, of machines that fly, and of a gas. I understand none of it. Describe to me, as to a child, how infantry crosses an open field in your time, how far a battle extends, and how long one lasts. Then tell me what your fleet can do that your army cannot: you have a Baltic enemy with more battleships than it ever had in my day, and sailors who are the enemy's best troops. Where are those ships, and who in the enemy's camp is clever enough to use them as a fortress?

**VIII. The man.** Will you command in person? I ask because you tell me you spent six years in the capitals of Europe trying to prevent this war and failed, and that you used nothing in July 1914 that cost you anything. A man who will not spend his credit at the decisive moment should not begin a campaign that will need all of it. Your mother the Queen, who gave you the crown and the lesson that seeing is believing — does she approve this war, and will she still approve it when she sees it?

Answer these and I will give you a plan, or tell you not to go. Answer them badly and I will know that too.

One more thing. You say you are not surprised by the fall of the Tsar; your engineers watched the bread fail in the capital because the trains could not bring it. Then you already know the only thing about Russia that matters. Whoever feeds Petrograd rules it. Build your campaign on that sentence and you may not need the rest of my questions.

I remain, on this island where the English keep me so that I cannot answer letters like yours,

**N.**

*P.S. — Do not send me the Siberian winter in prose. Send me the thermometer readings, by month, at the stations on your road. I have a precise interest.*

### B.2 Julian's reply

*Written after six weeks' staff work by the war council, the railway board, the Polytechnic, the Treasury, the Office of Public Measures and the Japanese liaison staff, with the consuls at Irkutsk, Tomsk and Harbin reporting. Victoria read Napoleon's letter before Julian answered it.*

**Thalassia, 20 December 1917**

Sire,

Your questions cost me six weeks and the sleep of about two hundred men. I will answer them in your order, and where I have no answer I will say so, because you would know anyway.

The maps come with this: the railway board's atlas of the line from Harbin to Chelyabinsk, sheet by sheet at twenty versts to the inch, with gradients, water stations, bridges and the places where it is doubled; the Polytechnic's map of Russia west of the Urals; and the hydrographic office's charts of the Gulf of Finland, the White Sea and the Kara Sea approaches. The thermometer tables are Annex C. You asked for them by month and by station; you have them for nine stations and thirty years, in Réaumur degrees, since I supposed that was what your artillery school used.

**I. The object.** I will give it to you as I gave it to my council, in three sentences. The railway, its grain and the engines my savers paid for stay out of the hands of Berlin and of the men in Petrograd. Siberia east of the Urals becomes a country of its own, with a government of Siberians, guaranteed by the coalition on a line we can hold. The men in Petrograd are left to a Russia that will not feed them.

You asked who signs for Russia. Nobody, yet. You asked why the men in Petrograd would respect a river. They would not. My railway board's answer is that the Ob is not a frontier but a quartermaster's limit: it is the last line the single track can feed through a winter. The staff do not believe the enemy will stop there either. They believe he will be too weak in 1918 to reach it, and that by the time he is strong enough we must have a Russia on the other side that is not him. Write that down against me; I think you will.

**II. The enemy.** I asked twelve men who have spent the last three years in Russia, and they gave me one answer. The peasant fears the landlord more. He has taken the land this year and no decree will make him give it back; any army that marches with the old proprietors in its baggage will lose the villages before it reaches them. He fears the commissar's grain requisition only when it arrives, and in the Siberian provinces there are no landlords to fear because there never were. That is the whole case for Siberia and the whole case against marching further: east of the Urals the land question does not exist, west of them it is the war.

The regime itself is six weeks old. It has the Petrograd garrison, the Baltic sailors, the Red Guard of the factories and the Latvian regiments, perhaps sixty thousand men who will fight, and it has the apparatus of the old army dissolving around it. It does not yet have an army. My staff say that is the only year in which it will not.

**III. The Germans.** Five days ago the new government signed an armistice with Germany at Brest-Litovsk, and its delegates are talking peace there now. So the question you put is already answered by events: a peace that gives Germany the Ukrainian grain and the Donets coal and frees forty divisions for France makes Petrograd an instrument of Berlin whether it wishes to be or not. I will say that to London and Paris when I move, and I will say it in their own words: the Entente's objectives, not mine. They will take it, because they need my credit more than my regiments, and because they cannot reach Siberia and Japan can.

Can I make peace with Germany first? Not yet. Germany believes it is about to win, and a man who believes he is about to win does not negotiate. In the autumn of next year, when the American divisions are in France and the blockade has done another year's work, I intend to stop the Treasury's lending to the Entente and offer the Central Powers bread for an armistice. That is the lever I did not use in July 1914. The Treasury says I can carry both wars until then, provided Japan lends to Russia's successor what it has been lending to Russia since last year, and provided I do not promise Wrangel's kind of Russian anything in gold. I note the condition.

**IV. The ground.** Annex A is the railway board's, and I will give you the figures that matter.

The line from Harbin to the Ob is single track over the Khingan and through Transbaikalia, double from Omsk westward. On the single stretches it passes twelve pairs of trains a day; on the doubled stretches, up to thirty. A freight train is forty wagons at sixteen tons, six hundred and forty tons, if the engine is one of ours. One such train a day feeds and arms a division holding a line; in battle a division eats two. Half the trains must carry the line's own coal, the railwaymen's bread and the country's commerce, or the line stops of itself. So: at the Ob, the line sustains five or six divisions fighting; at the Urals, three or four; at Moscow, as your army was sustained from Smolensk.

Who holds the road: the railway workers, whose workshops at Krasnoyarsk, Chita and Omsk are the strongest Bolshevik cells in Siberia; the Cossack hosts of the Transbaikal and the Irtysh, who hold the junctions and will sell them; and the Siberian peasantry, who hold nothing but can stop everything, as you say. The board's doctrine is that the line is held by the men who run it: our crews have run the engines since 1915, and a railwayman who is paid in Pacifican gold and whose family is fed from our depots does not strike. It is a doctrine. It has not been tested.

A second line of operation: the Baltic is closed by the German fleet until there is a German peace; the Black Sea by the Turk. There remain the White Sea, where the British sit at Archangel and will soon sit at Murmansk, and the Kara Sea, which is open to ships for six weeks from August and lets a convoy carry stores up the Ob and the Yenisei to the line. The staff has a plan for both. Neither makes a second front. They make the Ob a depot instead of a terminus. The rivers of the east, the Amur and the Shilka, do the same from May to October. Everything in this war is seasonal, Sire, except the enemy.

Winter: the plan stops. Not because of the cold, which the Siberians do not notice, but because the rivers close and the single track must carry what the barges carried. The depots are filled while the water runs; a division goes forward only when the line can feed it, and in January it feeds fewer. I have an officer whose whole mind is this sentence, Marcus Vance, and I have given him the line.

**V. The partners.** Japan's army is twenty-one divisions, three of them already in the Primorye and on the Manchurian border, with the ports of the east in their hands since 1899; you will note on the map that my coalition's door to Russia is a Japanese door. Tokyo wants the Amur, Northern Sakhalin, the fisheries and a Russia that cannot come back; it will go into Siberia whether I lead or not. That is my private reason for going, and I give it to you because you asked for the seam. The joint war council of 1907 gives us a common plan at sea and nothing on land. I propose a council command with a Japanese field commander and the line under Vance; Tokyo will accept it if the Siberian state we create has a Japanese share in it, and I have decided it shall.

Peking: its price is the Chinese Eastern Railway back in Chinese hands, Outer Mongolia back from the Russians who detached it in 1911, and the end of the conventions of 1860 in the north. All three are things the men in Petrograd have also promised China and will never deliver. I can deliver them because a Siberian government will not refuse me, and I intend to pay in advance, in public, so that the day Peking asks for more there is a treaty to point at.

The Siberians: in two days a Siberian Regional Duma convenes at Tomsk; the men who called it have wanted a Siberian country since before I was born. They are allies, and they are the purpose. Now your Poles. I have read that paragraph more times than the rest of your letter together, and I will give you the answer I gave my mother when she asked the same question in different words. I will keep the promise. I am going into Siberia to build a country, not to hold a line, and if a Russian general offers me Moscow for the price of it I will tell him that Pacifica exists because a province refused to be handed back. You say one will offer. I believe you. I am writing this down so that you can hold it against me, and so can she.

**VI. The Russians on my side.** The old army's generals are at Bykhov under arrest or on the Don, where Alekseyev is raising volunteers; it will be a small army of officers, and an army of officers is a landlord's army in the eyes of the peasant. Krivoshein, Stolypin's minister, whom I met in 1908, is a man who could govern a Russia; he is not a man who could take one. Admiral Kolchak passed through San Francisco in October on his way home from the American navy, and dined with my staff; a brave man and a sailor, and the staff's note on him reads "will want all of Russia and cannot hold Omsk." I have no Russian who can take Moscow with Russian soldiers, and I am not looking for one, because of what I said under V. Whoever ever signs for Russia must sign for the Russian debt. My savers hold it, and I will not forgive it for a flag.

**VII. The instruments.** Annex B is the war college's primer, written for you. The short answer: artillery decides, and infantry does not cross a field; it follows a curtain of shellfire across one, into trenches that the guns have emptied, and the machine guns on the far side kill whoever arrives before the curtain. A battle is weeks long and a front is hundreds of miles. That is France. Russia is too large and too empty for it; there the war goes back to movement, and movement means the railway, armoured trains, and cavalry, which has come back from the dead. The gas exists; Haber's Germany used it first and everyone uses it now. We have it. I would rather not need it.

The Baltic fleet: nine battleships and two dozen cruisers and destroyers, behind minefields in the Gulf of Finland, at Helsingfors and Kronstadt, with crews who are the regime's best troops and no officers left. It is a fortress and a hostage both. I cannot reach it until the German war ends. When I can, the squadron is Kuroda Kiyoshi's, my brother's, who took one of those ships' sisters in 1905 and sent her crew to a camp at Matsuyama. He says the sailors' families live in Petrograd and that a fleet is taken through its families. I do not yet understand him.

**VIII. The man.** I will not command the army. I will be at Irkutsk with the council, because a sovereign who commands a Japanese field army loses either the army or the partnership, and I need both. My mother read your letter before I did. She agrees that the regime must end. Her condition is her own: the Office publishes only what is true, lists and photographs, so that the world sees the men in Petrograd through their own words; and nothing is done in Siberia that she would have to lie about. Then she asked me what I would do if a Russian general offered me Moscow for Siberia. You and she have never met and have asked the same question in the same week, which I take as a judgment.

Your last sentence I have built the plan on. The Petrograd bread failed because the trains could not bring the Siberian and Volga grain; my railwaymen watched it. Whoever holds the Siberian grain and the line that carries it holds the only thing Petrograd cannot make. We shall hold both. The enemy will then starve or come east for it, and if he comes east he comes to where the line can feed six divisions and he can feed none.

Send me the plan, or tell me not to go.

**Julian**

### B.3 Napoleon's second letter (the plan)

**Longwood, St Helena**

Monsieur,

Your annexes are better than your plan. That is not an insult; it is the usual condition of a staff that has been asked the right questions for the first time. I have had the railway atlas on the floor of my bedroom for a fortnight, and Montholon complains that he cannot cross it without treading on Omsk. Here is my answer. It is a plan, with one refusal inside it.

**The verdict.** Go. Not because the war is good but because it is already happening without you: your Japanese partner holds your door and will walk through it whether you lead or not, and a war that one's ally begins alone is the worst war there is. You know this; you told me so under V. So go, and go this summer, because of the one fact in your letter that outweighs the rest. The enemy has sixty thousand men who will fight and no army. Monsieur, that was France in 1793. I was there. Europe gave us two years, and in two years we beat Europe. The men in Petrograd have the same material we had, the same fear of the old masters coming back, and the same gift for using it; if you give them two years they will have an army of a million peasants who believe you brought the landlord with you, and then you will fight my war of 1812 with better engines. You have one summer. Every page of your plan that speaks of 1919 should be burned.

**The error.** Your plan is a quartermaster's plan. It is excellent and it will lose the war, because it holds a river where the trains stop and waits for the enemy to come and be beaten. He will not come. He will do the thing men who shoot always do when they are short of bread: he will take it from his neighbours. The Volga is black earth from Kazan to Saratov, and he does not need your Siberian grain while he holds it. He will starve the villages, not the regime; the regime moves to Moscow and requisitions. So your sentence about Petrograd's bread is true and insufficient. Whoever feeds Petrograd rules it, yes; the regime will simply stop living in Petrograd. The decisive line of this war is not the Ob. It is the Volga, and you cannot feed a Pacifican division there. Therefore the war on the Volga must be fought by Russians, fed by Russia, and the whole object of your campaign is to make such Russians possible. Everything else you do is in their service.

**The plan.** Three movements, in one year.

*First, the line, by seizure, not by advance.* You have engine crews on every section, gold in every railwayman's pocket, and Cossack hosts who will sell the junctions. Buy them, all of them, in one week, and in that week occupy the workshops at Krasnoyarsk, Chita and Omsk with the troops you already have in the Primorye and on the Manchurian border; three Japanese divisions are more than enough against cells of workmen. Do not march up the line; appear on it everywhere at once. By the time the news reaches Petrograd the line is yours from Harbin to the Urals and the enemy has lost the only road he had to reach you. Your council will say this is not a campaign. Tell them that Ulm was not a battle either.

*Second, the gate, not the river.* Hold the Urals: Chelyabinsk, the Kama crossings, and the mountain line to Perm. Your arithmetic says three or four of your divisions there and six at the Ob; take the three, because a mountain with three divisions is worth a river with six, because the mines and works of the Urals are the enemy's arsenal and must not be his, and because from Perm you can look at Vyatka and the northern road out of Moscow, which is the road a government takes when it runs. Mark that road on your map. You will need it.

*Third, the Russian army, on the Volga, with your hands kept off its sleeve.* Here is the refusal. You wrote that you are not looking for a Russian who can take Moscow. You will have to, and I will tell you how to do it without losing Siberia, which is the only thing in your letter you care about enough to be stupid for.

**The Russian.** Not the admiral: your staff's note is exact, and besides, a sailor on a steppe is a man who thinks the land is water and will drown in it. Not the officers on the Don, for the reason you gave yourself; a landlord's army loses the village before it enters it. The man you want does not yet exist, and you must make him, by two acts.

The first act is the land. Proclaim, in your name and in the name of whatever Russian government you recognise, that the land the peasant took this year he keeps, and that no army of yours will give it back. Do it before you fire a gun, do it in print, and let your mother's Office carry it into every village the commissar's requisition reaches. In one stroke the commissar has nothing left to offer the peasant but the requisition, and you have the only thing in Russia that recruits. I will tell you something I have told no one on this island: in 1812, at Moscow, I had the decree in my hand to free the serfs, and I did not sign it, because I wanted Alexander back as an ally and an emperor does not raise a jacquerie against another emperor. It was the one order that would have won me the war. Sign yours.

The second act is the signature, and it is the answer to your Poles. You have written to me that you will keep your promise, and I believe that you believe it, and I wrote the same of Poland in 1807. A promise written down against oneself is what a man writes when he already feels its weight. The general will come to you in the second winter, when the regime has its army and you have your arithmetic, and he will offer Moscow for the price of Siberia, and you will pay, because by then Moscow will be the war and Siberia a province. I know this because I was both of you. So do not rely on your honour. Rely on paper. Before a single rifle reaches a Russian general, there must exist a Russian government, however small, however much a thing of your making, sitting at Omsk, and its first act must be a treaty recognising the Siberian state and its frontier, signed, sealed, printed by your Office and deposited in Peking and Tokyo as well. Then every general who wants your guns must take them from that government's hand and inherit that government's signature. A promise kept by treaty is one you can keep when you no longer wish to. A promise kept by conscience lasts until the first good offer. Make the Russia first; let the Russians take Moscow second; and let the price of Siberia be paid before the goods are seen, as you said yourself of Peking. You have the right instinct for the Chinese and the wrong one for yourself.

If you do this, the general who takes Moscow owes you his existence, his credit and his signature, and your mother's question has an answer she can live with. If you do not, you will win the war and she will tell you what you have become, and she will be right.

**Particulars.**

*Command.* A council command is a command that waits. Give the field to the Japanese general, entirely, and keep two things in your own hands: the line and the money. The man who feeds and pays commands; the man who gives the orders only believes he does. You will have the reality and Tokyo the honour, and that division is the only one a coalition survives.

*The door.* You have let your partner hold your line of retreat. I let Schwarzenberg hold my right in 1812 and he went home when it suited Vienna. Buy from Peking a port of your own at the end of the Chinese line and your own trains on the Khingan, pay in advance and in public, so that the day Tokyo's ambitions and yours separate, you are not standing in a Japanese doorway.

*Winter.* Your plan stops in winter. Make it the only season you attack in. A frozen river is the best road in Russia; the Berezina taught me the price of an unfrozen one. Fill the depots while the barges run, as your officer Vance intends, and then use the depots to move in January, when the enemy's rivers are roads and his barges are nothing. The Siberians do not notice the cold, you say. Then recruit Siberians, and let the cold be your ally instead of mine.

*The fleet.* Tell your brother that I understand him perfectly, and that he is the only man in your letter who thinks like a soldier. A fleet without officers is a crowd, and a crowd is governed by what it loves. He will not need to fight that fleet. He will need to be patient with it, which for a sailor is harder.

*The gas.* No. Not for the dead; I have made enough of those to lecture no one. Because the one thing a conqueror cannot afford is to be found doing what he accuses his enemy of, and your mother has built your empire's whole credit on the sentence that seeing is believing. The day one photograph of yours is a lie, every list she ever published becomes a lie with it. You said you would rather not need it. Arrange not to.

*The debt.* Good. A creditor is obeyed longer than a conqueror, and he does not have to garrison anything.

**Your timetable, as I read it.** The line in the summer of 1918. The Urals in the autumn. In the winter the regime, having lost Siberia's grain and your Office having published its decrees, will do what revolutions do when they are frightened: it will begin to kill in categories, and publish the categories, and that will recruit for you better than any proclamation. By 1919 it will have an army, because you invaded; that is the price and there is no not paying it. Your Russian takes Moscow in the second winter or never, and the government that runs from Moscow runs north-east, past Perm, where your three divisions are standing on the road. Then you will find that the capital was never the object. The men were.

I have given you the plan I did not have. Whether you will use it I cannot tell from here; no one ever took my advice who was not under my command, and you are not. But you asked, which Alexander never did.

**N.**

*P.S. — Minus forty at Irkutsk. My Guard died at minus twenty on the road from Smolensk, and they were the best infantry in Europe. I repeat: Siberians.*
