## Author direction (31 October 2026)

> For the next CR I would like to add some historical depth and texture to the Omsk section. I think the character of Mikhailov is worth featuring in the alternate timeline… If he was as smart as people were saying and wanted power, it makes sense to me that in the alternate timeline he would sense where the wind was blowing and join forces with Pacifica, at least on a surface level. This could even make him more dangerous. I also like the character of his mistress and want her on page.

> Mikhailov requires a well-matched counterpart from Pacifica. Someone who sees the game he is playing and can talk to him eye to eye. A meeting between him and Julian would make a good scene.

> I like her agency but this story is too complicated and would detain the central plot too much. I think it's neater if the prophecy holds (while itself remaining off page). The Russian revenge against Mikhailov can come from one of the many other officers he betrayed. Maria can follow her dream of going to Japan after rejecting Mikhailov. Perhaps she can play a more interesting role there.

> Volkov, Maria as hostess and Japanese informant. If she marries in Japan, it must be consequential and matter in some way to the plot.

## The shape in one paragraph

Mikhailov is the man who sees first that the money in the east is Pacifica's. He meets his match in the Reserve Bank's paymaster at Omsk, loses the gold to him in August, owes him his freedom in September, and on the evening of 17 November sells him the coup he organised. Julian lets the coup begin so that it fails in public. Mikhailov's price was his ministry; what he gets is Harbin, where he is London's man on the surface and Pacifica's underneath. In 1921 he tells Julian to his face what the Tomsk vote has done to the 1918 promise. In June 1927 Kashevarov shows Churchill Mikhailov's copy of the British promise of credit, the one paper that proves the Petrograd account British (CR-046), and London reads in it who gave Pacifica the Omsk papers. In January 1928 Volkov, the officer whose papers were really Mikhailov's gift, learns the truth from the British and kills him. Maria refuses Mikhailov, goes to Japan, keeps a Tokyo salon where Kolchak, the British naval attaché and the fleet faction drink, and reports to the Navy General Staff. She marries a fleet-faction officer, and in May 1932 her warning is how Kiyoshi's son knows which ships to hold. She does to her husband what Mikhailov did to Volkov, and loses her place by it instead of buying one.

### Why these choices

**Why Kashevarov is a banker, not a spy.** Canon already wins Omsk by the railway and the paymaster (CR-041, CR-044). A counterpart from the Office of Public Measures would turn the night into an intelligence story, and the book has decided it is a story about who pays. Mikhailov was a finance minister who printed money and bought grain with it. The only man who can talk to him eye to eye is the one who holds the gold he could not touch.

**Why the sale does not cheapen CR-044.** The railway still wins the night: every sentence of the approved and CR-044 text stands. The sale explains why the railway was ready, and it puts a cost on Pacifica's side. Julian let the Directory's ministers be arrested, and some were marched toward a grove, so that the coup would fail in public. The book can keep that from the reader until Volkov learns it in 1927.

**Why Volkov.** He made both the September and the November arrests (*hist.*), and both times swore he had no accomplices. Mikhailov is the accomplice he protected twice. When he swears it the second time, at his own court, he believes it. That is the betrayal that justifies a killing ten years later without any further plot.

**Why the marriage matters.** The author asked that a Japanese marriage, if there is one, be consequential. It is consequential in three ways. (1) It puts a Russian woman inside the fleet faction's homes, which is where §36's coup is planned. (2) It answers how Kiyoshi's son "holds its Japanese units loyal" in §36, without taking the act from him: he still has to hold them, and she only tells him where. (3) It closes Mikhailov's arc in a different key. The woman who refused him does what he did at Omsk, sells a coup to the other side, but she does it to keep a settlement alive rather than to keep a ministry, and she loses her husband and her country by it. The prophecy's "another who will love me and give me everything" is Kashiwagi, and she betrays him. None of this needs a viewpoint chapter; at most two scenes (the refusal at Harbin; one evening in the Tokyo drawing room).

**What the history gives and what it does not.** Historically Maria became Mikhailov's wife at Harbin in 1921, bore his son Georgy on 25 October 1921, lived as "a housewife" ("I married; I live with my husband"), and left for San Francisco with her son in 1945; Mikhailov was deported to the USSR and shot in 1946 (Petin 2019). Canon diverges at the refusal, which is historical, by letting it stand. Her Japan is invented, but it was her stated destination in 1919. Her son's education at Waseda is historical, and his San Francisco death in 1997.
