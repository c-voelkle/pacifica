## The thing itself

**The Petrograd account** is a cover account at a Petrograd bank, kept by the British secret service's station in Russia under Samuel Hoare (*hist.*: Hoare headed British intelligence in Russia in 1916–17). Its entries, read together with the Cheka's own British file, show four payments:

1. **December 1916: "the December affair".** A payment against Rasputin's murder in the Yusupov palace on the night of 16–17 December 1916 (Old Style). *Hist.:* the British-agent version (Oswald Rayner of Hoare's station, Andrew Cook 2005) is a claim, disputed for lack of any trace in the service's files (Douglas Smith 2016). Canon makes the payment fact. Who fired stays OPEN.
2. **Winter 1916–17: the palace coup.** Payments to the men around Guchkov who meant to force Nicholas II to abdicate in March 1917 (*hist.*: the Guchkov–Krymov plot is real; British money in it is invented). February overtook them.
3. **November 1918: Omsk.** The British mission's written promise of credit to whatever government sat in Omsk on 19 November is drawn on the same account. Two copies were made: the mission's and the finance minister's. Mikhailov sold his copy to Kashevarov on 17 November (CR-045 M4). The officer who initials it is Captain Leo Steveni (*hist.*: with the British military mission in Petrograd from 1915; Knox's liaison officer at the White Army's Omsk headquarters, November 1918 to November 1919). He carries the account east.
4. **February 1920: Copenhagen.** On 12 February 1920 James O'Grady and Maxim Litvinov sign the Anglo-Soviet agreement for the exchange of prisoners at Copenhagen (*hist.*). Through that channel, in this history, London pays gold for its prisoners and for the Cheka's British file to be burned. Dzerzhinsky takes the gold, lets the British go and keeps the file.

**Britain's reasons, one per payment.** Every payment has a cold reason of state, and canon records it beside the entry, so that Britain stays a competent power and not a cartoon:
- 1 and 2: the fear of a separate peace. The Empress, Rasputin's circle and the court party were believed in London to be steering Russia toward Germany, and Russia leaving the war in 1917 would have freed a million German soldiers for the West.
- 3: Kolchak. London wanted an admiral who had offered himself to the Admiralty in 1917 (*hist.*), not a Directory of Socialist-Revolutionaries under a Pacifican paymaster.
- 4: its own people and its own name. Lloyd George's government wanted out of Russia and was already dealing with Moscow (*hist.*). Paying to bury 1916–17 is what governments do.

**What the Copenhagen gold did, without anyone intending it.** It paid Dzerzhinsky's Chekists on Kronstadt in their last weeks. Britain's prisoners came home through Copenhagen while the coalition's hostages, the old surveyor Vance among them, stayed on the hostage deck of the *Petropavlovsk*, and Kiyoshi went aboard for them. Nobody in London aimed this at Japan, but it is the most dangerous line in the file. It stays with Julian, Kashevarov and the Chronicler and is never told to Hirohito, as the Altai secret is not (§32). Kiyoshi's son never learns it.

### Why this holds together

- **It answers "why would Britain care?"** Opened, the account would cost Britain far more than Baku. Federal Russia has buried the Romanovs in the Peter and Paul Cathedral, keeps the Dowager Empress and Kirill as the monarchists' figureheads, and has Kolchak, Britain's friend, as its navy minister. Told that Britain paid for the Tsar's fall, it would end Britain's position in Russia, the Entente guarantee that Baku rests on included. The Crown would see the King's cousin paid off his throne and then refused asylum. Downing Street, breaking with Moscow over subversion in 1927 (*hist.*), would have a cabinet minister whose station subverted an allied monarchy. Churchill gives up the review because the oil is the first thing he would lose if the account came out.
- **It answers "how does Churchill know at once?"** The pencilled ledger reference next to a British paper he recognises.
- **It gives the file a body count before Omsk does.** Stalin in 1921, Mikhailov in 1928, both by other men's grudges. The pattern is British method, and the file itself shows Britain paid for a killing before, in December 1916.
- **It keeps Britain's dignity** (planning note §8). Britain loses something real (the review), keeps its dignity (nothing is published, and it settles its own accounts in its own way), and leaves Pacifica holding a cost it did not have (Mikhailov and Manchuria).
- **It ties three existing threads to one cause.** Victoria's silence at the burial (CR-044 B4), Stalin's flight to Georgia (CR-044 B6) and the 1927 collection (CR-041 N14) were three separate lines. Now they are one story.
