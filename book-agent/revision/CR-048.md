## History

- **The five states in 1900** (*hist.*, US census; the alternate figures differ, see R1):

| State | Population | Black | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| South Carolina | 1,340,316 | 782,321 | 58.4% |
| Georgia | 2,216,331 | 1,034,813 | 46.7% |
| Alabama | 1,828,697 | 827,307 | 45.2% |
| Mississippi | 1,551,270 | 907,630 | 58.5% |
| Florida | 528,542 | 230,730 | 43.7% |
| **Total** | **7,465,156** | **3,782,801** | **50.7%** |

  The Mississippi Delta, the Alabama Black Belt and the Lowcountry ran far higher, often 70–80%.
- **Cotton.** The boll weevil crossed the Mississippi about 1907, reached Alabama about 1909, Georgia 1915 and South Carolina 1917; cotton prices collapsed in 1920 (*hist.*).
- **Convict leasing.** Alabama leased convicts to the Birmingham coal mines until 1928 (*hist.*).
- **Black leadership.** Booker T. Washington spoke at Atlanta in 1895 and ran Tuskegee until his death in 1915; Carnegie gave Tuskegee $600,000 in 1903; Rosenwald's school programme began in 1912 (*hist.*). W. E. B. Du Bois taught at Atlanta University 1897–1910 and ran the Atlanta Studies, the first sustained statistical studies of Black life; his data charts were shown at the Paris Exposition of 1900; he founded the Niagara Movement at Fort Erie in 1905, helped found the NAACP in 1909 and edited *The Crisis* from 1910 (*hist.*). Franz Boas gave Atlanta University's commencement address in 1906 at Du Bois's invitation (*hist.*).
- **Ernest Everett Just** was born in Charleston in 1883, went north at sixteen, taught at Howard from 1907, won the first Spingarn Medal in 1915 and, blocked from a research post in the United States, worked in Naples and Berlin from 1929 (*hist.*).
- **Violence.** Wilmington (November 1898); Atlanta (September 1906), stoked by Hoke Smith's campaign, where Black residents of Brownsville armed and defended their district; Springfield, Illinois (August 1908), which led to the NAACP; the Klan refounded on Stone Mountain (1915); *The Birth of a Nation* (1915); East St. Louis (1917); Chicago (1919); Tulsa (1921); the Greenville levee camp in the 1927 flood (*hist.*).
- **Universities.** Columbia dismissed Cattell and Dana over the war in 1917 (*hist.*).
- **Dividing a state's debt.** Gran Colombia's debt was split by population in 1834 (New Granada 50%, Venezuela 28.5%, Ecuador 21.5%); the treaties of Saint-Germain (1919) and Lausanne (1923) divided Austrian and Ottoman debt by the revenue each territory had yielded before the war (*hist.*).
- **Readmission.** Congress readmitted Virginia in January 1870 on "fundamental conditions", among them that its constitution never be amended to deprive any citizen or class of citizens of the vote except for crime. Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment (1868) repudiated Confederate debt and any compensation for emancipation. Virginia's convention of 1901–1902 and North Carolina's suffrage amendment of 1900 later disfranchised most Black voters; George Henry White of North Carolina's Second District, the last Black congressman of the period, left in 1901 (*hist.*).
- **Wilmington.** On 10 November 1898 white supremacists overthrew Wilmington's elected biracial government (*hist.*).
- **Harpers Ferry.** John Brown raided the arsenal on 16 October 1859 with money from six Northern backers; Virginia hanged him at Charles Town on 2 December (*hist.*).
- **Parchman.** Mississippi's state penitentiary farm in the Delta, Sunflower County, opened in 1901 and worked its convicts on cotton under armed trusties (*hist.*).
- **Howard University** has received an annual federal appropriation since 1879 (*hist.*).
- **Marcus Garvey.** Born in St. Ann's Bay, Jamaica, in 1887; a timekeeper for United Fruit in Costa Rica and a newspaper editor in Colón in 1910–1911; in London 1912–1914; founded the UNIA in Kingston in 1914 and moved to Harlem in 1916. The *Negro World* from 1918, the Black Star Line from 1919, the convention and Declaration of Rights at Madison Square Garden in August 1920, the Liberia programme of 1920–1924. He met Edward Young Clarke, the Klan's acting Imperial Wizard, in Atlanta in June 1922. Convicted of mail fraud in June 1923, imprisoned in the Atlanta federal penitentiary from 1925, his sentence commuted by Coolidge in November 1927, deported to Jamaica in December 1927 (*hist.*).
- **Garvey and the empires.** British colonies banned the *Negro World*; British and American officials exchanged intelligence on him. The UNIA had more local divisions in the rural South than in the North (Rolinson, *Grassroots Garveyism*, 2007). Bilbo's Greater Liberia bill of 1939 drew Garveyite support. Garvey held up Japan's rise as proof of what non-white peoples could build (*hist.*).
- **The Tribune.** Robert R. McCormick was co-editor of the *Chicago Tribune* from 1914 and its sole editor and publisher from 1925 (*hist.*).
- **Union philanthropy in the South.** Rockefeller Sanitary Commission against hookworm, 1909–1914 (*hist.*).

### R2 — the Protocol of Richmond (1898)

**The debt (MODEL).** Canon fixes three Pacifican sums (Events E19, E41, E46): $25m lent to the 1871–1872 refinancing, $6m taken in the 1888–1889 compensation bonds and $6m of coupons paid under the guarantee in 1893–1895. The totals below are estimates consistent with them.

| Class | What it is | Face (MODEL) | Rule | Rump | Gulf | Virginia and North Carolina |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| State debts | Each state's own bonds, including Texas's and the Upper South's own abolition bonds of 1884–1885 | — | Stay with the state | its own | its own | their own |
| A. Association debt | The war debts refinanced on customs security in 1871–1872 | $120m | Follows the security: share of customs and revenue collected 1894–1896 (Saint-Germain, Lausanne) | 35% · $42m | 45% · $54m | 20% · $24m |
| B. Compensation bonds | Paid to owners at final abolition, 1888–1889 | $260m | Follows the compensation: where the owners paid lived (SC, GA, AL, MS, FL, LA) | 85% · $221m | 15% · $39m (Louisiana) | 0 |
| C. Guarantee advances | Coupons Pacifica paid in 1893–1895, with interest | $7m | As class B | 85% · $6m | 15% · $1m | 0 |
| **Total** | | **$387m** | | **$269m** | **$94m** | **$24m** |
| Per person (1900) | | | | **$38** | **$15** | **$6** |

Class A's shares are MODEL readings of where the customs were collected (New Orleans and Galveston, then Charleston, Savannah and Mobile, then Norfolk and Wilmington). Class B's follow the 1880 Black population of the six states, a proxy for the enslaved in 1888: Louisiana held 15% of it.

**How each party settles.**
- **Virginia and North Carolina** pay $24m in cash on leaving, raised by state bonds in New York; the Union's own Treasury pays nothing (R9). Pacifica's customs officers leave Norfolk and Wilmington when the money is paid.
- **The Gulf** converts $94m at its founding in 1900 into a Gulf Republic 4% loan on oil royalties and its own customs, placed through San Francisco; Pacifica's officers leave Galveston and New Orleans. This is what lets the Gulf keep its own statistics and fiscal sovereignty, and it fits the Gulf compact's limit on debt held by Union creditors.
- **The rump** keeps $269m, about forty percent of its output in 1900, under continued customs co-administration and Pacifica's guarantee. Service at 3.5–4% with a sinking fund takes about $12–13m a year, most of its central revenue. The coupons are paid; the schools are not built.

**Why it fits.** Pacifica does not write a punitive rule; it insists on the textbook ones. Their result lands on the Deep South because that is where the compensation went. The planters' own bonds bind the rump, and Pacifica need do nothing but administer the customs correctly.

> **[PROPOSED — CR-048]** The rump that remains governs from Montgomery. Just under half its seven million are Black, far more in the Delta, the Black Belt and the Lowcountry, and the share falls every decade as people take the road north. The Protocol of Richmond of 1898 divides the Confederate debt by rules no one can call unjust: the war debt follows the customs that secured it, the compensation bonds follow the owners who were paid. Virginia and North Carolina pay their small share in cash and leave; the Gulf turns its share into a loan on oil and sends Pacifica's customs officers home. The rump keeps more than two-thirds of the whole, about forty percent of its output. Pacifican customs officers still sit in Charleston, Savannah, Jacksonville and Mobile, and the coupons are paid before the schools. Pacifica offers Montgomery the Gulf compact in 1900, unchanged. Montgomery never signs, because the sixth test would oblige it to register, school, examine and keep alive the people it rules, and the offer is reprinted every year. Cotton carries the state until the weevil crosses the river in 1907; after it, the modern places in the rump are foreign: Pacifica's Birmingham works and Mobile station, Union money in Florida. Convicts are still leased to the mines, but not to Pacifica's, which pay the Realm's wage-board rates and hire more Black miners than any employer in the South.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-048]** Pacifica reads its 1871 guarantee narrowly: it protects the Confederate States from foreign invasion, not from their own people. It refuses the planters' calls for help, sells arms to no one and offers a mediation that neither side accepts. The narrow reading keeps Union regulars out, so the trouble can never be ended by war. Pacifica's part is a thin layer of money and a loud honesty. From 1901 the statistics bureau funds Du Bois's Atlanta Studies on the bureau's counting rules; the Office of Public Measures buys pictures from Black photographers at its standard rates; Carquinez offers fellowships to Black scholars the Union will not appoint. Every payment is in the Office's published accounts. Each year the Office prints the Montgomery table, the six measures for the rump beside the Gulf's and, on the same yardstick, the Black districts of Union cities. The charter forbids a false figure. Where the camera points is a choice.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-048]** The revolution never comes, and it never needs to. In Atlanta in 1906, Black families in Brownsville arm against a mob and a pogrom nearly becomes a rising; on 16 October 1910, the anniversary of Harpers Ferry, Josiah Haynes, a Delta-born veteran of the Union's Black cavalry, crosses from Memphis with forty men, opens the camps at the Parchman prison farm and calls the Delta to rise. It does not rise. The militia and the posses take or kill his men within four days, the reprisals fall on families who never heard of him, and Mississippi hangs him in December while half the North calls him a martyr. The emergency becomes chronic. The movement it feeds does not stop at the border. Virginia and North Carolina have come back into the Union on fundamental conditions: every resident a citizen, no one's vote taken away except for crime, no Confederate debt for the Union to pay. Their white machines accept the conditions on paper and write poll taxes and tests equal on their face, and over a million Black citizens and the Union Congress argue about them for a generation, and Washington must answer Springfield in 1908, East St. Louis in 1917 and Chicago in 1919, segregate its service under Wilson and half undo it, alternately appeasing and striking at an assertive Black minority it can no longer ignore. Each concession raises expectations and each backlash radicalises the next demand.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-048]** The cost falls on Roosevelt's programme. Garrisons and refugee relief draw on the money meant for laboratories, and when letters on Haynes's men name the Northern professors who paid for his raid, Congress passes the Parchman Act of 1911, attaching loyalty and neutrality oaths to the programme's grants, so the instrument built to keep talent at home helps send it abroad. A celebrated dismissal becomes the case every European scholar reads. Ernest Everett Just, born in Charleston and refused a research post in the Union, signs the Parchman oath at Howard, and after Chicago's riot takes a Carquinez fellowship in 1920 to survey Kāneʻohe Bay; he is on the reef when the Crown Prince dives it. Union theorists, most of them revanchists and loudest in McCormick's *Chicago Tribune*, say Pacifica made the whole crisis, and cite the Atlanta grant, the raid backer who once held a Carquinez fellowship, the Office's purchases, Carnegie's Tuskegee gift and *The Crisis* carried as freight on Pacifican lines. All of it is public and lawful; none of it proves design. Pacifica's attention is on London.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-048]** After 1916 the movement's mass belongs to Marcus Garvey. His divisions spread through the Delta and the Black Belt, and the generation that watched Parchman fail joins him instead of raiding: pride, uniforms and the road out take the place of a rising. Montgomery tolerates a man who preaches separation, and in Atlanta in 1922 he meets the Klan's Edward Young Clarke. Emigration becomes the one programme the rump's populists and the Black nationalists share, and the rump cannot pay for it; its revenue is pledged to the coupons. Garvey makes the Pacific order his proof of what non-white peoples can build, a Hawaiian queen at its origin and a brown crown at its head, though in Colón in 1910 he had seen West Indians paid well on Pacifica's canal and offered no road to its citizenship. The *Tribune* calls his praise Pacifica's payroll. Pacifica answers by refusing him in public. Its yards sell the Black Star Line no ships, its finances having failed the published credit test, and Honolulu declines his lecture. Garvey is a British subject whom London hates, and Julian will not let London think Pacifica plays the race card against its empire in the years before the Concord. In 1927 the Union deports Garvey to Jamaica in concert with the British, and Thalassia, which watches the two services work together for the first time, refuses him too.
