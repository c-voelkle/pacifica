## What this revision changes

- **Tone.** The review found the memorandum and the institutional chronology written like a constitutional brief: “lawful funding arrangements”, “appropriate existing authorities”, “no new confederal education power is presumed”. That register belonged to the drafter's caution, not to Elias. The memorandum (N2) and the narrative of the approved groups are rewritten as what happens, in Elias's voice where he speaks. No new power or office is asserted: the money is the mining royalty and the rules live in the school's contracts, as canon already has it (CR-034, CR-035).
- **Cast.** Five of the seven invented people are cut. Two remain because the approved plot needs them: **Liang Wenqi**, who carries the computing branch (the Boole insight, the relay machines, the 1867 failure) and the first Japanese fellowship; and **Mele Kealoha**, who catches that failure and is the Hawaiian participant in the civic language of *mana*. Their roles are tied to existing canon rather than left as islands. The records clerk, the instrument maker, the experimental master, the dissenting mathematician and the fission chemist are removed. Their institutional work passes to the Calculation Office, its workshop and historical people; their atomic roles return with S1/S2.
- **Anchors.** C1 now rests on Babbage's mechanical notation (1826), the Analytical Engine as described by Menabrea and annotated by Lovelace (1842–43), Boole's logic (1847, 1854), the Scheutz engines (1853–59) and, as the measure of the lead, Jevons (1869–70), Peirce (1886) and Shannon (1937).

## Author direction

- Elias is a thoughtful, perceptive lawgiver in the Lycurgus/Numa role. His nomos establishes the laws, arrangements and institutions through which the scientific community develops.
- Agon between rival geniuses, schools competing for talent, and counties competing in accomplishment.
- Gifted youth meet nature and real danger abroad, including mountains, islands and Japan, and gain judgment and glory through expeditions. Exceptional achievement may carry more distinction than examination rank.
- The demanding pursuit of unknown truths belongs chiefly to the exceptionally gifted scientific and governing leadership. The wider population honours ability and reliable work: “Things work in this country.”
- *Mana* as the sacred dimension, with a specifically Pacifican civic development shaped by Hawaiian participants, without equating the whole historical tradition with technical merit.
- Institutions, computing and discoveries develop together; historical discovery dates are comparisons, not physical deadlines.
- As few invented scientists and mathematicians as possible: one or two, only where the plot needs them (2 November 2026).

## The nomos

Elias wants a productive sovereign society that can enlarge its own capacities and outlive him. His own improbable education taught him that ability is everywhere and opportunity almost nowhere; Plutarch taught him that a lawgiver works through habits, not decrees; the pastor's house, dispossession, Mexico and the bilingual republic taught him how much knowledge sits scattered among people who cannot use it. *On the Public Arts* is where he works out what to do about it. He has no foresight of what the school will find.

The memorandum's central passage:

> A people is as strong as the abilities it raises. Find ability early, train it hard, give it work and judge it by its works. Whoever holds an office holds it for its work, and no one may make him give a false account of that work: not a patron, not a minister, not I. A falsified account robs everyone who must build on it, and I will treat it as theft from the commonwealth. What we build must let those who come after us see further than we do, and beat us.

And on contest:

> Give ability its trial and achievement its honour. Let the schools fight for able minds and the counties for the excellence of their works. Whoever wants the greatest trusts must first prove himself in study, in rivalry, and in undertakings where his judgment answers with his life. The commonwealth pays for these trials and remembers those who win them, and those who fall.

Its working rules are few. Ability gets visible routes to recognition. A scientist's living does not depend on reaching the convenient conclusion. Accounts are kept so they can be corrected. Criticism has a home outside the patron's house. Masters must teach. Institutions may be improved by those who run them. Contests carry real consequences. Gifted young people are sent where instructions run out.

Elias pays for it from the mining royalty and writes its rules into the school's contracts (CR-034, CR-035). The memorandum is never published as a law; it is the brief Genth, Zeuner and Sylvester are hired on.

## The ethos and its practices

The public character is proud and buoyant: pleasure in mastery, practical confidence, fidelity, gratitude to teachers and companions, emulation, and a discriminating respect for ability. Most people's honour rests on dependable work, and that is enough: things work in this country. Outstanding ability can inspire something close to reverence, and a school gains weight through generations of achievement and remembered obligations.

Scientific life adds a hunger for difficult discovery and for recognition from judges who can tell. Its manners are direct, teasing and competitive. “Show us” is an invitation, not an insult. Eminence carries obligations: magnanimity, fair acknowledgement, teaching successors. People act confidently on limited knowledge and say plainly what is still unresolved. Jealousy, concealment and stolen credit happen, and they cost.

Hawaiian participants give this a sacred register. Achievement and remembered obligation acquire *mana* for some people: the master's tools, the returning fellow, the works that stand. It is a new civic usage, contested by those who hold *mana* to genealogy and the gods, and it never becomes a compulsory theology or a measure of the whole tradition. Its strongest public form comes after 1887, when Honolulu calls the Five *nā Kahuna*, the priests and masters of their crafts (CR-027).

| Custom | Occasion | What it cultivates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An apprentice's first acknowledged work | Experienced makers test and argue over a first accomplished piece; acceptance is marked in the workshop's book and with a meal that names the teacher. Examinations stay separate. | Mastery, accountability, gratitude. |
| Public proving of a work | After the engineers' tests, residents see the bridge, waterworks or harbour installation used; the people responsible explain how it performs and the builders are named. | Confidence grounded in visible accomplishment. |
| County showing | Annual displays, practical contests and awards, grown out of fairs and regattas, judged from outside the county and later held in the county halls. | Local loyalty, emulation, pride in results. |
| Fellowship departure and return | The fellow states the undertaking before the people who pay for it; on return, records, results, accounts and testimony come before any honour. Failed ventures and lost companions are remembered by name. | Daring, judgment, fidelity and earned glory. |
| Challenge evening | A researcher sets out what is established, what is conjecture and what would settle it; an appointed respondent and the room attack it, including the people who built the apparatus and ran the calculation. | Intellectual courage, answerability, fellowship. |
| Contest with declared terms | Questions and criteria are published before proofs, predictions or demonstrations; outside judges award distinction and, where agreed, money. | Agon, fair dealing, serious rivalry. |
| Corrections in the record | Journals keep the original claim, the correction and the name of whoever found the difficulty; the important cases are taught. | Candour, and a confidence that survives revision. |
| A master's welcome and leave-taking | Inaugural lectures and feasts name teachers and collaborators; leave-takings name successors and unfinished questions, and the master's instruments and notebooks pass on. | Magnanimity, transmission, institutional continuity. |

These customs take hold through particular events, unevenly and with disagreement, as the chronology shows.

## Chronology of the approved groups

### 1850–1851: what the gold should leave behind

Genth accepts the Republic's offer in 1851: a laboratory, assistants, an independent salary, the right to publish and time for his own chemistry (canon). Elias begins *On the Public Arts* the same year and works on it until 1854. Genth's contract is the memorandum's first clause put into practice. The first computing institution is people, not machines: human computers, commercial arithmetic machines and checked tables meet the assay office's and the surveyors' needs, and the school keeps a register of readings, sources and corrections from the start.

### 1852–1854: the first precedents

Genth's laboratory becomes the Carquinez school in 1852 on the mining ordinances and the royalty; in 1853 Genth exposes the Superintendency mill's losses and Elias publishes the report (canon). Elias makes the report a precedent on purpose: an honest, unwelcome conclusion will always have a home. Zeuner arrives in 1854, and his testing floor makes the difference between a convincing account and dependable performance something anyone can watch. The workshop marks its first accepted pieces, and the custom of the first acknowledged work begins.

Elias wants young people sent out where judgment is tested. The school's royalty pays for the first travelling fellowships from 1854. An exceptional expedition can later count for more than examination rank; the qualifying examinations still govern the professions and services they cover.

### 1855–1858: calculation, rivalry and journeys

Sylvester arrives in 1855 and proposes the **Calculation Office** the same year. Ten years of actuarial work have shown him what a calculating staff can do: it serves navigation, surveying, insurance and engineering, and the workload is real and permanent. He brings London's mathematics with him, including Menabrea's account of Babbage's Analytical Engine and Lovelace's notes on it (1842–1843), and Babbage's mechanical notation (1826). Liang joins the Office's mathematical staff and reads Boole's *Laws of Thought* (1854).

In **1856** Carquinez commissions a Scheutz-type printing calculator. Georg and Edvard Scheutz had finished their second engine in 1853; it went to the Dudley Observatory at Albany in 1857, and the British government ordered a copy from Bryan Donkin for the General Register Office's life tables, delivered in 1859. Pacifica's commission makes it the third customer, racing London for the same kind of machine. Installation and adaptation take **1858–1860**; the engine computes particular tables well and nothing else, which is the Office's lesson about machines.

In **1856–1857** Liang travels with the Japanese partnership to compare measures and calculation practice in the cooperating workshops (O4 governs the journey's hazards). His Japanese hosts judge the exchange and gain from it. On his return he accounts for his work to those who paid for it before he is honoured, and returning fellows become the school's admired examples. In 1858 the school sets its first gold prize questions (canon), and proofs, experiments and expeditions become different routes to distinction. The schools begin to fight for the people who win them.

### 1859–1861: the laboratory and the challenge evening

From 1859 women may sit the state examinations (canon), the first result of Victoria's campaign. Genth founds the **Laboratory of Light, Electricity and Matter** in 1859, inside the school: photographic recording, mineral analysis and electrical measurement give it practical work and instruments. Mele joins its recording staff. The Calculation Office and the school's register make its comparisons cumulative.

The **challenge evening** begins here as a laboratory habit: a new claim is stated, an appointed respondent questions it, and the room names what would settle the dispute. A conjecture earns a trial before it earns belief.

### 1862–1865: the war tests the founder's priorities

Most of the school's capacity goes to surveying, telegraphs, machinery and the navy. Elias keeps the laboratories and the Calculation Office running regardless: he counts the ability to work out unfamiliar problems as a strategic asset and pays for it from the royalty while the war lasts. Genth finds indium in 1863 (canon).

In **1863** Liang and the Office's workshop demonstrate a relay arithmetic apparatus. The idea is Liang's: telegraph relays wired in series and in parallel behave as Boole's “and” and “or”, so a circuit can be designed on paper as algebra before it is built. Peirce makes the same observation in a letter in 1886 and Shannon proves it in 1937; in Pacifica it is a working engineer's method from the 1860s. The demonstrator is slow and temperamental, and years of work separate it from useful service.

### 1866–1872: rival schools, and a method that fails

Yerba Buena College is founded in 1866 and takes Sylvester and Cohn (canon), the first great contest for talent. **[PROPOSED]** Both colleges claim the Calculation Office; it becomes a shared service of the League with its machines at Carquinez, where Liang stays.

In **1867** Mele finds that a checking rule Liang devised has been discarding recurring observations as instrument faults. The register has kept everything, so the discarded readings can be reconstructed, and they are real. Liang has to acknowledge in public that the procedure that made his name was wrong. Genth puts the next computing money into independent checking and delays the machines' extension. Mele is credited only as an assistant, and the dispute over her credit is not settled. The colleges agree terms for recording and publishing omitted observations and contrary results: the tools for organising knowledge are criticised too. A later feast or correction entry does not erase what it cost.

Relay arithmetic enters service in **1868–1870**, and limited preset sequences follow by **1872**. Procedures pile up and become teachable, and the staff learn not to mistake a calculation that runs for an account that is true. Zeuner's return to Freiberg in 1871 becomes the model leave-taking: Pacifica loses his daily presence and keeps a European connection.

### 1873–1879: the programmed machine

Carquinez names californium in 1873; Michelson begins his precision work in 1878 (canon). A programmed electromechanical calculator enters service in **1876–1879**. Its sequences are written in a common procedural notation developed in **1878–1882** from two London sources, Babbage's mechanical notation and the operation tables of Lovelace's notes. Repeated calculations become cheap and checkable, and the Office's workshop gains prestige because dependable operation is a recognised achievement. The machine is an engineering divergence built on Liang's Boolean method, not something that follows automatically from the existence of relays. In London, Jevons is demonstrating a mechanical “logic piano” (1869–1870); the contest with Europe is now in the open.

Public provings, visiting scholars, contests and apprentice acknowledgements make skill visible. Fellows go out to Japan, Hawaiʻi, the mountains and the islands (O4), and come back to shape the customs rather than merely undergo them. In Victoria's educational circle and Kalākaua's cultural programme, Hawaiian participants, Mele among them, develop the civic language of *mana*; its relation to ancestry and to other religious meanings stays alive and argued over.

Elias's Hawaiian breach of 1874–1876 stands. A founder committed to trustworthy institutions can also treat political limits as made for other people, and the ethos does not resolve that conflict.

### 1880–1890: the founder dies

The Development Fund begins in 1880; Tesla arrives in 1884 (canon). The Calculation Office standardises its comparisons, keeps discrepancies and checks independently; school welcomes and professional feasts name teachers, assistants and makers, and the disputes over credit go on.

Elias dies in 1887; Victoria becomes Regent and remains Queen (canon). Memorials give his founding decisions emotional weight, and Honolulu's name for the Five, *nā Kahuna*, gives the masters of the crafts a sacred one. Hollerith's coded-record tabulation joins the Office from 1888 (CR-027, PROPOSED) and serves Noe's registers, public returns and scientific administration; numerical computing, record sorting and interpretation remain distinct jobs. New travelling awards respect the service bonds attached to Realm scholarships from 1888.

### 1891–1900: things work in this country

In **1897** Fresno's hall rings its bell for a week when the county reaches all four standards (canon). County halls, independently checked returns and local celebrations make competence a source of collective pride. Families honour schools, physicians, reliable works and successful fellows without knowing any of the science behind them. By 1900 the founders' circle has become several generations; Liang is past sixty-five and Mele past sixty, living memory of the careful account, the worthy opponent, the difficult journey and the person who makes the work dependable.

## Returned for rework: the atomic branch (S1, S2, G1)

The proposal dated radioactivity 1861, atomic transformation 1870, the electron 1876, a compact nucleus 1882, isotopes 1883–1886, the neutron 1888 (confirmed by 1890), fission 1892, chain-reaction feasibility 1893–1894, an oversight settlement in 1894, a commitment in 1895 and a civilian research reactor in 1898. Niépce de Saint-Victor's photographic reports of 1857–1861 remain the seed. The review found that only that first step had a mechanism. Before these dates come back to the author, the next draft must supply:

1. **A mechanism for each milestone:** what instrument, material or question makes it reachable on that date in this timeline.
2. **Strong radiation sources first.** The nucleus and neutron experiments need intense alpha sources, so the isolation of the radioactive elements has to come before 1882 rather than be deferred.
3. **Discoverers from history or canon.** The invented-cast limit is reached; the master who resists, the dissenter who changes schools and the chemist who reads fission must be carried by historical or canonical people (the Carquinez and Yerba Buena scientists, Morley, Michelson, Tesla, Steinmetz, Soberanes) unless the author decides otherwise. The neutron goes to experimenters, not to a mathematician.
4. **Uranium.** A reactor needs tonnes of it. The Colorado Plateau's carnotite lies west of the divide and is probably Pacifican; resolving the E13 boundary question then becomes part of the plot, with a mining beat in the early 1890s.
5. **Energy.** With relativity unchanged (CR-036 D21), the energy of fission has to be measured, not derived; say how.
6. **Consequences.** Results published since 1861 mean every great power knows by 1894 what fission could do. Sketch what that does to 1900–1932 (CR-048 A6 and the European, Russian and Concord arcs) before the dates are approved, and say what power or prosperity an 1898 reactor brings Pacifica ([success contract](../../docs/success-contract.md)).
7. **The five-seat problem.** If an atomic programme exists, decide whether it claims a seat among the Five and who loses one.
