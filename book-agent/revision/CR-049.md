## What this revision changes

- **Tone.** The review found the memorandum and the institutional chronology written like a constitutional brief: “lawful funding arrangements”, “appropriate existing authorities”, “no new confederal education power is presumed”. That register belonged to the drafter's caution, not to Elias. The memorandum (N2) and the narrative of the approved groups are rewritten as what happens, in Elias's voice where he speaks. No new power or office is asserted: the money is the mining royalty and the rules live in the school's contracts, as canon already has it (CR-034, CR-035).
- **Cast.** Five of the seven invented people are cut. The author set a limit (“one or two if absolutely necessary”), not a choice of people; the drafter proposes keeping two because the approved plot needs them: **Liang Wenqi**, who carries the computing branch (the Boole insight, the relay machines, the 1867 failure) and the first Japanese fellowship; and **Mele Kealoha**, who catches that failure and is the Hawaiian participant in the civic language of *mana*. Their roles are tied to existing canon rather than left as islands. Their names, dates and episodes are PROPOSED. The records clerk, the instrument maker, the experimental master, the dissenting mathematician and the fission chemist are removed. Their institutional work passes to the Calculation Office, its workshop and historical people; their atomic roles return with S1/S2.
- **Anchors.** C1 now rests on Babbage's mechanical notation (1826), the Analytical Engine as described by Menabrea and annotated by Lovelace (1842–43), Boole's logic (1847, 1854), the Scheutz engines (1853–59) and, as the measure of the lead, Jevons (1869–70), Peirce (1886) and Shannon (1937).
- **Scope.** The chronology covers the approved groups. The atomic branch is reworked separately under [The atomic branch, reworked](#the-atomic-branch-reworked-s1-s2--approved).

## Author direction

- Elias is a thoughtful, perceptive lawgiver in the Lycurgus/Numa role. His nomos establishes the laws, arrangements and institutions through which the scientific community develops.
- Agon between rival geniuses, schools competing for talent, and counties competing in accomplishment.
- Gifted youth meet nature and real danger abroad, including mountains, islands and Japan, and gain judgment and glory through expeditions. Exceptional achievement may carry more distinction than examination rank.
- The demanding pursuit of unknown truths belongs chiefly to the exceptionally gifted scientific and governing leadership. The wider population honours ability and reliable work: “Things work in this country.”
- *Mana* as the sacred dimension, with a specifically Pacifican civic development shaped by Hawaiian participants, without equating the whole historical tradition with technical merit.
- Institutions, computing and discoveries develop together; historical discovery dates are comparisons, not physical deadlines.
- As few invented scientists and mathematicians as possible: one or two, only where the plot needs them (2 November 2026).

## The nomos

Elias wants a productive sovereign society that can enlarge its own capacities and outlive him. His own improbable education taught him that ability is everywhere and opportunity almost nowhere; Plutarch taught him that a lawgiver works through habits, not decrees; the pastor's house, dispossession, Mexico and the bilingual republic taught him how much knowledge sits scattered among people who cannot use it. *On the Public Arts* is where he works out what to do about it. He has no foresight of what the school will find.

The memorandum's central passage:

> A people is as strong as the abilities it raises. Find ability early, train it hard, give it work and judge it by its works. Whoever holds an office holds it for its work, and no one may make him give a false account of that work: not a patron, not a minister, not I. A falsified account robs everyone who must build on it, and I will treat it as theft from the commonwealth. What we build must let those who come after us see further than we do, and beat us.

And on contest:

> Give ability its trial and achievement its honour. Let the schools fight for able minds and the counties for the excellence of their works. Whoever wants the greatest trusts must first prove himself in study, in rivalry, and in undertakings where his judgment answers with his life. The commonwealth pays for these trials and remembers those who win them, and those who fall.

Its working rules are few. Ability gets visible routes to recognition. A scientist's living does not depend on reaching the convenient conclusion. Accounts are kept so they can be corrected. Criticism has a home outside the patron's house. Masters must teach. Institutions may be improved by those who run them. Contests carry real consequences. Gifted young people are sent where instructions run out.

Elias pays for it from the mining royalty and writes its rules into the school's contracts (CR-034, CR-035). The memorandum is never published as a law; it is the brief Genth, Zeuner and Sylvester are hired on.

## The ethos and its practices

The public character is proud and buoyant: pleasure in mastery, practical confidence, fidelity, gratitude to teachers and companions, emulation, and a discriminating respect for ability. Most people's honour rests on dependable work, and that is enough: things work in this country. Outstanding ability can inspire something close to reverence, and a school gains weight through generations of achievement and remembered obligations.

Scientific life adds a hunger for difficult discovery and for recognition from judges who can tell. Its manners are direct, teasing and competitive. “Show us” is an invitation, not an insult. Eminence carries obligations: magnanimity, fair acknowledgement, teaching successors. People act confidently on limited knowledge and say plainly what is still unresolved. Jealousy, concealment and stolen credit happen, and they cost.

Hawaiian participants give this a sacred register. Achievement and remembered obligation acquire *mana* for some people: the master's tools, the returning fellow, the works that stand. It is a new civic usage, and those who hold *mana* to genealogy and the gods contest it in the newspapers and from the pulpit. Its strongest public form comes after 1887, when Honolulu calls the Five *nā Kahuna*, the priests and masters of their crafts (CR-027).

| Custom | Occasion | What it cultivates |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An apprentice's first acknowledged work | Experienced makers test and argue over a first accomplished piece; acceptance is marked in the workshop's book and with a meal that names the teacher. Examinations stay separate. | Mastery, accountability, gratitude. |
| Public proving of a work | After the engineers' tests, residents see the bridge, waterworks or harbour installation used; the people responsible explain how it performs and the builders are named. | Confidence grounded in visible accomplishment. |
| County showing | Annual displays, practical contests and awards, grown out of fairs and regattas, judged from outside the county and later held in the county halls. | Local loyalty, emulation, pride in results. |
| Fellowship departure and return | The fellow states the undertaking before the people who pay for it; on return, records, results, accounts and testimony come before any honour. Failed ventures and lost companions are remembered by name. | Daring, judgment, fidelity and earned glory. |
| Challenge evening | A researcher sets out what is established, what is conjecture and what would settle it; an appointed respondent and the room attack it, including the people who built the apparatus and ran the calculation. | Intellectual courage, answerability, fellowship. |
| Contest with declared terms | Questions and criteria are published before proofs, predictions or demonstrations; outside judges award distinction and, where agreed, money. | Agon, fair dealing, serious rivalry. |
| Corrections in the record | Journals keep the original claim, the correction and the name of whoever found the difficulty; the important cases are taught. | Candour, and a confidence that survives revision. |
| A master's welcome and leave-taking | Inaugural lectures and feasts name teachers and collaborators; leave-takings name successors and unfinished questions, and the master's instruments and notebooks pass on. | Magnanimity, transmission, institutional continuity. |

These customs take hold through particular events, unevenly and with disagreement, as the chronology shows.

## Chronology of the approved groups

The institutions, the computing milestones, the customs and the scenes carried by Liang and Mele are approved. Details marked **[PROPOSED]** inline are not.

### 1850–1851: what the gold should leave behind

Genth accepts the Republic's offer in 1851: a laboratory, assistants, an independent salary, the right to publish and time for his own chemistry (canon). Elias begins *On the Public Arts* the same year and works on it until 1854. Genth's contract is the memorandum's first clause put into practice. The first computing institution is people, not machines: human computers, commercial arithmetic machines and checked tables meet the assay office's and the surveyors' needs, and the school keeps a register of readings, sources and corrections from the start.

### 1852–1854: the first precedents

Genth's laboratory becomes the Carquinez school in 1852 on the mining ordinances and the royalty; in 1853 Genth exposes the Superintendency mill's losses and Elias publishes the report (canon). Elias makes the report a precedent on purpose: an honest, unwelcome conclusion will always have a home. Zeuner arrives in 1854, and his testing floor makes the difference between a convincing account and dependable performance something anyone can watch. The workshop marks its first accepted pieces, and the custom of the first acknowledged work begins.

Elias wants young people sent out where judgment is tested. The school's royalty pays for the first travelling fellowships from 1854. A fellow who comes back with something no examination could test outranks the examination's top scorer.

### 1855–1858: calculation, rivalry and journeys

Sylvester arrives in 1855 and proposes the **Calculation Office** the same year. Ten years of actuarial work have shown him what a calculating staff can do: it serves navigation, surveying, insurance and engineering, and the workload is real and permanent. He brings London's mathematics with him, including Menabrea's account of Babbage's Analytical Engine and Lovelace's notes on it (1842–1843), and Babbage's mechanical notation (1826). Liang joins the Office's mathematical staff and reads Boole's *Laws of Thought* (1854).

In 1856 Carquinez commissions a Scheutz-type printing calculator from Stockholm. Georg and Edvard Scheutz finished their second engine in 1853; it will go to the Dudley Observatory at Albany in 1857, and the British government will order a copy from Bryan Donkin for the General Register Office's life tables. Carquinez orders its machine in 1856, a year before Albany and London buy theirs, and the race for the tables is on. Installation and adaptation take **1858–1860**; the engine computes particular tables well and nothing else, which is the Office's lesson about machines.

In **1856–1857** Liang travels with the Japanese partnership to compare measures and calculation practice in the cooperating workshops. His hosts at Nagasaki test him as hard as he tests them, and they keep the better of his methods; he learns how much of a numerical account rests on assumptions no one wrote down. On his return he accounts for his work to those who paid for it before he is honoured, and returning fellows become the school's admired examples. In 1858 the school sets its first gold prize questions (canon), and proofs, experiments and expeditions become different routes to distinction. The schools begin to fight for the people who win them.

### 1859–1861: the laboratory and the challenge evening

From 1859 women may sit the state examinations (canon), the first result of Victoria's campaign. Genth founds the **Laboratory of Light, Electricity and Matter** in 1859, inside the school: photographic recording, mineral analysis and electrical measurement give it practical work and instruments. Mele joins its recording staff. The Calculation Office and the school's register make its comparisons cumulative.

The **challenge evening** begins here as a laboratory habit: a new claim is stated, an appointed respondent questions it, and the room names what would settle the dispute. A conjecture earns a trial before it earns belief.

### 1862–1865: the war tests the founder's priorities

Most of the school's capacity goes to surveying, telegraphs, machinery and the navy. **[PROPOSED]** The navy wants the laboratory's instrument makers and the Office's computers for its gunnery tables. Elias gives it the tables and keeps the people: he counts the ability to work out unfamiliar problems as a strategic asset, and the royalty that pays for it buys one fewer gunboat a year while the war lasts. Genth finds indium in 1863 (canon).

In **1863** Liang and the Office's workshop demonstrate a relay arithmetic apparatus. The idea is Liang's: telegraph relays wired in series and in parallel behave as Boole's “and” and “or”, so a circuit can be designed on paper as algebra before it is built. The demonstrator is slow and temperamental, and it takes years of work to make it useful.

### 1866–1872: rival schools, and a method that fails

Yerba Buena College is founded in 1866 and takes Sylvester and Cohn (canon), the first great contest for talent. **[PROPOSED]** Both colleges claim the Calculation Office; it becomes a shared service of the League with its machines at Carquinez, where Liang stays.

In **1867** Mele finds that a checking rule Liang devised has been discarding recurring observations as instrument faults, her own among them. The register has kept everything, so she reconstructs the discarded readings, and they are real. Liang has to acknowledge in public that the procedure that made his name was wrong. Genth puts the next computing money into independent checking and delays the machines' extension. Mele is credited only as an assistant, and she does not let it go. The colleges agree terms for recording and publishing omitted observations and contrary results, and the case becomes the first that the journals' corrections columns teach.

Relay arithmetic enters service in **1868–1870**, and limited preset sequences follow by **1872**. Procedures pile up and become teachable, and the staff learn not to mistake a calculation that runs for an account that is true. Zeuner's return to Freiberg in 1871 becomes the model leave-taking: Carquinez loses his daily presence and keeps a European connection, and he sends it students.

### 1873–1879: the programmed machine

Carquinez names californium in 1873; Michelson begins his precision work in 1878 (canon). A programmed electromechanical calculator enters service in **1876–1879**. Its sequences are written in a common procedural notation developed in **1878–1882** from two London sources, Babbage's mechanical notation and the operation tables of Lovelace's notes. Repeated calculations become cheap and checkable, and the Office's workshop gains prestige because dependable operation is a recognised achievement. It runs because Liang's circuits were designed on paper first.

Public provings, visiting scholars, contests and apprentice acknowledgements make skill visible. Fellows go out to Japan, Hawaiʻi, the mountains and the islands, and come back to shape the customs rather than merely undergo them. In Victoria's educational circle and Kalākaua's cultural programme, Hawaiian participants, Mele among them, develop the civic language of *mana*, and the argument over its relation to ancestry and the gods goes on.

In 1874–1876 the same Elias who wrote that no one may make an officer falsify his work ignores the kingdom's own judges. A founder committed to trustworthy institutions treats political limits as made for other people, and the ethos he founded has no answer for him.

### 1880–1890: the founder dies

The Development Fund begins in 1880; Tesla arrives in 1884 (canon). The Calculation Office standardises its comparisons, keeps discrepancies and checks independently; school welcomes and professional feasts name teachers, assistants and makers, and the disputes over credit go on.

Elias dies in 1887; Victoria becomes Regent and remains Queen (canon). Memorials give his founding decisions emotional weight, and Honolulu's name for the Five, *nā Kahuna*, gives the masters of the crafts a sacred one. From 1888 Hollerith's punched cards tabulate Noe's registers at the Office of Public Measures (CR-027, PROPOSED). **[PROPOSED]** Liang, past fifty, wants the registers' work for his relays and loses it to the young American's cards; the Calculation Office ends up borrowing the method that beat it. Travelling awards from 1888 carry the Realm scholarships' service bonds.

### 1891–1900: things work in this country

In **1897** Fresno's hall rings its bell for a week when the county reaches all four standards (canon). County halls, independently checked returns and local celebrations make competence a source of collective pride. Families honour schools, physicians, reliable works and successful fellows without knowing any of the science behind them. By 1900 the founders' circle has become several generations; Liang is past sixty-five and Mele past sixty, living memory of the careful account, the worthy opponent, the difficult journey and the person who makes the work dependable.

### Who discovers it (requirement 3)

Canon supplies Genth (Carquinez), Michelson and Morley (Yerba Buena), Sylvester and Tesla; the approved cast supplies Liang and Mele. Three historical people are added, **PROPOSED**:

- **William Francis Hillebrand** (born Honolulu, 12 December 1853; *soil*: Pacifican by birth). Historically he studied at Cornell and Heidelberg (doctorate 1875) and worked with Bunsen, opened an assay office at Leadville in 1879, ran the Geological Survey's Denver laboratory from 1880, and found a gas in uraninite that he took for nitrogen and Ramsay later showed to contain helium. In this timeline he goes from Oahu College to Carquinez and to Bunsen in Heidelberg, comes back in 1878, and because Leadville is Union ground he assays the Western Slope for the Republic. He is the analytical chemist of the branch, and its furnace chemist in the 1890s.
- **Eugen Goldstein** (born Gleiwitz, 1850, to a Jewish family; *pull*). Helmholtz's student, who named cathode rays in 1876 and found canal rays in 1886, spent his career at the Berlin and Potsdam observatories. Carquinez offers him, at twenty-six and before his doctorate, an assistant's post under Genth with citizenship (CR-036's offer) in 1876. He takes his doctorate in 1881 and becomes master of the Laboratory of Light, Electricity and Matter that year; he is the author of the nucleus and the man who resists the neutron.
- **Nagaoka Hantarō** (born 1865; *pull*). Historically he graduated in physics at Tokyo in 1887, studied in Berlin, Munich and Vienna from 1893 and proposed his Saturnian atom in 1904. In this timeline the Japanese partnership sends him to Carquinez in 1887. He is the young experimenter who changes schools.

The first proposal's dramatic roles survive with real people. Goldstein is the master who resists and pays for it. Nagaoka is the dissenter another school takes in. Hillebrand is the chemist who reads fission.

### Chronology and mechanisms

**1859–1864: uranium's activity (S1).** Niépce de Saint-Victor's reports that uranium salts darken photographic plates in the dark reach Carquinez through the *Comptes rendus*. The laboratory's mineral cabinet holds Joachimsthal pitchblende and the uranium yellows Patera's factory has made there since 1853. Mele's plates, stored beside them, fog in the dark. The laboratory tests the effect against darkness, black paper, months of storage and every uranium compound it has, and adds a gold-leaf electroscope, which discharges near uranium at a rate that follows the uranium content. *Mechanism:* plates, the electroscope and Genth's analytical chemistry. In 1861 Genth publishes that the activity belongs to uranium itself, with Mele named as observer. By 1864 thorium is found active too, Liang has fitted the electroscope readings, and pitchblende proves several times more active than its uranium can explain. Something else is in the ore.

**1864–1869: hunting the active substances (requirement 2).** Carquinez already finds elements with the spectroscope (indium 1863). It buys the Joachimsthal factory's residues, which nobody else wants, and separates them fraction by fraction, assaying each with the electroscope instead of the balance. In **1868** an active substance follows bismuth through every separation: a new element. In **1869** a far more active one follows barium and shows new lines in Genth's spectroscope. These are the elements history calls polonium and radium. Their Pacifican names are OPEN; californium is the precedent for a name that sticks (R3).

**1867–1872: transformation.** **[PROPOSED]** The recurring readings Liang's rule discarded in 1867 were electroscopes near thorium preparations, drifting with the laboratory's draughts because thorium gives off an active gas. (The approved 1867 episode does not say what the readings were; this identifies them.) *Mechanism:* the gas is chemically inert; its activity dies away at a fixed rate and leaves activity on whatever it touches; and a thorium product separated chemically loses its activity while the thorium regains it, both at fixed rates that Liang's curves describe exactly. In **1870** Genth and Liang conclude that one element is turning into another. The paper names them and not Mele, whose readings found the gas, and her grievance of 1867 becomes a quarrel the whole League hears. Morley confirms the result independently at Yerba Buena in **1872**, and that year the journal's corrections column adds her name to the discovery: the first time the custom gives credit back.

**1872–1877: strong sources and their heat (requirements 2 and 5).** Pacifica turns industrial chemistry on the residues. The school and the Superintendency's mill work tonnes a year; historically the Curies needed about a tonne for a tenth of a gram. By **1876** Carquinez holds weighable salts of the barium-borne element and short-lived preparations of the bismuth-borne one, the first intense sources anywhere. In **1877** Morley's calorimeter shows the barium-borne salt keeping itself warmer than its surroundings with no sign of slowing: gram for gram, more heat over a year than any chemical reaction gives once. The energy is measured, not derived; the figure per atom waits for counting (1883).

**1876–1881: the electron and the alpha particle.** Goldstein arrives in 1876 and takes over the laboratory's discharge tubes. Michelson begins at Yerba Buena in 1878 (canon). *Mechanism:* Plücker had bent the glow with a magnet in 1858, and the Sprengel pump (1865) gives a vacuum good enough for an electric field to bend the rays too, the step Hertz missed in 1883. Their ratio of charge to mass is the same whatever the gas or the cathode, and the same as the part of the radium-type radiation a magnet bends. In **1879** Goldstein and Michelson announce a common negative constituent of matter. Their tubes almost certainly also show X-rays; what follows from that is for requirement 6.

In 1879 Hillebrand reports a gas driven out of Joachimsthal pitchblende as nitrogen. In **1881** he looks again with a better spectroscope, beside the gas collecting in sealed tubes of the barium-borne salt, and finds in both the yellow line of the solar element of 1868: helium. He prints the correction to his own claim. *Mechanism* for the radiation: strong electromagnets and electric plates bend the heavy rays from the 1876 sources, slightly, and their charge-to-mass ratio comes out at half of hydrogen's, the value for helium that has lost both its charges. The heavy, positively charged radiation is helium. Hillebrand remembers the nitrogen.

**1882–1883: the nucleus.** *Mechanism:*
- Screens of Sidot's blende (1866) show each alpha particle as a single flash. Mele's observers count the flashes by eye in a darkened room, as Geiger and Marsden's counters did in 1909.
- The source is the bismuth-borne preparation, and the target is beaten gold leaf.
- A few particles bounce back. Liang computes what spread-out charge and concentrated charge would each do to the particles; only a concentrated charge explains the rebounds.

In **1883** Goldstein proposes that almost all of an atom's mass sits in a tiny positive core. Counting also gives the number of atoms that decay in a gram each second, so the 1877 heat can now be stated per atom. This is mechanics and counting rather than new theory, so it stays inside R6's rule that theory remains European.

**1884–1886: isotopes and Morley's deficit (requirement 5).** Three routes converge:
- Hillebrand finds pairs of active substances that no chemistry separates.
- Hillebrand finds lead from uranium ores lighter than common lead.
- Goldstein's positive rays, bent by electric and magnetic fields, sort atoms of one element by mass.

By **1886** an element can have atoms of different weights. *Mechanism for the deficit:* in 1884–1885 Morley settles the ratio of oxygen to hydrogen to a few parts in ten thousand (his historical measurement of 1895, brought forward by the laboratory's need for it), and the League's mills have by then driven litres of helium out of pitchblende for him to weigh. In 1886 his atomic weights show helium lighter than four hydrogens by about seven parts in a thousand, well outside his error. Liang and Morley conjecture that the missing mass and radium's heat are the same thing, but they have no way to turn a mass into an energy and say so. No one in Pacifica can say why; Einstein's 1905 paper still gives the reason from Europe (CR-036 D21 unchanged).

**1885–1887: the counter.** Counting flashes by eye cannot see uncharged particles or measure single pulses. At Yerba Buena Michelson's laboratory builds a counter in which a single particle sets off a small discharge in gas between electrodes at high voltage, read on a quadrant electrometer whose swings are photographed; Tesla's works supply the steady high voltage. It is the Rutherford–Geiger counter of 1908, built twenty years early on Pacifica's electrical industry. Neutron numbers are measured by the activity they induce in metal foils, read on the electroscope.

**1887–1890: the neutron (S2).** *Mechanism:* the bismuth-borne source, which gives little penetrating radiation of its own, is put against beryllium. Something comes out that a magnet cannot bend and that goes through lead. Goldstein reads it as an extremely hard ray, as Bothe, Becker and the Joliot-Curies did in 1930–1932. But it knocks hydrogen nuclei out of paraffin with more energy than any ray could give them. Nagaoka, working under Goldstein, asks to measure recoils and is refused; Yerba Buena gives him Michelson's counter. Recoils of hydrogen and nitrogen, worked through by Liang with the conservation of energy and momentum, give a neutral particle of about hydrogen's mass. Yerba Buena announces in **1888**. Carquinez confirms it in **1890**, and Goldstein accepts it then. He keeps his chair and the credit for the nucleus, but the next generation's best students go to Yerba Buena.

**1891–1894: fission and the chain.** *Mechanism:* neutrons, made more effective by slowing them in paraffin or water, are fired into uranium and leave a confusion of new activities. The physicists read them as elements heavier than uranium. Hillebrand's group finds one that behaves in every test like barium. Remembering helium, he trusts the chemistry. In **1892** the uranium core is understood to split. The fragments make ionisation pulses many times larger than any alpha particle's, a direct measurement of the energy, far beyond anything chemistry knows. In **1893–1894** counting shows that each fission releases more than one new neutron, so a self-sustaining reaction is possible in principle. Who decides what is done with that, and how openly, is G1 and requirement 6.

**1881–1898: uranium and the installation (requirement 4).**
- **Small quantities, 1860s–1870s:** pitchblende and factory residues bought from Joachimsthal.
- **1881:** Tom Talbert finds yellow uranium-vanadium ore on Roc Creek in western Montrose County (historical date; ore bodies do not move, R5). Under the 1865 line the Western Slope is Pacifican. **[PROPOSED]** The ore lies in Uncompahgre Ute country, which under CR-033 cannot be sold; the Republic leases the mining ground through its own office and pays the lease fee to the Ute (terms OPEN), so the claims open without the removal of 1881. Hillebrand assays the ore in 1881–1882, and small mines work it for radium-type sources, with the uranium stockpiled as a by-product.
- **1891:** the Ames plant near Telluride (historical date) is on Pacifican ground. This corrects the planning ledger's E13 note ("Union ground"), and its alternating current powers the Western Slope mills.
- **1893–1897:** after the feasibility result, the Roc Creek and Paradox Valley deposits are mined for uranium itself. Historically the same ground shipped ten tons of ore of about one-fifth uranium oxide in 1898. A research installation of the historical kind needed tens of tonnes of uranium as metal and oxide and hundreds of tonnes of very pure graphite.
- **Materials:** Hillebrand's group runs electric furnaces on Tesla's hydroelectric power to make the graphite (Acheson's furnace graphite dates historically from 1896) and the uranium metal (Moissan's, from the 1890s).
- **1898:** the controlled research installation, if those materials arrive. The date most likely to slip is purity; the fallback is 1900.
