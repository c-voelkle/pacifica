# CR-050-R1 — Humboldt College, and the walled college at Hetch Hetchy

**Status:** Part A APPROVED and applied, 3 November 2026. Part B: the site is APPROVED in direction; its terms (B2–B5) are PROPOSED and not yet integrated.

**Authorization:** "I like it. ## Part A — the rename (APPROVED, applied)

**A1. Humboldt College.** The private college founded in San Francisco in 1866 by the city's merchant and banking houses of every faith (CR-035, CR-036 D11) is **Humboldt College**, not Yerba Buena College. The name was chosen by the drafter on the author's instruction; the author may still replace it.

**Why Humboldt.**
- Alexander von Humboldt (1769–1859) was the most admired scientist of the century: the great measurer of nature, cosmopolitan, liberal, an opponent of slavery.
- His name suits a college of pure science and measurement founded without a religious test by merchants of every faith, many of them German-Jewish.
- American cities celebrated his centenary in 1869. **[PROPOSED]** Humboldt College leads San Francisco's celebration.
- It is a person's name, not a city's, and reads as a serious nineteenth-century foundation, beside canon's Vallejo College (CR-038).

**Alternative held in reserve.** Ralston College, after William Ralston (CT-RALSTON, already in canon), as chief founder. It ties the college to a canon financier, but it gives one banker the credit for a foundation by "houses of every faith", and Ralston is ruined in 1875.

**Scope of the rename.**
- **Revised:** current canon (`master_storyline.md`, `character_bible.md`, `character_registry.json`, `AGENTS.md`, `historical_background.md`) and the linter pattern for the neutron's announcement.
- **Unchanged:** earlier decision records (CR-035, CR-036, CR-049, CR-050) and their register entries keep the old name as provenance, as AGENTS.md requires for historical CR attachments. A forbidden pattern keeps the old name out of current canon.

## Part B — the walled college at Hetch Hetchy (site APPROVED in direction; terms PROPOSED)

### B1. The site (APPROVED in direction)

**The valley.** The valley floor lies at about 3,800 feet, some three miles long and at most half a mile wide, with granite walls about two thousand feet high. It has meadow and black oak, Tueeulala and Wapama Falls on the sunlit north wall, Hetch Hetchy Dome above them, Kolana Rock on the south, and the Grand Canyon of the Tuolumne and Rancheria Falls to the east.

**The approach.** The Sierra Railway runs to the state's line near Chinese Camp. The line climbs through Moccasin and Groveland to the waterworks camp at Mather, where the Eleanor branch turns north. The Works' spur continues along the south rim of the gorge to a short tunnel through the granite spur at the valley's mouth. **[PROPOSED]** The tunnel is an invention. Its mouth is the gate, with the guardhouse, the siding and workshops, and the post office where the inner circle's letters are read.

**The layout (PROPOSED).**
- **South terrace near the gate:** Carquinez's laboratories.
- **The meadow:** the challenge-evening hall under the oaks, its north window framing Wapama.
- **East end near Rancheria:** Humboldt's compound.
- **Beyond Humboldt:** the research installation, in a hall cut into the granite.
- **North bench across a footbridge, behind a rockfall earthwork:** the houses, the school and the clinic.

**San Francisco's water (PROPOSED).** San Francisco takes Muir's alternative: Lake Eleanor and Cherry Valley on the Tuolumne, with the Spring Valley Water Company bought out early as a bridge. The waterworks railway and camps cover the Works' traffic. Muir wins his last fight without knowing why, and he dies in 1914.

### B2. The public face: the Tuolumne Station (PROPOSED)

The lesson of Los Alamos is that the place was never the secret. Hispano and Pueblo neighbours, among them women from San Ildefonso Pueblo, worked on the Hill throughout the war; they knew there was a laboratory and nothing about what it did. The secret lived behind laboratory doors, in papers and in heads.

So the valley does not pretend to be empty. It is publicly and truthfully **the Tuolumne Station of the Light and Matter Works**, a high-Sierra research station.
- **Its published work** is the true noise of CR-050 N1: radiation medicine and the exposure registers' clinic, atmospheric ionisation at altitude, Meitner's beta-ray spectra, Dempster's isotope survey without uranium, luminescence. Inner members publish this open work under the station's name, so prominent names stay visible (the Flerov lesson).
- **The valley floor is closed** to the public as a working station on state land: bought from its private claimants, leased from the Me-Wuk (B3) and protected as part of the Tuolumne watershed, whose water the Moccasin powerhouse and the irrigation districts downstream really use.
- **What anyone can learn** by looking or asking (buildings, lights, scientists, a railway, a clinic) is already in the station's annual report. A talkative witness can confirm only what is published.

### B3. The Me-Wuk compact (PROPOSED)

**What is actually at stake.** By 1909 no one lives in the valley year-round. The Tuolumne Me-Wuk, with Mono Paiute from across the crest, use it seasonally: the autumn acorn harvest, summer camps, and the burning that keeps the meadows open. Sheepmen and a few private claimants graze it. Nothing has to be evicted. What has to be settled is the Me-Wuk's seasonal right, and by CR-033 that needs their consent.

**The terms**, concluded in 1909–1910 with the Tuolumne Me-Wuk as a body, on CR-033's model of the Ute lease on the Western Slope:

1. **Lease, not purchase.** The Works leases the valley floor's use for the seal's term, renewable. The fee is paid to the band every year, and ownership of the right stays theirs.
2. **Better ground in exchange.** The band receives title to Poopenaut Valley below the gorge and protected rights to oak groves on the Tuolumne and Cherry Creek outside the fence, more acorn ground than the valley held. Historically San Francisco's reservoir drowned the valley's groves with no compensation at all.
3. **The valley's own right kept, in one form.** Each autumn the band's gatherers harvest the north-side groves for an agreed fortnight. Their camp is by the houses, at the west end, away from the laboratories and the installation. This is the only outside presence on the meadow, and it is announced, bounded and routine.
4. **Work with honour.** Me-Wuk men are hired as the valley's wardens. They patrol the rims and the trails, which are their own country, and keep the meadows by burning, as they always have. They are paid at the Works' rates.
5. **The clinic** serves the band's families.
6. **The same bond for everyone inside.** Every warden and burner who works inside the fence signs the bond that binds every Pacifican employee of the Works, no more and no less. Gatherers who come only for the harvest sign nothing and are shown nothing.

**Why it is airtight.**
- **Nothing to tell.** No Me-Wuk enters a laboratory, the hall or the installation. What they see is what the station publishes. Outsiders learn nothing from them that is not in the annual report. This is the Los Alamos structure, and it held there for three years with whole pueblos next door.
- **Their interest is the station's survival.** The compact gives them more land, money, work and medicine than any alternative on offer; historically the alternative was the drowning of the valley. A leak that closed the station would cost them the lease, the wages and the groves.
- **The vulnerability becomes the alarm.** The wardens on the rims are the first to meet a stranger asking questions: a journalist, a hiker off the trail, a foreign visitor. They report him. Curious minds who come to talk to the Me-Wuk meet the people paid and trusted to notice them.
- **The real risk is inside, not outside.** As everywhere, the leaks that matter would come from people who know the work: scientists, their wives and children. The rule of Los Alamos applies to all: no work talk outside the laboratories and the hall, and the post is read. The Me-Wuk are the safest people in the valley because they are never told anything.

**Literary note.** A Me-Wuk warden on the rim watching the lights below, who knows exactly where everything is and nothing of what it does, is a point of view the Chronicler can use without breaching anything.

**OPEN:** the Mono Paiute's share; the band's leaders' names (historical, not invented).

### B4. Humboldt's compact of 1910 (PROPOSED)

**Why Humboldt comes in.** Its laboratories have run on the Works' sources since the 1880s, and the cut-off of December 1908 shows how quickly they can stop. Its best people will be recruited behind the wall whether it signs or not. Staying outside means missing the greatest physics of the age. Its founding houses live on the state's credit. It signs because it must, and it negotiates hard because it can: without Humboldt the Works loses the rival that keeps its own results honest (E2: answerability).

**The Compact of the Tuolumne, 1910.**

- **Parties:** the Light and Matter Works (Hillebrand) and the Polytechnic estate (Soberanes) for the state; Humboldt College's trustees and its president.
- **Term:** the seal's term, to November 1928, renewable.

1. **Its own ground.** Humboldt holds its own compound at the valley's east end, with its own director, workshops and budget line. The Works funds the inner work of both schools. Humboldt's share is fixed in the compact for five years at a time and cannot be cut within a period.
2. **Admission.** Humboldt nominates its own people. Admission keeps the two-sponsor rule, but one sponsor may be Humboldt's own master. Carquinez cannot veto a Humboldt nominee except on the bond.
3. **Equal access.** Sources, materials, the Works' instruments, time on the installation by a schedule published inside, the internal journal, and the right to name its own respondent at every challenge evening.
4. **Independence of method.** The Works cannot direct Humboldt's research within the inner field. Humboldt may pursue rival routes, and does: its rival route to light uranium (CR-050 M1).
5. **Contests between the schools** run under declared terms and are judged by three: one judge named by each school and a chair named by the sovereign's key. This is not the Polytechnic chief's, since the Polytechnic is Carquinez's own estate.
6. **Credit.** The sealed roll lists results by school, and at the opening each school is credited by name.
7. **Property.** Nothing in the sealed work is private property. Patents, where any are filed, are held in the state's name until the opening. Humboldt's open work stays its own, and the state endows chairs at Humboldt for the people it seconds.
8. **A champion.** The Works pays for Humboldt to recruit a champion of its own choosing. Humboldt chooses Robert Millikan.
9. **Disputes.** Disputes between the schools go to the panel of three. Disputes over secrecy go to the two keys. Humboldt's president has one right of appeal to the sovereign in person in each dispute.
10. **Exit.** Humboldt may leave the inner work on a year's notice. Its people stay bound by their bonds to the end of the term, as Nagaoka is when he resigns in 1920.
11. **Under protest.** Humboldt's trustees sign under written protest: an argument for openness in Bohr's terms. The protest is deposited with the seal and published with it at the opening in 1927, the corrections column honouring dissent as well as discovery.
12. **Its public life is unchanged.** It teaches, publishes its open work and keeps its name. Its people in the valley appear as staff of the Tuolumne Station.

**Literary note.** Clauses 5 and 11 give the rivalry a stage and the dissent a voice. Millikan's demand of 1920 (CR-050 S4) is a claim under clause 6, which the compact already answers.
