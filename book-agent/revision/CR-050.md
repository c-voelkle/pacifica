## What this CR does

CR-049 left the decisive period open: what Pacifica does between fission (1908) and the demonstration at sea (1927) to make its lead last. A review of CR-049 against Rhodes found four linked blockers:

1. Europe has run the same neutron experiments since 1902, so it cannot plausibly stay blind to fission for nineteen years without a stated mechanism. Historically four laboratories converged within weeks in January 1939.
2. Theory cannot keep its historical dates once Pacifica has published the electron (1879) and the nucleus (1883).
3. No one in the storyline finds the light uranium isotope or shows that it is the one that matters.
4. The magnet halls are a single phrase, though historically electromagnetic separation was one of the largest and most troublesome industrial efforts of its century.

This CR answers all four with one design: the 1908 result is sealed, not suppressed or falsified; the work continues behind a wall where the old rivalries keep running; Europe is fed a great deal of true and excellent science that has nothing to do with energy; ore is bought behind a reputation for greed; and the decisive industrial facts are kept inside.

## G1–G3: the seal (1908–1909)

### The occasion

Julian, a year on the throne, spends the spring and summer of 1908 touring Europe's capitals (CR-040). He comes home having seen the armies and the men who command them; in October Austria annexes Bosnia, and Europe is one crisis from war. That autumn Hillebrand's group trusts its chemistry, as Hillebrand trusted it over helium in 1881, and concludes that the uranium core splits. The fission paper is written in November 1908.

Victoria, no longer Regent, hears of it first, through Hillebrand. She wants it printed. Julian, back from Europe, does not.

### The argument

Both sides argue from Elias's memorandum (CR-049, N2), and both are quoting it correctly.

- **Victoria, in Bohr's part.** Openness is what makes the commonwealth's science self-correcting. "A falsified account robs everyone who must build on it": a withheld account robs them too. She also doubts it can be done at all: "you would have to turn the country into one factory."
- **Julian, in Szilard's part.** Nothing false will be printed; the account is kept, not falsified, and it will be opened. A small chance that Europe builds this first is not small if it means the end of the commonwealth. Rabi's arithmetic, in Rhodes: a ten per cent chance "is not a remote possibility if it means that we may die of it."
- **Hillebrand's compromise.** The Paris Académie des sciences has kept sealed, dated envelopes as proof of priority since the eighteenth century. The paper will be deposited the same way: dated, witnessed, sealed, and opened in due course. Rhodes gives the closer precedents: Szilard's secret patent held by the Admiralty (1936), and the two Columbia papers (Fermi's group; Szilard and Zinn) mailed to the *Physical Review* on 16 March 1939 "to establish priority" while asking the editor to hold them.

### The settlement (PROPOSED)

- **Deposit.** The fission paper (November 1908) and the chain result (1909) are sealed in the Thalassia archive, each in the hand of its authors, witnessed by the Works' director and the Polytechnic chief.
- **Keys.** Opening needs the sovereign and the Polytechnic chief together: Julian with Soberanes, then with Vance III from 1915 (as CR-049 already provides). The Works' director is custodian.
- **Victoria's term (G3).** She accepts the seal on one condition, written into the deposit: it is opened no later than twenty years after it is made, that is, by November 1928. That gives 1927 its reason. Julian opens it a year early, at the demonstration, on his own timing.
- **Enforcement.** The superseded 1892–1895 design's mechanisms carry over (CR-049, F1): the source contracts and the register. From 1908 the Works stops licensing strong neutron sources abroad, citing domestic demand and the new safety rules (N1), which are both true.
- **What is never done.** No false paper, no false denial, no published claim that uranium has a single isotope, no correction of a true result abroad by a false one. Pacifica stays silent and lets Europe's own errors stand: that is the moral price of the policy, and the walled college feels it.

**Character consequence.** This is the first crack between Julian and Victoria, before the Senate quarrel of 1910–1911 and the Siberian break of 1921. In 1920, when Hahn publishes (G4), Victoria demands the seal be opened; Julian holds her to her own term. Her break of 1921 now has two causes, Siberia and the seal, and she can say the same thing about both: he asked one party and not the other.

## G2: the walled college (1909–1927)

**From Rhodes.** Groves wanted strict compartmentalisation; Oppenheimer won a central laboratory "where people could talk freely", and Serber's opening lectures delighted scientists who had only been guessing. The lesson for Pacifica is that the customs of CR-049 survive the wall if they move inside it.

- **Inside.** Challenge evenings, contests with declared terms, an internal journal with its corrections column, and a sealed roll of honour that names who did what. The custom of "corrections in the record" (CR-049, E4) is kept in full; it is only the audience that shrinks.
- **The rivalry continues.** Yerba Buena, the private college, goes behind the wall under protest and keeps its independence of method. Its champion is Millikan, pulled in 1910 (ledger L8). Carquinez and the Works hold the main line; Yerba Buena runs a rival route to light uranium under a contest with declared terms. The magnet route wins first, in 1919–1921, because it rests on known physics; it wins at enormous cost (M1).
- **Who stays outside.** Some well-known names must remain visible, because the disappearance of the best names from the journals is itself a signal. **[APPROVED]** Rutherford, arriving c. 1909–1910, refuses to go behind the wall, as Rabi refused Los Alamos. He stays the public face of Pacifican physics and of New Zealand's drift towards Pacifica, does his artificial disintegration openly (historically 1917–1919; the ledger leaves it undated), knows there is a wall and never asks what is behind it.
- **Site (OPEN).** The college needs isolation; the halls need Sierra power and cover. Suggestion: the halls on the western Sierra among the electrochemical works (M1), the college at a separate remote site; one candidate is the Owens Valley, which would need land terms under CR-033 like the Ute lease.

## G4–G5: Europe's rediscovery and the opening

### How Europe is slowed, without a false word (PROPOSED)

- From 1908 strong neutron sources stop leaving Pacifica (G1).
- Meitner leaves Berlin for Pacifica around 1910, breaking up Europe's best team (historically Hahn and Meitner began working together in 1907).
- Europe's "elements heavier than uranium" (CR-049, 1902–1907) go uncorrected. Pacifica publishes nothing about them, true or false.
- The war of 1914–1918 empties Europe's laboratories (historically Hahn served in chemical warfare).
- Europe's attention is fully occupied by excellent true noise (N1).

### Hahn (G4)

**[APPROVED]** In **1920** Hahn, working in Berlin with Vienna's sources, finds barium among uranium's products and publishes. Europe confirms it within weeks, as it did in 1939. Britain and Germany start small, slow programmes; Europe's physicists conclude, as Bohr did in 1939, that a weapon would need a factory no one can afford. Hahn is honoured, probably with a chemistry Nobel in the early 1920s. Pacifica says nothing. Meitner, inside the wall, knows the truth, which reverses her historical exclusion from Hahn's 1944 prize.

The 1913 alternative is not taken.

### Hillebrand

William Francis Hillebrand died on 7 February 1925 (historical date; he died in Washington). He leaves the seat in 1921 under CR-038's rule and dies two years before the seal is opened, his greatest result known only to the people behind the wall. **[APPROVED]** In canon he dies on the historical date, in Honolulu (S4).

### The opening (G5)

At the demonstration in autumn 1927, or in the days before it, Julian and Vance III open the seal. The Works publishes the 1908 and 1909 papers in facsimile, with the sealed roll of honour, through the corrections column. The names restored: Hillebrand and his group (fission, 1908); the counting team (more than one neutron per fission, 1909); Meitner (the light-isotope conjecture); Dempster, Nagaoka and the proof team (1915–1917); Langmuir and the halls. Hahn is named as the independent second finder (R3), and the custom that gave Kate Curran her credit back in 1872 now gives it back on the largest scale it will ever have. **[OPEN]** Whose term for the splitting sticks, the sealed paper's or Hahn's; R3 suggests the first finder's.

## K1: theory and the ledger's P13 row

- **1883–1900: the crisis of the stable atom.** The 1883 nucleus proves an atom with a tiny charged core, which classical physics says should collapse. Balmer's formula (1885) and Rydberg's (1888–1890) make it sharper. Kelvin, Lorentz, Larmor, FitzGerald and Poincaré build models that fail. **[PROPOSED]** Nagaoka proposes his Saturnian atom at Carquinez in the 1890s (historically 1904), the brave Pacifican model that fails; theory stays European (R6).
- **Planck, December 1900 (unchanged).** He reaches the quantum by his own route, the thermodynamics of heat radiation and the German national laboratory's measurements of 1899–1900. Pacifica can sharpen the data (Langley's bolometer, which he carried up Mount Whitney in 1881, on Pacifican ground; Michelson's precision) but not the leap. The motif holds: Pacifica measures and asks, Europe answers, as Einstein did for Morley's mass deficit.
- **Einstein, 1905 (unchanged).** Light quanta in March, relativity in June. What changes is acceptance: historically light quanta were resisted until Compton (1923); Millikan's measurements of 1914–1916 confirmed Einstein's equation while Millikan himself still rejected the quantum of light. Here the photoelectric effect has been measured precisely in Pacifica since the 1890s (the electron is known from 1879), and physics has needed something like the quantum since 1883. **[PROPOSED]** The photon is accepted by about 1907–1908.
- **The quantum atom, 1907 (P13 changes).** **[APPROVED]** Arthur Erich Haas (born 1884) in Vienna combines Planck's quantum with the nuclear atom of 1883 and Balmer's formula, and derives hydrogen's spectral constant (historically in 1910 he estimated the hydrogen atom's size from Planck's quantum and Thomson's atom, from Thomson's atom). Vienna becomes Europe's centre in theory as well as radium. **[PROPOSED]** Bohr (born 1885) extends it to heavier atoms around 1911–1913, so his historical work survives as the second step rather than the first.
- **Quantum mechanics a few years early.** **[PROPOSED]** c. 1922–1924.
- **The edge of the lead.** A mature European nuclear theory, roughly Gamow's alpha decay (1928) and Bohr's reasoning of 1939, is what lets outsiders work out which uranium isotope matters. Here it arrives in the mid-1920s. The 1927 demonstration therefore sits at the edge of Pacifica's lead in knowledge, while its lead in industry is still a decade or more.

## K2: other knock-ons (PROPOSED)

- **Atomic number, late 1880s.** Scattering measures the nucleus's charge; Peirce's fits across foils give charges near half the atomic weight and in the order of the periodic table. The idea that charge, not weight, orders the elements (historically van den Broek 1911, Moseley 1913) comes while Mendeleev is alive to see his table's anomalies explained.
- **Kelvin and Darwin, c. 1880.** Morley's heat measurement of 1877 removes Kelvin's objection about the age of the Earth twenty-five years early.
- **X-ray medicine from c. 1880,** and with it X-ray injury (see N1).
- **Europe's counter-institutions.** **[PROPOSED]** Vienna's Radium Institute is founded in the 1890s (historically funded in 1908 and opened in 1910, with Stefan Meyer as acting director), on the radium Vienna learned to keep after the cheap years. The Kaiser Wilhelm Society keeps its historical date (1911) but is founded explicitly to compete with Pacifica. The Cavendish remains Britain's centre.
- **The public idea of atomic energy.** Soddy's radium lectures inspired Wells (*The World Set Free*, 1914). Here the cycle starts about thirty years earlier: an atomic romance by Verne or the young Wells around 1890–1900, and a common theme in Hearst's press. But the public also absorbs Soddy's view of 1904 that nature alone sets the rate of release, and his hope that nature would guard her secret, as the conclusion that the energy can never be tapped (Soddy himself, by 1909, imagined transmutation remaking the world). That belief is Pacifica's best cover until 1927. When Hahn publishes in c. 1920, the primed press explodes, and that too is cover: everyone talks about the idea, and no one sees the factory.

## K3 and N1: no radium craze; true noise

The author's rule: Pacifica publishes the dangers, and the dangers are part of a deliberate programme of true, non-energy science.

### Radiation and health (PROPOSED dates)

- **The raw material is perfectly timed.** In 1879 the physicians Härting and Hesse identified the miners' disease of Schneeberg in Saxony as a malignant lung tumour (historical; they classed it as lymphosarcoma, and it was confirmed as lung cancer later). Pacifica has its own miners at Joachimsthal and on the Western Slope, weighable radium handled since 1876, X-ray tubes from about 1879 and Koch's school.
- **1880s:** the Works' physicians record radiation burns and blood changes among source handlers.
- **1888 onwards:** exposure registers for miners, mill hands and laboratory staff are tabulated on Hollerith's cards at the Office of Public Measures. Pacifica founds radiation epidemiology. Kate Curran's counting room enters the record as the first cohort studied, a dignified place rather than only a tragic one.
- **1890s:** the link between the mines' emanation and the miners' cancer is proposed (historically the 1920s–1930s); ventilation rules on the Western Slope and in the Ute lease's mines.
- **1890s–1900s:** radiotherapy at Carquinez and Honolulu; the Works' internal protection rules become a public standard about 1900.
- **c. 1910:** **[PROPOSED]** Pacifica convenes an international conference on radium and X-ray protection (historically the international committee came only in 1928). It is honest science, it is diplomacy, and it explains why the Works now licenses fewer strong sources abroad.

The result: radioactivity is understood by the public as medicine and danger, not power. The radium craze of history (tonics, cosmetics, luminous-paint factories) is strangled at birth.

### The rest of the programme

All published, all first-rate:

- radiometric dating and the age of the Earth, from Hillebrand's uranium-lead work of 1886 (historically Boltwood, 1907);
- radioactive tracers in chemistry and biology (historically Hevesy, 1913);
- atmospheric ionisation and cosmic rays (historically Hess, 1912), on mountain and balloon fellowships;
- luminescence and its instruments;
- Meitner's beta-ray spectra (her real work of the 1910s–1920s), Rutherford's artificial disintegration, and Dempster's isotope survey (U1).

**Two cautions from history.** In 1942 a young Soviet physicist, Flerov, noticed that Western fission papers had stopped appearing and wrote to Stalin (Rhodes's sequel, *Dark Sun*). The noise must therefore be excellent, because a fall in quality is a signal; and the stars must stay visible, because missing names are a signal. Staff the wall partly with people whose absence nobody notices.

## R1: radium diplomacy

### Hiding behind greed (from 1908)

Because Pacifica publishes the dangers, radium stays valuable for respectable reasons: hospitals want it for radiotherapy, navies for luminous instruments. From 1908 the Works buys whole ores wherever it can, radium and uranium together, and is seen as a ruthless radium trust, like Standard Oil, or Union Minière's historical radium monopoly. Greed is a motive every banker understands; the real motive disappears behind it.

### Staged charity and concessions

The historical model: in 1921 American women raised the money for a gram of radium, which President Harding presented to Marie Curie at the White House. Radium was already a gift of state.

- **Gifts.** Grams of radium presented under the Works' name to foreign institutes, universities and hospitals, with ceremony and newsreel, timed to concessions over ore, mining ground or transit.
- **The asymmetry.** What Pacifica takes in exchange is always the uranium side, the one trade no outside observer thinks worth anything until 1927.

### Changes to CR-049's ore plans (PROPOSED)

- **Principle.** CR-049's "lets every partner keep the radium" stands for the years before 1908, when it is harmless. After 1908 it would be the most revealing pattern possible, buying the waste and leaving the treasure, so it is superseded by this section.
- **Joachimsthal (1908–1909).** Austria keeps its radium, because Vienna's Radium Institute is a matter of national prestige, but the finance rider is greedy in a way everyone can read: the Works takes the exclusive marketing of Austrian radium outside the Empire and a share of its sales. The uranium compounds and residues clause sits among those terms as an afterthought. Stefan Meyer's question still comes; it is now harder to ask, because the obvious answer is money.
- **Shinkolobwe (1915–1919).** Unchanged in structure. Belgium's free radium allowance for its hospitals is the charity on show.
- **Fergana (1919–1921).** The concession is presented as a radium play beside Emba.

## U1: light uranium, 1909–1917 (APPROVED)

Each step gives credit to someone who historically did the same work later.

1. **The anomaly, 1909–1912.** The walled college's measurements show natural uranium behaving as if only a small part of it splits easily, while thorium behaves differently: the clue Bohr worked from in February 1939. **Lise Meitner**, arriving about 1910, frames the conjecture that the readily split part is a rare isotope hiding in the common one. She gets the interpretation history rightly credits her with. Goldstein, past sixty, attacks it at a challenge evening; it is his last stand, and he loses.
2. **The faint companion, c. 1915.** **Arthur Jeffrey Dempster** (born Toronto, 1886) studied in Germany from about 1911 and left for the United States when the war broke out in 1914; historically he went to Chicago, built his mass spectrometer in 1918 and found the light uranium isotope in 1935 on long exposures, where Aston had missed it. Here Pacifica recruits him in 1914, and he sees it about twenty years early. His instrument descends from Goldstein's positive-ray sorting of 1886. *Canada:* his recruitment must fit the Canada policy to be set in its own session.
3. **The proof, 1916–1917.** A few grains separated in Dempster's instrument are tested in a neutron beam (historically Nier and Dunning, 1940), under wartime pressure. **[PROPOSED]** Nagaoka leads the test. The result gives Julian and Vance III the hard fact on which to authorise the halls.

**Sealing, without a false paper.** Dempster's instrument and his element-by-element isotope survey are published, as Aston's work was (Nobel, 1922): it honours him and gives Europe real results. Uranium is left out of the published survey. Heavy elements are the hardest to resolve and naturally come last, so the gap looks like slow progress, not silence. Uranium is never published as having a single isotope. The lead on this point lasts until Europe's nuclear theory matures in the mid-1920s (K1).

## M1: the magnet halls (APPROVED)

### People

- **Irving Langmuir** (born Brooklyn, 1881; *pull*). Historically he graduated from Columbia's School of Mines in 1903, took his doctorate under Nernst at Göttingen (1906), and at General Electric built high-vacuum valves (c. 1912–1913, at the same time as Harold Arnold at Western Electric, whom the courts later held to have anticipated him) and the condensation pump (1916), worked out how hot metals emit electrons, and won the 1932 Nobel in chemistry. Here he comes in 1903 to Tesla's electrical institute instead of Göttingen and becomes Tesla's student. He is master of the halls.
- **Tesla**, about 61 in 1917: patron, supplier of power and showman. He leaves the seat at 68 in 1924 (CR-038). Tesla's grand visions against Langmuir's patient measurements is the halls' human conflict.
- **Steinmetz** (died 1923): the electrical supply.
- **Dempster:** the halls are his instrument made enormous. Historically Lawrence's separation units were his cyclotron magnets turned into giant mass spectrometers; the page stays at that level.
- **Hillebrand's chemists,** after 1921 under his successor: recovering the product.

### Consequence for the valve (corrects CR-049)

The valves of c. 1895 (CR-049, S2) stay soft, erratic tubes. They are good enough for the counters, but unreliable. Langmuir's hard-vacuum valves come about 1908, roughly five years before history. This keeps the valve inside the tools Pacifica actually has.

### What makes the halls believable

These details come from the history of Oak Ridge's electromagnetic plant.

- **Power and cover.** The halls draw a visible share of Sierra hydropower. They stand among the aluminium and carbide works of the ledger's Sierra electrochemical cluster (K9), where power-hungry plants are normal.
- **Silver.** Copper was so short that the United States lent its Treasury silver for the magnet windings. Pacifica winds its magnets with Comstock silver lent from the Mint.
- **The workforce.** At Oak Ridge, young women with high-school educations famously ran the units better than the physicists. Here the operators are examined young women (examinations open to women since 1859), and "things work in this country" (CR-049) is proved in the halls.
- **Failure first.** The first hall fails within weeks and has to be rebuilt; a year is lost.
- **The contest inside.** Yerba Buena's rival route runs under a contest with declared terms (G2). The magnet route wins first.

### X2: the breeding line and fusion (APPROVED; thorium deferred to a later CR)

**Why Pacifica breeds.** There is no reason for Pacifica not to, and a strong reason for it to. The light isotope is less than one per cent of uranium. A state that has spent fifteen years cornering the world's uranium (CR-049; R1) multiplies the worth of its monopoly many times over if it can turn the common isotope, and thorium, into fuel. Breeding is the durability of the lead in its purest form. Historically breeding was pursued when uranium was thought scarce (1940s–1950s) and dropped when large new deposits made it cheap; in Pacifica scarcity is the policy, so the case never weakens.

**Why it does not come first.** The weapon (M1) and breeding compete for the same scarce things:
- **One colossus at a time.** The halls are already Pacifica's largest industrial enterprise; historically the plant that made element 94 was of comparable scale.
- **Materials.** A breeder needs fast neutrons, a liquid-metal coolant and the chemistry to separate the bred fuel; each took years historically, after the first reactors were running.
- **Secrecy.** The chemistry of the bred elements is as revealing as fission. It stays inside the wall.
- **Off the page.** Historically the bred element also became a second road to the weapon. As with the device (CR-049), that side stays sealed in the Thalassia archive; the story only needs to say that the Works knows.

**Japan and the fuel cycle.** Japan has almost no uranium. The best-known physicist in Pacifica, honestly wrong in public, is the seal's best cover.

But Langmuir founds plasma physics, as he historically did (he named "plasma" in 1928), from the halls' ion sources. That gives Pacifica the seed of a small fusion programme of tinkerers, experimenters and engineers from the late 1920s that yields knowledge, not power. **[PROPOSED]** Its custom-defining moment is a premature claim of fusion, publicly retracted in the corrections column (historically ZETA in 1958), which shows that the nomos holds even for its most glamorous science.

### X5 and X6: recommendations (APPROVED)

**X5: not the stratosphere.** The author's instinct about fallout is right in one sense, but a stratospheric shot fails in 1927 for three reasons.

1. *It cannot get there.* Piccard's balloon first reached the stratosphere in 1931 with a light gondola; lifting a four-tonne device there in 1927 is not credible.
2. *It does not show what the demonstration must show.* Burst high above the sea, the device does nothing to ships. Its visible signs are a flash and an artificial aurora (as over Hawaii after a high-altitude test in 1962), which make a poor proof for attachés who came to see a fleet sink.
3. *It does not remove fallout; it spreads it.* What reaches the stratosphere comes down over the whole hemisphere for a year or more. Historically, stratospheric injection is where worldwide fallout came from.

The real choice is between the two historical tests at Bikini in 1946:

- **An air burst over the target line** (Bikini's first test) leaves little local fallout. But warships proved tougher than expected: few ships sank, and the press found the result disappointing.
- **An underwater burst** (Bikini's second test) gives the approved image, the mile-high column and the sunk ships, and contaminates water and ships badly.

**Recommendation.** Keep the underwater burst, because the demonstration's purpose is the fleet, and plan the fallout as a Pacifican state would:
- **Move the site south.** The great-circle midpoint between Yokohama and San Francisco lies in the North Pacific south of the Aleutians, among rich fisheries. The prevailing westerlies and the North Pacific Current would carry contamination towards Alaska and Pacifica's own coast. A site further south, in the empty centre of the subtropical gyre, is safer and still "between the two crowns".
- **Close the zone** weeks in advance, with Japan's fisheries authorities warning their fleets.
- **Never reboard; scuttle** the target ships afterwards.
- **Publish the measurements** of the zone afterwards, which is true noise again and shows the ethos in action.

**X6: yes, the materials can arrive by 1913, if Julian funds the installation in 1909.** Historically, from Joliot's measurement in spring 1939 to Fermi's first pile in December 1942 took about three and a half years, with wartime money. 1909 to late 1913 is four and a half years, so the pace is comparable.
- **Graphite.** Acheson's furnace graphite exists from 1896. Its problem was trace impurities that absorb neutrons; in 1942 the remedy was choosing the right raw coke and testing every batch. Pacifica has cheap California petroleum coke from its 1876 oil programme, and from 1909 it has neutron sources and counters to test each batch. The purity problem appears in 1910–1911, when the first test assemblies absorb too much, and is solved in 1911–1912.
- **Uranium.** Pacifica has been stockpiling uranium since 1881 as a by-product of radium. Purifying it and making metal was a crisis in 1942 that chemists solved in months once the requirement was stated. Hillebrand is the leading analytical chemist of his generation, so the problem is his.
- **The first installation runs mostly on pressed oxide,** with some metal, as Fermi's did in 1942.

**Recommendation:** first installation in **late 1913**. Wartime demands from 1914 then pull Hillebrand's chemists away, which explains why the light-uranium proof waits until 1916–1917. Europe's graphite error after Hahn (1920–1921) goes uncorrected.

## S1–S4: how the secret is made and kept (APPROVED)

The pattern is Rhodes's. In 1939 secrecy began as a scientist's initiative (Szilard), was argued among scientists (Rabi, Fermi, Teller, Wigner, Bohr), went to the state only when it became too serious to hold alone (Wigner), and held only once the people who controlled publication enforced it (Pegram, the editors). It failed whenever it cost someone credit: Joliot published, in part, to secure priority. Pacifica follows the same sequence, and its institutions solve the problem of credit that broke secrecy in 1939.

### S1: whose initiative (1908–1909)

1. **November 1908: the challenge evening.** By custom (CR-049, I1) Hillebrand's barium result is stated at a challenge evening before it goes to the journal. The appointed respondent is **Charles Sanders Peirce**, sixty-nine. He does the arithmetic on the spot: the energy per split, measured from the pulses, times the atoms in a block of uranium. Then he asks Szilard's question of 1939: does the split release neutrons? If it does, he argues, the conservative course is to assume a chain is possible and act on that, not to wait for proof. It is his own logic of abduction (CR-049, E2: a conjecture earns a trial before belief), and it is Rabi's arithmetic in Rhodes, made by the man who once threw away true readings and learned the cost.
2. **November–December 1908: the voluntary hold.** Hillebrand withholds his own paper "for verification", which is true. Six or eight people know. This is the Szilard phase: no state, no law, only agreement among scientists.
3. **December 1908: Victoria and Soberanes.** Hillebrand tells Victoria, his patron since the charter, and Soberanes, the Polytechnic chief, whose estate holds the General Staff. Victoria says: publish once it is verified.
4. **1909: the count.** The counting team shows more than one neutron per split. As with Wigner in March 1939, the matter is now too serious for the scientists to hold alone. Hillebrand and Soberanes go to Julian, now home from Europe.
5. **1909: the argument and the seal.** Victoria against Julian, Hillebrand's compromise and the settlement (G1–G3).

**Answer:** the initiative is the scientists' (Peirce's argument, Hillebrand's hold); the sanction is the state's. That is historically the believable order, and it keeps the nomos intact: the commonwealth's scientists police themselves first.

### S2: how everyone is brought in (1909–1910)

- **The seal removes the cost to credit.** In 1939 secrecy broke because priority mattered: the Columbia papers were mailed "to establish priority", and Joliot published. Pacifica's sealed envelope and sealed roll of honour make silence cost nothing in credit. Every result is dated, witnessed and guaranteed its name. This is the central mechanism; the rest supports it.
- **The work is better inside.** The walled college has the world's sources, the best instruments, the halls later, and its own challenge evenings. A scientist loses an audience, not a community.
- **There is nowhere else to do it.** The Works' source trade (CR-049) means that a dissenter who leaves cannot continue the work anywhere else for years.
- **Yerba Buena.** The private college is brought in under a contest with declared terms (G2), its independence of method guaranteed in writing. Millikan arrives in 1910.
- **The legal base.** **[PROPOSED]** No new law. Julian's ordinances must be laid before the Senate's next session (canon), so a secret ordinance is impossible. The rules are written instead into the Works' charter (a state champion with the state's shares, CR-039) and the Polytechnic estate's own statutes, which already bind its officers, its General Staff and its examined staff. Following CR-049's precedent ("the rules live in the school's contracts"), secrecy lives in the contracts too. Victoria recognises the form: personal rule carried out through the estates, beyond the Senate's reach. It is a second strand in the 1910–1911 Senate quarrel.

**Two circles of admission:**
- **The outer college** publishes the true noise (N1). Every recruit starts here and is not told what lies further in.
- **The inner circle** works on fission. Admission needs sponsorship by two masters already inside and a reason: the work needs that person. Admission is marked by signing the seal's bond.

**[PROPOSED]** Szilard, arriving at twenty-two in 1920 (historically the chief advocate of voluntary secrecy in 1939), finds it already invented and admired. From his admission (c. 1924) he becomes its sharpest critic from inside and designs the 1927 opening.

**The bond.** It extends the service bonds of 1888 (CR-049, I2). The signatory keeps the seal for its term. Breach is treated under the Polytechnic estate's discipline as theft from the commonwealth, in the memorandum's own words. Citizenship comes with every chair (CR-034, CR-036), so foreign recruits are Pacificans under the same discipline.

**A promised opening.** Victoria's twenty-year term (G3) does a second job: it makes secrecy bearable for Bohr-minded people. History's most damaging leak of atomic secrets, Fuchs's, came from a vetted man with an ideological motive. Vetting cannot see conviction. A secret with a written end date gives an idealist a reason to wait instead of acting alone.

**Watching, as at Los Alamos.** The inner circle's letters pass through the Works' own post office and are read, as Los Alamos mail was censored. The halls' operators are told only that they make "a separated material for the Works' instruments", which is true and incomplete, as at Oak Ridge.

**The kahuna register.** **[PROPOSED]** Since 1887 Honolulu has called the Five *nā Kahuna*, the masters of their crafts (CR-027). Some Hawaiian participants describe the wall in the language of guarded knowledge, *kapu* knowledge held by those trained to hold it. Others contest it in print as Kamakau's heirs (CR-049, E3). This is a civic usage, not a claim about the old religion, and it gives the secret a sacred register in Hawaiian hands.

**c. 1919: Hearst's press.** A Hearst reporter notices how much Sierra power the new "electrochemical works" draw. Hearst holds a seat among the Five and spikes the story; it costs him with his own editors. This is the superseded 1892–1895 design's "test of Hearst's press" (CR-049, F1), carried over and redated.

**1920: Hahn.** This is the Joliot temptation. Two inner members want to publish Pacifica's priority before Hahn is honoured. Victoria demands the opening and is refused (G1). **[APPROVED]** The two have opposite motives, echoing the two historical ways secrecy broke in 1939: Joliot's concern for priority and Bohr's principle of openness.

- **Robert Millikan, for credit.** He is Yerba Buena's champion since 1910 (ledger L8), fifty-two, and ambitious for his college. He wants Yerba Buena's share of the record (the neutron of 1899 and its part in the work behind the wall) published before a German takes the glory. Historically he was a man who guarded credit closely; his treatment of his student Harvey Fletcher's share in the oil-drop experiment is still argued over. Pacifica holds him the way the system was designed to: the sealed roll names Yerba Buena's results separately, and his open work, the elementary charge measured by the oil drop at Yerba Buena (1910–1913), is put forward publicly. He receives the Nobel Prize in 1923, as he did historically, for true work done in the open. He stays inside: the seal bought with public honour.
- **Nagaoka Hantarō, on principle.** He is fifty-five, at Carquinez since 1887 and a Pacifican by his chair. He argues as Bohr would: once Hahn has published, the race is open, and silence now hides only Pacifica's head start, which is a deception of the world in all but name. He loses the argument and resigns. He is held to the bond and returns to Tokyo Imperial University (historically his home), and he keeps the seal even from his own government. His silence in Tokyo is the proof Julian needs that a Japanese scientist can carry the seal. It leads directly to Nishina's admission in 1922 and to Hirohito being told (J1). In 1927 both crowns honour him: the man who kept Pacifica's secret from his own emperor because he had given his word, and who argued against it to the end.

**1921–1925: Hillebrand's silence.** He keeps the seal to his death for four reasons:
- his record is safe, dated and witnessed: he of all men knows (helium and nitrogen, 1879–1881) that a claim lives or dies by its record;
- the term guarantees the opening;
- he is a native son among *nā Kahuna*;
- he believes Peirce's argument.

**[APPROVED]** He dies in Honolulu, his birthplace, on 7 February 1925. Julian visits him in his last weeks and tells him the year the seal will be opened.
