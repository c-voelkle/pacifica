## What this CR does

The review found that CR-050 asks Europe to stay blind to fission for twelve years, a period in which history solved the same puzzle in four, with worse conditions. This CR moves the lead off that foundation.

History supplies the better one. Germany knew of fission from January 1939 and never came near a weapon; at Farm Hall in 1945 its physicists first assumed that no other country could have built one, and lectured each other on the technical problems (Badash pp. 174–175). Britain understood that a few kilograms of the separated light isotope would do only in March 1940, and believed it only because a factory was then conceivable. **The facts that mattered were two: that separated light uranium explodes with fast neutrons in kilogram amounts, and that it can be separated by the ton of feed at industrial scale.** Fission itself was never the secret that counted.

So Pacifica's lead in this CR has three layers, each with an honest lifetime:

| Layer | What it is | Expected life | How it is held |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The science | Fission (1908), the chain (1909), delayed neutrons (1910–11), the light isotope (1909–17) | Until about 1920–21 | The seal, for priority; Europe's own errors |
| The two hard facts | Kilogram critical mass of the light isotope; industrial separation works | Europe reaches the first on paper about 1926 and disbelieves the second until the demonstration of 1927 | True signposts that point the wrong way; disbelief |
| The industry | The halls and their feed, the materials, the tooling, the trained people, the next routes | Five to eight years after 1927, and widening while Pacifica keeps moving | Size, bottlenecks, know-how, speed |

The secret is then rebuilt to fit nineteen mostly peaceful years: a smaller valley population, a tighter inner meeting, one real leak that is contained, a European service that looks and does not believe, and a true public account of the 1924 test.

### E2: throttling, not a cut-off

The review showed the cut-off of December 1908 bought almost nothing: Europe could make its own neutron sources from radium and beryllium (Hahn's own institute did in 1938, Krafft notes p. 162), radium was sold cheaply in Europe from 1903 (Shea p. 5), Vienna made its own radium from the 1890s (CR-049), and mesothorium was on the market as "German radium" (Badash p. 168). Stopping every foreign subscription in one month was a loud signal at the worst moment.

**Replacement:** from 1909 the Works lengthens its waiting lists, raises its prices and applies the new safety licensing (N1) to all buyers, home and foreign. Europe's laboratories turn to their own radium–beryllium sources over two or three years, and complain about Pacifican greed, which is the cover R1 wants.

### E3: no hole in the literature

The disappearance of the field's leaders from uranium is itself the Flerov signal (CR-050). Hillebrand's group therefore keeps publishing, through 1908–1920, true work on everything around uranium that does not touch the split: the natural decay series and their branchings, the first isomer (the analogue of Hahn's historical uranium Z of 1921, Berninger pp. 213–219), protactinium's analogue, tracer chemistry and radiation medicine. The quality stays high and the names stay visible. The rule is CR-050's: nothing false, and nothing published about Europe's transuranics either way.

**Dempster's uranium line, 1921 (APPROVED).** Once Bohr infers the rare isotope at the head of the actinium series (E5; the hypothesis was current by 1921, Berninger p. 214), a survey of the isotopes that still leaves out uranium would be a conspicuous gap. So in 1921 Dempster publishes the light uranium line from his own long exposures, a true measurement and an honour for him, answering a question Europe is already asking. Nothing about its fission is published. It costs nothing, because the idea is no longer the secret. The secret is the proof of 1916–17 (Part II) and the separation (M1–M2).

### E4: the Nobel that entrenches the error

Fermi's Nobel Prize of 1938 for "new radioactive elements" made the transuranics settled fact in textbooks and lectures (Weart p. 101). Here the equivalent goes in **1911 to Marie Curie**, historically the year of her second prize. In this timeline she is the second finder of polonium and radium (CR-049), so her historical prize for them does not exist; the prize goes instead to her school's chemistry of the products of neutron bombardment. The laboratory most likely to break the error (Curie and Savitch nearly did in 1938, Weart pp. 105–107) becomes the one most committed to defending it.

**Pacifica's part (APPROVED).** Pacifica co-published the transuranic work of 1902–07 (CR-049). When the Nobel committee sounds out Carquinez in 1911, Hillebrand withdraws his group from consideration "pending verification", which is true. The prize goes to Paris alone, and no Pacifican name stands on an error the Works knows to be one. The corrections column will record the withdrawal at the opening of 1927.

### E5: why Hahn takes until 1920

Krafft's central point is that the Berlin discovery needed a radiochemist, an analytical chemist and a physicist working as equals (Krafft pp. 136–140). Badash's is that Hahn on his own hedged and needed others to say plainly what he had found (Badash pp. 167–168). Hahn's delay rests on that, and on six further reasons, each of which can be shown on the page.

**Can he not find someone to replace Meitner?** He finds collaborators, but no one like her:
- **Otto von Baeyer** (historical, born 1877), the physicist with whom Hahn and Meitner measured beta spectra (Shea p. 12), works with him from 1910. He is a careful measurer. Heisenberg's distinction fits: Meitner asked "why", Hahn asked "what" (Krafft p. 139). Von Baeyer asks "what" too.
- **The physicists who ask "why" are elsewhere.** Haas's quantum atom of 1907 and Bohr's extension of it (CR-050 K1) make atomic spectra the most exciting problem in Europe, and nuclear chemistry is a backwater. Historically Rutherford proposed in 1913 to leave nuclear theory to the next generation (Stuewer p. 22). Here, having published his model in 1912, he treats the structure of the nucleus as settled, and what is left looks like detail.
- **Meitner was rare.** In Berlin she was barred from the upstairs laboratories, refused as an author because she was a woman, and given her first paid post only in 1912 (Shea pp. 10–11). People of her kind are drawn to Pacifica, where the examination has been open to women since 1859.
- **The war takes the next generation:** Moseley killed, Chadwick interned, Geiger at the front (Stuewer p. 22).

**The reasons, in order:**
1. **1908–1910: the error looks right.** Hahn and Meitner are busy with beta spectra and the thorium series (Shea pp. 11–12). The transuranic chains of 1902–1907 are accepted on both sides of the world.
2. **1910: Meitner leaves** (CR-050). Hahn loses his questioner.
3. **1910–1914: Pacifica's pricing keeps him in industry.** His research is supported through the Knöfler company, which markets his mesothorium as "German radium" (Badash p. 168). When the Works' throttling (E2) and the radium trust (CR-050 R1) push radium's price up, Europe's demand for mesothorium rises with it, and so does Hahn's commercial chemistry. Pacifica does nothing false: it charges what it likes, and the best radiochemist in Germany spends his years making a cheaper radium.
4. **1911: the prize** (E4). The transuranics carry a Nobel Prize, and doubting them means doubting Paris.
5. **1912: the institute and the law.** Hahn moves into the new Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry and builds his department (Shea p. 13). In the same year Rutherford's satellite model (E6) makes splitting look physically impossible. Hahn, by his own account no theorist, defers to physics: in 1938 he still called a split "contrary to all previous laws of nuclear physics" before softening "laws" to "experience" (Weart pp. 112–113).
6. **1914–1918: the war** (table above).
7. **1919: the trigger.** Back in Berlin, Hahn does what he historically did in 1921: he goes over his old measurements and finds small inconsistencies he had ignored (the pattern in Berninger p. 214). **Friedrich Paneth** (historical, born 1887), formerly of the Vienna Radium Institute, is now his analytical partner and brings Vienna's sources. *Historically Paneth was in Hamburg from 1919 and in Berlin from 1922; here he joins Hahn in 1919.* Paneth's separations make the barium undeniable in 1920.
8. **1920–1921: the reading.** **Niels Bohr** interprets by letter, as Hahn consulted him in November 1938 (Krafft pp. 137–138). His reading abandons Rutherford's model and carries him in months to the rare isotope. That is acceptable now that the lead does not rest on it.

### E6: Europe's equivalent of Gamow's prohibition — Rutherford's satellite model

Weart's finding is that what kept physicists of the 1930s from fission was a theory. Gamow's barrier theory of alpha decay (1928) said that nothing much heavier than an alpha particle could escape a nucleus (Weart p. 102). Europe before quantum mechanics needs its own prohibition, and history supplies one in Rutherford.
- From the **uranium paradox**, Rutherford built a "satellite" model of the nucleus in the 1920s. The paradox: fast alpha particles bounce off uranium in the normal way, yet uranium emits slower ones. The model: an indivisible core surrounded by neutral satellites that break up only once they have left. He clung to it even after Gamow (Stuewer pp. 28–29).
- The nuclear-electron picture behind it "pervaded physics during the 1920s", textbooks included (Stuewer p. 30).

**Proposal.**
- Rutherford, outside the wall since about 1909–10 (CR-050 W2), finds the paradox about 1911 with Pacifica's strong thorium sources and publishes the satellite model in 1912.
- It explains everything then known. Alpha and beta emission are the loss of outer satellites, and the core does not divide.
- Coming from the most famous experimentalist in the world, it becomes Europe's prohibition and enters the textbooks within a few years. It reaches Hahn through Paneth, who teaches it in his lectures at Prague and Hamburg. Paneth is thus both the brake and, in 1920, the analyst whose separations break it.
- **The inner circle knows it is wrong and lets it stand** (E3's rule: nothing published about it either way). The cost goes on the page. In 1912 Meitner, newly admitted, asks at a challenge evening to warn him privately, and Hillebrand refuses.
- It is the same man who later calls power from fusion reactions moonshine (CR-050). He is wrong twice, sincerely, as he historically was about his model (Stuewer p. 29). Both errors are his own, and both serve as signposts (T1).

### Millikan's two faces

Kargon shows a public evangelist: in 1919–1923 he told audiences that controlling atomic energy would be worth "a billion" dollars and bring "a new world for man", and he planned to "bust up" atoms with high voltage (Kargon pp. 75–77). Inside the wall that temperament is the largest leak risk; outside it is the best signpost. **Proposal:** Millikan speaks publicly, often and sincerely about cosmic rays and atom-building in the stars, the one subject on which his enthusiasm points away from the halls. The bond and the Nobel of 1923 (CR-050 S4) hold the rest.

## T2: the memo nobody funds (APPROVED)

The Frisch–Peierls memorandum of March 1940 showed in three pages that a few kilograms of the light isotope would explode. Europe gets its equivalent here, on paper, before the demonstration:

- **1924–26:** asked by Tizard after the Black Rock statement (S6), and once early quantum mechanics (CR-050 K1) allows a fast-neutron estimate, **Ralph Fowler** (historical, born 1889; APPROVED) calculates that a mass of separated light uranium in the range of kilograms to tens of kilograms would explode. Fowler is a Cambridge theorist; he was wounded at Gallipoli and worked on anti-aircraft ballistics with A. V. Hill in the war *(outside the texts)*. Historically he married Rutherford's daughter Eileen in 1921. With Rutherford in Pacifica from about 1909–10 rather than in England, the marriage does not happen (author).
- The memo also says what the separation would cost: a plant "larger than any chemical works in the Empire", on power Britain does not have to spare.
- **1926:** the Treasury under Churchill (Chancellor 1924–29, canon CT-WINSTON-CHURCHILL) declines, on the advice of **Frederick Lindemann** (historical, born 1886; Oxford's professor of experimental philosophy from 1919, Churchill's friend from 1921) that the factory is impossible in peacetime and probably anywhere, and that the Black Rock shot was chemical or theatre. Historically Lindemann advised Churchill in August 1939 that atomic explosives were no near danger *(outside the texts)*.
- **1927:** Churchill, who in June is shown one sheet of the Petrograd account (CR-046), sees the column of water in the newsreels in the autumn. The memo is in his own files.

This keeps faith with the review: Europe reaches the first hard fact on paper by 1926, which is believable, and fails on the second, which is what the demonstration proves.

## M1–M6: the industrial moat

### M1: combined separation

Norris shows that the electromagnetic plant alone was too slow: Y-12's first 200 g came a year after construction began, about 8 kg by Christmas 1944, and Little Boy's 64 kg existed only because Y-12 was fed with uranium already enriched by thermal diffusion (S-50) and gaseous diffusion (K-25) (Norris pp. 364–367). S-50 was simple: columns of concentric pipes, steam on one side and cooling water on the other; a third of it was running 66 days after ground was broken, and its product was only "slightly enriched" but gave "a significant increase in efficiency" as feed (pp. 364–367): "Add a third method and combine them" (p. 365).

**Proposal:**
- **Ralston College's rival route under the Compact of the Tuolumne (CR-050-R1 B4 clause 4) is thermal diffusion.** The effect is old physics (Ludwig 1856, Soret 1879, *outside the texts*). It needs a uranium compound that is liquid or gaseous at workable temperatures; a volatile uranium fluoride (historically first made by Otto Ruff in Germany about 1909, *outside the texts*) is within reach of Hillebrand's furnace chemistry and Moissan's fluorine of 1886.
- **1918–1920:** the contest with declared terms (CR-050-R1 B4 clause 5). Ralston College's columns cannot reach weapon purity alone; the judges give the contest to the halls in 1920.
- **1921:** Langmuir, as new director, reads Ralston College's report and puts the columns in front of the halls. The halls' output rises by perhaps a fifth to a half, as S-50's did for Y-12; the rest of "going big" is the size of the plant (M2). Millikan's college loses the contest and makes the bomb possible, and the sealed roll records both (clause 6). This is the Compact paying off as drama: the rival keeps the champion honest and then saves it.

### M2: build big (APPROVED direction; figures MODEL)

A gun device of CR-049's kind needs on the order of 40–60 kg of the light isotope (Conant's 1944 estimate for 10–20 kilotons, Norris p. 363; Little Boy used about 64 kg). The author's direction is to build big, because Pacifica can afford it.

**Scale.**
- **Size, not better units.** The halls' performance per unit stays at Y-12's level or below, with 1920s vacuum and valves. "Going big" comes from the size of the plant: first-stage halls rising to about two and a half to three times Y-12's electromagnetic plant by 1925–26 (Y-12 was some two hundred buildings on 825 acres, Norris p. 202).
- **A thermal-diffusion feed plant** several times the size of S-50, raising the halls' output by a fifth to a half (M1). Its steam needs a powerhouse of its own, as S-50 used K-25's (Norris pp. 206, 365).
- **A second stage of smaller halls**, as Y-12's Beta stage finished the Alpha product (Norris p. 202). Weapon-grade material exists only once the second stage runs.
- **Power (checked, APPROVED; figures MODEL).** At three times Y-12 the halls draw 600–800 MW by 1925. On the current canon population (CR-020 B10–B12, CR-033) that is about a twentieth of Pacifica's generating capacity (3–9 per cent) and roughly a tenth of its electricity (7–16 per cent), because the halls run day and night while the rest of the grid does not. Y-12 had 200 MW earmarked by 1945 *(outside the texts)*; Groves asked for at least 100 MW for Hanford (Norris p. 214). This is possible only because Pacifica electrified early (ledger L4, K9). Against the historical West's capacity the same plant would take more than a third of the region's electricity, which is not credible. The halls do not hide in the electrochemical district: they are its largest load, and the Office's tables say so. The full check is `outputs/CR-051/power-check.md`.
  - **1919–1921: the war's nitrogen power.** Pacifica's arc nitrogen plants (ledger K11), built to free it from Chilean nitrate and to supply Entente munitions (CR-020 B9), draw several hundred megawatts by 1918. The explosives demand ends with the armistice and the Russian campaign in 1919, freeing some 200–300 MW of Sierra hydropower, already built and wired for heavy, steady loads, in the year the first halls are built. The halls stand where the arc furnaces stood, which is why they are in the electrochemical district at all.
  - **1920–1926: the Works' own rivers.** A hydro company of the Works (as at Rock Island, S7) builds about 400–500 MW of new plant on the Pit and the upper Feather, finished by 1925–26. Historically PG&E built Pit 1 (1922) and Caribou (1921) there *(outside the texts)*. The cost is on the order of $100m for dams and lines **[MODEL]**, affordable, but every dam is public. Under CR-033 nothing is built without the consent of the Pit River peoples (Achumawi and Atsugewi) and the Maidu, on terms like S8's **[OPEN]**.
  - **No second supply.** Unlike a pile, a hall that stops loses product, not safety.
  - **The feed plant** burns oil for its steam, perhaps 0.4–0.75 million tonnes a year **[MODEL]**, a few per cent of California's output at most. It appears in the Office's fuel tables.

### M3: the windings

In 1942 silver replaced copper because copper was a scarce war metal; the Treasury lent 14,700 tons, and every ounce was recovered after the war (Norris pp. 202–203). In this timeline the halls' windings are ordered in 1917–1918, when Pacifica is fighting in Russia and copper is a war metal, so the reason holds. The quantity does not: Norris's 14,700 short tons (about 13,300 tonnes) is nearly twice the Comstock's entire historical silver output of about 7,000 tonnes *(outside the texts)*.

**Proposal:** the Mint lends the silver it holds in 1918 for the first two halls, a few thousand tons. The later halls are wound in copper bought cheaply in the postwar slump *(outside the texts)*. In 1923 the first halls are rewound in copper and the silver goes back to the Mint, every bar weighed: a small scene for the Chief, and a line in the Mint's annual report that no one reads.

### M4: the third route

Gaseous diffusion through porous barriers rests on Graham's law of the 1840s. Its difficulty is the barrier, which defeated Kellex for a year (Norris p. 205). **Proposal:** a gaseous-diffusion pilot runs in the inner circle about 1926, and a plant is built 1930–32. It is not needed for 1927. It is the next generation, and it means that when Britain starts copying the halls after 1927 it is copying the method Pacifica is about to abandon, as the calutrons were abandoned after 1946 *(outside the texts)*.

### M5: the bottleneck moat

Every part of Oak Ridge's trouble was a bottleneck someone had to solve: the magnets, the vacuum, the recovery of splattered material (more than 90 per cent of the light isotope had to be scrubbed out of the machines, Norris p. 205), the feed chemistry. Pacifica turns each into a lead that has nothing to do with secrecy:
- **Graphite:** petroleum coke and electric furnaces (CR-050 X6), tested batch by batch.
- **Vacuum:** Langmuir's condensation pump (1916) and the pump oils, made only by the Works' suppliers.
- **High voltage:** the hard valves (Langmuir, c. 1908, CR-050) and the regulating gear from Tesla's and Steinmetz's works.
- **Fluorine and uranium chemistry:** Hillebrand's furnace and recovery plants.
- **Beryllium**, for sources and reflectors.
- **The magnet works**, with the only shops able to wind coils of that size.
- **The people:** the examined operators of the halls (CR-050 M1), the recovery chemists and the vacuum workshops. What a rival needs most after 1927 is not a fact but ten years of practice.

Exports of these goods are restricted as commercial property of a state champion (CR-039), which is true.

### M6: keep moving

After 1927 the lead is defended by speed, not by the old secret: gaseous diffusion (1930–32), the breeding line (CR-050 X2), the Pacifican and Tokyo stations (CR-050 T7), and plasma work from the halls' ion sources (CR-050 X2). Each year Pacifica operates, its absolute lead in practice grows even as the relative lead in knowledge shrinks.

## C1–C5: the people who build it

CR-050 has a scientific director (Langmuir), two political keys and no one who builds. Groves's own lesson was that the industrial project needed one hard-driving builder with authority over contractors and a habit of setting impossible dates (Norris pp. 192–206). He also needed an ore man (Sengier, the Murray Hill office) and an ordnance officer (Parsons).

### C1: the Chief (APPROVED)

**Two phases of leadership (APPROVED, author, fifth round).** Groves was not the American programme's first leader. Fission research ran for three years under scientists and a civilian committee (Bush, Conant, the S-1 section; Norris pp. 165–186). Groves was appointed in September 1942, when the work had to become factories, and Fermi's first pile went critical three months later without his having chosen it. Pacifica follows the same pattern:

In the first phase the programme is a sealed laboratory inside a state champion, with the Polytechnic's key on the seal. Its military side is security: the guardhouse, the post office and the bonds, run by a junior officer of the Polytechnic's staff **[no name needed]**. It becomes a military industrial programme in 1917, when the war, the isotope proof and the halls arrive together. The Chief's arrival is Groves's scene: a stranger in uniform among people who have kept a secret for eight years without him.

**Distinction: the siege of Tsingtao, autumn 1914 (APPROVED).** It is already canon: Pacifica declares war on Germany on 23 August 1914, the day Japan does, and takes Tsingtao and the German islands with its partners (CR-040, §28). Historically Tsingtao fell to a methodical engineers' siege. Over two months the attackers landed men in rain-flooded country, rebuilt the railway inland, dug parallels and saps toward the German redoubts, and brought up heavy siege guns *(outside the texts)*. The Chief commands the Pacifican engineers in the combined siege. He rebuilds the Shantung railway behind the lines, puts the heavy guns and their ammunition forward across flooded ground on time, and pushes the saps toward the redoubts. The siege also puts him beside Japanese officers in a joint operation, a qualification the Works needs from 1922, when Nishina enters the wall (CR-050 J1). From 1915 to 1917 he runs the anchorage and works Pacifica holds at Tsingtao under occupation, the lease of canon §30 coming only with the settlement of 1919; in 1917 he is called home.

**The Chihuahua alternative (OPEN).** Whether Villa would attack Pacifica, and why, is undetermined (author). C5 sets out the question for the military CR.

**Identity: William Luther Sibert (APPROVED).** Historical: born 1860 at Gadsden, Alabama; West Point 1884, Corps of Engineers; builder of the Gatun locks and dam on the Panama Canal (1907–14); in 1914 a member of the American Red Cross's board of engineers on the Huai River in China; commander of the 1st Division in France in 1917, relieved by Pershing late that year as insufficiently aggressive; director of the Chemical Warfare Service, 1918–20; died 1935 *(outside the texts; checked in review)*.

*In this timeline.*
- **Youth.** Born in Alabama, a Confederate boy after 1861, he takes the Polytechnic's examination about 1879 and serves his time in the engineer corps (CR-034 A20). He is one of the examined few from the South, the talent flow canon already has (CR-048 A3). **[APPROVED]** His road runs through the school of Pacifica's Birmingham works, sixty miles from Gadsden (canon treaty rights): the works' school examines local boys for the Polytechnic, and he is one of its first. **[To align at integration]** Canon gives the works no founding date; they must exist by the late 1870s for this route (Birmingham itself was founded in 1871, *outside the texts*).
- **The canal.** He joins the regency's canal works in 1889, at twenty-nine, and commands its Atlantic division from about 1895, building the locks and the great dam of the lock-and-lake canal, opened in 1900 (canon). Historically he took over the same works at Gatun at forty-seven, eighteen years later. It is the largest construction job of its age, and in this timeline it is his.
- **China and Tsingtao.** In the summer of 1914 he is in China on a Huai River conservancy survey, Pacifican engineering and credit for a Chinese-owned undertaking as canon's China policy has it. The Huai basin borders Shantung, so when war comes in August he is the senior Pacifican engineer within reach of Tsingtao and becomes the siege's chief engineer.
- **The Works, 1917–1927.** Called home at fifty-six or fifty-seven, he builds the halls, the feed plant, the Columbia station and the test site. He retires after the demonstration of 1927, at sixty-seven.

*Why him.*
- **The record.** No one in Pacifica has built anything larger or kept a larger site working to schedule. Groves's qualification was the Pentagon; Sibert's is the canal.
- **The temperament, and why it is better than Groves's.** Historically Sibert was quiet, methodical and unflashy; Pershing removed him for lacking drive. Here that is the Chief's virtue. Groves owed Hanford's survival to DuPont's insistence on 504 extra tubes against the physicists (Norris pp. 225–226), and he later cut Hanford's cooling times to save weeks (pp. 368–369). Sibert is DuPont, not the speed-up. He keeps the margins: the extra capacity in the halls, the holding ponds, the full cooling times promised to the Wanapum (S8). The pressure to cut them comes from the 1927 deadline, from Tesla and from Szilard. The scene in which he refuses Julian a month by saying "then the device is a month late" is the one Groves never played.
- **The blind spot remains (L1).** A builder who has never failed to finish thinks in fixed quantities: corner the ore and the problem is closed. Hoover and Szilard tell him otherwise.
- **The echo.** Historically he ran America's chemical warfare in 1918–20. Here he does not; the Haber thread (canon S10) and Hahn's gas war (Badash pp. 169–170) are Europe's, and Sibert's war is the one Pacifica fights in silence.

*Profile kept from the earlier draft:* honest to the last bar of silver (M3); his conflict with Langmuir in the halls is Groves's with Lawrence ("my reputation is made; yours is at stake", Norris p. 205), with the roles of patience and haste reversed.

**Alternatives considered:** Funston (distinguished in the Philippines; dies February 1917); Goethals (the canal, no combat); Patton (canon, cavalry, needed in Russia); one leader from 1909 with a Manchurian distinction (set aside by the author's choice of two phases).

## S1–S8: the secret, rebuilt for nineteen years

### S1: who lives in the valley

Norris records that when Groves let half a dozen people at the Met Lab be told about plutonium's spontaneous fission, "within half an hour the whole lab knew" (p. 362). Two circles cannot share a valley three miles long with one meeting hall, one tram, one school and one clinic.

**Proposal:**
- The **valley holds the inner circle** and the staff it needs: the families, the installation's crew, the workshops, the clinic, the school, the post office and the Me-Wuk wardens (CR-050-R1 B3, unchanged).
- The **outer college** (CR-050 S3) works at Carquinez and on Ralston College's campus. Admission still runs through it; a recruit admitted to the inner circle moves to the valley.
- The **Tuolumne Station** keeps its public face (CR-050-R1 B2): its published work is done by inner members under the station's name, so prominent names stay visible.

### S2: the inner meeting

Groves blamed the Los Alamos colloquia, set up at Condon's urging, for making staff careless and letting a machinist, Greenglass, learn what he passed to the Russians (Norris pp. 255–256). The challenge evening is that colloquium.

**Proposal:**
- The valley's challenge evenings are for members of the inner circle only; the outer college keeps its own on the campuses.
- **Instrument makers and machinists who build to inner drawings sign the inner bond**, not the staff bond. The Greenglass position is inside the circle and under its discipline.
- Compartments inside the inner circle stay light, as Oppenheimer's did, because the work needs talk (CR-050 G2). The protection is the bond, the post and the valley, not ignorance among colleagues.

### S3: the valley's size and its attrition

Los Alamos grew from a few hundred in spring 1943 to 3,500 in January 1944 and 8,200 by the end of 1945; every estimate was low, and children kept being born (Norris p. 246). The floor of Hetch Hetchy is about a square mile.

**Cap (MODEL):** about 150 inner scientists and engineers by 1927; with families, staff, the school and the clinic, about 1,000–1,200 residents. Housing on the north bench and Ralston College's row (CR-050-R1 B6). Growth beyond the cap goes to the campuses or the halls' district, never into the valley.

**The Columbia station (APPROVED).** The valley is not the only secret.
- **Land.** The towns of White Bluffs and Hanford and their orchards are bought out in 1919–20 at prices fixed by outside appraisers, with time to take in the harvest. Groves's buyers fought farmers over low appraisals and uncut crops (Norris p. 217); the Works pays above the appraisal and lets the crop come in, because a grievance is a leak.
- **Construction.** Thousands of building workers in 1920–23 know they are building pile buildings and a chemical plant for "the Works' isotopes". That is true and incomplete, as at Hanford, where tens of thousands did not know what they built (Norris p. 257).
- **Staff.** The operating staff live in a company town on the river, under the staff bond. The chemists of element 94 are inner-circle members under the inner bond, and they rotate with the valley.

**The attrition register.** The secret must survive people. Rules, to be shown on the page through a few cases:

| Event | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| Death of a member | Papers to the custodian; the family keeps the house for a year; the funeral is a master's leave-taking (CR-049 E4) |
| Marriage to an outsider | The spouse lives in the valley and signs the staff bond, or the member returns to the outer college |
| Children | Told nothing; schooled in the valley to fourteen; at the Polytechnic's examinations they are ordinary candidates. The first valley-born children (from 1911) are teenagers by the mid-1920s and know only that their parents "work at the Station" |
| Resignation | The bond runs to the end of the term (Nagaoka, 1920) |
| Ralston College's exit right | Never invoked; threatened once, in Millikan's claim of 1920 (CR-050-R1 B4 clause 10) |
| Foreign families abroad | Letters through the Works' post office, read (CR-050 S3) |

### S4: the contained leak — the Tribune's Sierra story, 1922 (APPROVED)

A secret never tested reads as the author's say-so.

- **Spring 1922:** a machinist from the valley's workshops, drinking in Groveland, tells a stranger that the Station "is making something that will blow a battleship out of the water". The stranger sells the story east. The *Chicago Tribune*, canon's revanchist voice (CR-048 R12), prints it under a large headline, with the halls' power draw taken from the Office's published tables and the Works' ore buying from the trade press: four years after the war, the Sierra's electrochemical load is higher than at the height of the munitions effort, and the Works is building dams on two rivers (M2). Every figure is true.
- **Pacifica says nothing.** It issues no denial, because it may not lie. Hearst's papers (Hearst sits among the Five) do not take the story up.
- **Europe dismisses it.** It comes from the paper that also blames Pacifica for the rump's troubles (CR-048 A5). Rutherford, asked by a London journalist, says honestly that he knows of no such thing, and that a bomb of the kind described would need a factory no country could build. Natural uranium cannot explode (T1).
- **The machinist** is found through the post office and the guardhouse log. He is tried before the Polytechnic estate's court under the bond, loses his pension rights, and serves out the term on full pay at a remote station of the Works without leave. Pacifica is not a gentle state, but it does not make men disappear.
- **The cost:** the story goes into a British file (S5) and into Szilard's argument, two years later, that the seal's term must end.

### S5: Europe looks (APPROVED)

Alsos's list of signs of a foreign programme was uranium, pure graphite, heavy water and beryllium, plants of certain sizes, and radioactivity in river water downstream of a pile (Norris p. 286). After 1920 every one of them points at Pacifica. CR-050 S4's tests are all internal; this adds the outside.

- **1922:** after the Tribune story, the Admiralty asks the Secret Intelligence Service (under Mansfield Cumming until 1923, then Hugh Sinclair, *outside the texts*) for what is known: the radium trust's purchases, the halls' power, the Works' recruiting, a closed valley in the Sierra.
- **Two friends, two readings.** Lindemann and Tizard are close friends in the 1920s; their famous quarrel belongs to the 1930s *(outside the texts)*. They disagree here as friends do.
- **Lindemann's reading:** radium, electrochemistry and a rich state's taste for secrecy. Aluminium, carbide and nitrogen: Pacifica is merely modernizing.
- **Tizard's reading:** Henry Tizard (historical, born 1885; at the Department of Scientific and Industrial Research from 1920, *outside the texts*) reads the Office's tables: the nitrogen output fell after the war and the power did not, so the power went to something else. He keeps the file open and asks for river-water samples below the Tuolumne. The samples taken in 1923 show nothing, because the research installation is small and its effluent held (S7). The file closes, and reopens in May 1924 (S6).
- **Germany and the Union:** the Kaiser Wilhelm Society reads the Tribune and laughs; the Union's revanchists believe it and are not believed.
- **The Columbia station:** what any service can learn there is that the Works runs a large isotope station beside a new aluminium smelter, with a powerhouse and a dam upriver. All of it is true, and all of it fits the Works' public business.
- **What Europe knows on the morning of the demonstration:** that fission gives power; that a bomb needs separated light uranium in kilograms (one memo, filed); that separation in quantity is impossible (most physicists); that Pacifica has unusual electrochemical works and a radium monopoly. Disbelief does the rest, as at Farm Hall (Badash pp. 174–175).

### S6: the 1924 test, in true words (APPROVED)

Trinity needed an evacuation force for 450 people, a governor warned about martial law, an officer in the AP's office and a false press release about an exploded ammunition magazine (Norris pp. 401–402, 406–407). Pacifica may not lie.

- **Before:** the Northern Paiute and Shoshone camps and the ranches downwind are warned and paid to move for the month (CR-050-R1 B6), told truthfully that "the Works will test an explosive of very great power, and dust from it may be harmful".
- **After, the same morning:** the Works and the General Staff publish one sentence: *"This morning the Light and Matter Works tested an explosive of a new kind and very great power on the Black Rock Desert, far from any settlement; the area is closed until further notice."* Every word is true.
- **Seen and heard:** the flash and the shock reach Gerlach, the Western Pacific's trains and the ranches; the seismographs at Berkeley and Lick record it *(outside the texts)*. The statement covers them.
- **Read abroad.** Europe cannot simply read it as chemical. It has had fission since 1920, the neutron count since 1921, the Tribune's story since 1922, and the announcement comes from an atomic firm.
  - **Tizard reopens his file** and asks Cambridge what such a device would need. That question is the trigger for Fowler's memo (T2).
  - **Lindemann answers** that the separation factory is impossible. The shot must therefore have been a very large chemical charge, or a piece of theatre to frighten London in the year of the debt talks.
  - **Disbelief therefore rests where P1 puts it: on the second hard fact.** Europe can imagine the bomb; it cannot imagine the factory.
  - The Tribune claims vindication and is again not believed.

### S7: siting (APPROVED)

Hanford was chosen for a large river, power, little rain and few people; three California sites failed the criteria (Norris pp. 215–216). The pilot pile was moved from the Argonne forest near Chicago to Oak Ridge for safety (p. 224); the separation plants stood on a plateau five miles from the river (p. 226). When cooling times were cut, iodine-131 went into the air (pp. 368–369).

**Proposal.** The valley keeps only the low-power research installation of 1913 (CR-050 X6), with its effluent held and not released to the Tuolumne. The production piles of the 1920s, the chemistry of element 94 and the breeder (CR-050 X2) go to **the Columbia at White Bluffs, the reach Groves's men chose for Hanford in December 1942**. The Northwest has been Pacifican since the 1860s (canon).

**Why there.**
- **Groves's own criteria still hold in 1919–20.** The Columbia offers plentiful pure water, little rain, a mild winter and very few people to move (Norris pp. 215–216). Of the sites Matthias's team surveyed, only Hanford met them all; the three in California failed.
- **The river is the largest in Pacifica.** Its flow dilutes what the cooling water carries, so Alsos's method of sampling river water downstream (Norris p. 286) tells a foreign service little. The river is also deep inside Pacifica, where no foreign scientist can sample it unnoticed.
- **It separates the sites.** It lies some six hundred miles from Hetch Hetchy and farther from the halls. Separated sites were themselves part of Groves's security: the couriers who carried plutonium from Hanford often did not know what they carried or where it went (Norris p. 371).
- **Rail.** The Northern Pacific's line through Pasco is close *(outside the texts)*.
- **Hazard has room.** The separation plant can stand, as Hanford's did, on a plateau miles from the river (Norris p. 226).

**What it costs.**
- **Consent.** The Wanapum live at Priest Rapids on this reach, and the Yakama and other peoples fish the river *(outside the texts)*. Under CR-033 nothing is taken without consent. The terms are in S8.
- **The salmon.** Historically Hanford's discharges made the river radioactive, and shortened cooling sent iodine-131 into the air (Norris pp. 368–369). Pacifica's rule is to keep the full cooling times and the holding ponds even under pressure. This is a promise to the Wanapum, written into the compact (S8). The pressure to break it comes in 1926–27 from the deadline, and the Chief refuses (C1).

**Power (APPROVED).**

*What is needed.* The production piles of the 1920s make heat but no useful electricity: their cooling water leaves too cool to raise steam *(outside the texts)*. The station needs electricity for:
- pumping a large volume of river water through the piles without interruption;
- the separation plant;
- the workshops and the town.

That comes to some tens of megawatts **[MODEL]**. The cooling pumps must never stop, because losing cooling water in a running pile is the worst accident the station can have. So the station needs **two independent supplies**, as Groves gave K-25 its own steam plant rather than rely on the Tennessee Valley Authority, whose power "might be subject to interruption from natural causes or sabotage" (Norris p. 206).

*Supply 1: a dam at Rock Island, on the main Columbia about twelve miles below Wenatchee,* about eighty river miles upstream of the station. Historically this was the first dam on the river's main stem, built 1930–33 *(outside the texts)*; here a hydro company of the Works builds it in 1920–23.
- **Its public purpose is true:** power for Wenatchee's orchards and for irrigation pumping in the Columbia Basin, and an **aluminium works at Wenatchee**, a real smelter that takes most of the output.
- **The station's draw hides inside a real industrial load**, as the halls stand openly in the Sierra's electrochemical district (CR-050 M1, M2). The station's tens of megawatts are small beside a smelter; the halls' hundreds are not, and are published as the district's largest load. The Office of Public Measures' tables show a smelter, which is true.
- **A transmission line of about eighty miles** runs down the river to White Bluffs.
- **Consent and fish:** the dam needs the consent of the peoples of that reach (the Wenatchi and the Columbia people, *outside the texts*) and fish ladders for the salmon (S8).

*Supply 2: a steam plant at the station* burning Washington coal from Roslyn in the Cascades, which comes by the Northern Pacific *(outside the texts)*. It carries the pumps alone if the line or the dam fails.

*Not at Priest Rapids:* a dam there would drown the Wanapum's village. *Not yet Grand Coulee:* a dam on that scale is not needed before 1932.

**Public face.** "The Works' Columbia station" publishes medical and tracer isotopes from its piles, which is true and is more true noise (CR-050 N1).

### S8: the Columbia compacts (APPROVED)

The Me-Wuk compact of 1909–10 (CR-050-R1 B3) and the Ute lease (CR-033) set the pattern:
- lease, not purchase;
- a fee every year;
- rights added, not only taken;
- work with honour;
- the clinic;
- the same bond for everyone inside;
- nothing to tell.

The Columbia adds a problem the valley did not have. Here the river itself is what the station uses and what the peoples live from, and the danger to it is real (Norris pp. 225–226, 368–369). The compact therefore needs a promise that can be measured and a remedy with teeth. Three instruments, 1919–1920:

**A. The White Bluffs compact, with the Wanapum** (who live at Priest Rapids, just above the station; *outside the texts*).
1. **Lease, not purchase.** The Works buys the private homesteads on the terraces outright and leases the Wanapum's rights of use over the station's ground until 1947, the term of the sealed record (L2), renewable. A fee is paid every year to the band; title stays theirs.
2. **Their village is untouched.** No dam at Priest Rapids and no works within sight of the village, written into the compact (S7).
3. **The river stays open.** The fence runs along the terraces, not the water. Fishing from the river goes on through the station's reach, and two named landing places outside the inner fence stay open in the fishing seasons. Landing elsewhere on the station's bank is forbidden.
4. **The sacred places are protected.** No works, roads or vaults on Gable Mountain, Gable Butte or Rattlesnake Mountain, and access for ceremonies *(outside the texts)*. Historically Hanford stored its plutonium in vaults at Gable Mountain (Norris p. 371); here the compact forbids it.
5. **The measured promise.** Cooling water returns to the river only after the holding ponds, and the full cooling times are kept (S7). The Office of Public Measures takes the river's measurements below the station and the salmon counts at the landing places each month, in the presence of a Wanapum observer, and publishes them every year. Publishing is consistent with the station's public face as a maker of medical isotopes (S7), and it is true.
6. **The remedy.** If the published counts fall below the baseline of 1919–1920 for three years running, or the station breaks its discharge limit, the band may end the lease on a year's notice, with compensation, and the Works must cut the piles' output until the limit holds. This is the clause the Chief cites in 1926–27 when he refuses to shorten the cooling times (C1).
7. **Work with honour.** Wanapum men who wish to are hired as river wardens. They patrol the station's reach by boat and are paid at the Works' rates. They are the first to meet a stranger on the water. Smohalla's teaching forbids ploughing and digging, though Wanapum have long taken seasonal wage work *(outside the texts)*; the compact never asks them to dig or build, and the warden's post is the kind of work it allows.
8. **The clinic** serves the band's families.
9. **The same bond.** Wardens sign the staff bond, no more and no less. No Wanapum enters a pile building or the separation plant; what they see is what the station publishes.

**B. The river accord, with the treaty fishing peoples** (the Yakama, and the Umatilla and Nez Perce who fish the reach; *outside the texts*).
1. Their fishing rights at their usual places on the river are confirmed in writing by the Works.
2. The same monthly measurements and salmon counts (A5) are sent to their councils.
3. A share of the White Bluffs fee, in proportion to use.
4. Their councils sit with the Wanapum on the panel that judges the remedy (A6), with the Office of Public Measures in the chair.

**C. The Rock Island compact, with the Wenatchi and the Columbia people** (the reach of the dam, *outside the texts*).
1. **Lease of the dam site and the reach it raises**, until 1947, renewable.
2. **A royalty on every kilowatt-hour** the dam sells, paid to the peoples of the reach. The Republic founded its school on a royalty on gold (CR-034); the Columbia's peoples are paid the same way for their river.
3. **Electricity at cost** for their villages.
4. **Fish ladders** built and kept by the Works, with passage counts published every year. Historically the Rock Island Dam was built with ladders *(outside the texts)*.
5. **A replacement fishing site** for any site the pool drowns, chosen by the peoples' councils.
6. **Work with honour and the clinic**, on the same terms as A7–A9. The dam's workers know nothing of the station; the dam is what it appears to be, a power plant for a smelter, orchards and pumps.

**Why it holds.** It holds for the same reasons as the Me-Wuk compact. There is nothing to tell. The peoples' interest is the station's survival, because the lease, the royalty, the wages, the clinic and the guaranteed fishing all end if it closes. The wardens on the water are the station's alarm. The new element is the measured promise. If the station harms the river, the peoples can close it, and everyone inside knows that. It is the strongest reason the margins hold.

**[OPEN]** The bands' leaders in 1919–20, names from the archives, not invented, as with the Me-Wuk (CR-050-R1 B6).

## L1–L3: securing the lead

### L1: ore as delay and leverage

Groves believed control of the ore could keep the bomb American; the effort "ran aground on the fact that uranium was not scarce in nature" (Norris p. 326). He predicted "ten, twenty or even sixty years" for a Soviet bomb; it took four, and once a search was ordered "major deposits were discovered within a short time" (p. 475). He later conceded that low-grade ores defeat control of raw materials (p. 480); a sixth of his own supply came from vanadium mill tailings (p. 327). The trust that ran his ore diplomacy bypassed the State Department, and still leaked through Maclean (pp. 339, 341).

**Proposal:**
- The ore programme of CR-049 and CR-050 R1 stands. Its purpose in canon is **time and leverage**: it slows Europe's first programmes after 1920 by a few years and gives Pacifica the stockpile its halls need.
- **The Chief believes more:** in 1926 he tells the keys that with Joachimsthal, Shinkolobwe and Fergana in hand no one will build a device for twenty years. **Szilard answers: five, once they know it works.** Hoover, who has found ore on four continents, backs Szilard: Saxony, Cornwall, Portugal and the low-grade ores of every continent exist *(outside the texts)*. All three are on record in the sealed roll; the opening of the second part in 1947 will show who was right.
- **The foreign-office seam.** The Joachimsthal rider, the Shinkolobwe transfer and the Fergana concession are negotiated by the Works through the estates (CR-050 S2). Pacifica's foreign ministry is told the truth it needs: that the Works buys radium and uranium for industry. At the opening in 1927 the foreign minister learns the rest, and has Acheson's line ("you set up your own State Department", Norris p. 341) for a scene.

### L2: the opening of 1927 in two parts

The Smyth Report was meant to draw a line, but "some felt that the report provided too much detail, with useful clues and shortcuts about what worked and what did not … Among the first readers were the Russians" (Norris p. 436). CR-050 G5 publishes the 1908 and 1909 papers in facsimile with a roll of honour that names Dempster, Langmuir and the halls. Those names alone tell an able reader which separation route works.

**Proposal:**
- **Part I, opened in autumn 1927 under Victoria's term:** the fission paper (1908), the chain result (1909), delayed neutrons (1910–11), Meitner's conjecture of the light isotope, the names of their authors and of the counting team, Peirce as respondent, Hahn as second finder, and Ralston College's protest (CR-050-R1 B4 clause 11). By 1927 Europe already knows all of this in substance; the opening gives it back its names.
- **Part II, sealed for twenty years from the demonstration (to autumn 1947):** Dempster's proof (1916–17), the contest and the feed, the halls, the device, the test, the breeding chemistry and gaseous diffusion, and the Chief's and Szilard's forecasts. It is dated, witnessed and promised an opening, so the nomos holds, and the people named in it know their credit is safe.
- **The term (APPROVED, author):** autumn 1947. Victoria's term only ever covered the deposit of 1908; Part II is a new deposit under the same form. **[OPEN]** Whether she lives to object.
- **The re-bonding of 1927 (APPROVED).** Every instrument tied to November 1928 is renewed at the opening (CT13):
  1. **The bond.** Every member of the inner circle signs a new bond for Part II, to 1947. It is dated, witnessed and promised an opening, like the first.
  2. **Those who refuse** retire on a full pension. They are free to speak of everything in Part I and remain bound, under the old bond's discipline, on what Part II holds. No one is forced to sign and no one is released into silence-breaking.
  3. **The Compact of the Tuolumne** is renewed to 1947. Ralston College's trustees again sign under written protest, and their protest of 1910 is published with Part I.
  4. **The Me-Wuk lease** is renewed to 1947, with a larger fee and the Poopenaut title confirmed.
  5. **Szilard** designed the opening and accepts Part II's sealing as its price: the science goes free now, and the factory twenty years later. His argument against it in the sealed record is part of Part II.
  6. **The idealist's guard** (CR-050 S3: a secret with a written end date gives a man of conviction a reason to wait) is renewed with the new date. It is weaker than in 1909, because twenty more years is a long wait, and the college knows it.

### L3: the lead after 1927 (APPROVED; figures MODEL)

- **Britain:** knows from the demonstration that separation works and has the 1926 memo in its files. With full commitment from 1928, a first device in roughly five to seven years, about 1933–35: comparable to Britain's historical effort (1947–52) and the Soviet one (1945–49), neither of which started from nothing either. Before 1932, nothing.
- **Germany:** later, and starting from power reactors.
- **Europe's reactor route (APPROVED).** Europe's power programmes of 1921 onward will try to build a pile, so the CR says what happens:
  - Graphite is blocked by Europe's own error (CR-050 X6), which Pacifica does not correct.
  - Heavy water is the other road. Heavy hydrogen is public through Dempster's survey (CR-050 X2), and Norway's cheap electrolysis at Vemork can concentrate it; historically Norsk Hydro produced heavy water from 1934 *(outside the texts)*.
  - **A European heavy-water pile runs about 1930–31**, aimed at power.
  - **Element 94 is an idea in Europe by 1932**, as it was to Turner in 1940 from the literature, but there is no production pile, no chemistry and no weapon route before the novel ends.
  - This is the second road the Works has known since 1915–17 (CR-050 X2), and it is the reason the Chief's and Szilard's forecasts differ (L1).
- **The Union:** later still; its best people are in Pacifica (CR-048 A3).
- **Pacifica in 1932:** gaseous diffusion running, the breeder lit, the stations working, five years of operating practice ahead of anyone. The lead in knowledge is gone; the lead in capability is wider in absolute terms than in 1927.

This supersedes CR-050's "its lead in industry is still a decade or more" for the bomb. It keeps it for capability as a whole.
