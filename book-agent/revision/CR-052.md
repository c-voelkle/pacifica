## What this CR does

Canon has never modelled energy. This CR adds a workbook that builds Pacifica's power from its sources (eight river basins and the strategic dams; steam on oil, coal and gas; the atomic stations; 22 oilfields one by one) and its uses (sixteen electricity sectors, thirteen fuel uses), on the financial workbook's own population, output and industry. Three cases sit on one switch: **Story** (canon with the author's decisions), **Stress** (the lean case) and **Max** (exploration: every source Pacifica can reach and every use that can absorb it).

What comes out (fifth build, Story case):

1. **Pacifica fuels itself in every year,** but not from its own oil alone. Finding the Los Angeles basin and San Joaquin giants from the 1880s brings their oil early, and pressure maintenance and water flooding from the 1910s (M8) keep it up: output reaches 228m bbl in 1913, peaks at about 304m in 1924 and is 255m in 1929. Realm coal at four times history carries the 1910s; in the late 1920s all the Pacifican-controlled Gulf crude is needed, with the CR-053 concessions' 20m bbl on top. Imports rise from 8% of primary energy in 1913 to 31% in 1929; no coal is imported in any year.
2. **California is the most motorized place on earth:** 0.33 vehicles per person across the Realm in 1929, about 0.41 in California, against about 0.22 in the US.
3. **Pacifica's electricity per person is about two and a half times the US's:** 2.3–2.9 times in 1920–32, about 2,400 kWh a head in 1929, on electric homes, half the main lines, Boulder Canyon and the Northwest's mills (the 4 November Max parts). On canon's previous uses alone it was about twice (1.9–2.3), historical California's own ratio in 1917.
4. **Using every source and use in reach it is 2.6–3.6 times the US** (Max, exploration), about 3,000 kWh a head in 1929, Norway's level for a country of 35 million. The remaining Story–Max gap is factory process heat, aluminium at Alcoa's scale and electrolytic ammonia (M2–M5), which await the financial workbook and rework (CR-054).

## The power seat: Harris J. Ryan (approved; registry entry at integration)

Historically Harris Joseph Ryan (1866–1934), born in Pennsylvania, Cornell 1887, taught at Cornell and from 1905 at Stanford, where his high-voltage laboratory made the 220 kV lines of the 1920s possible; he was president of the American Institute of Electrical Engineers in 1923–24 and died in 1934 *(to verify)*. In this timeline (author's direction: Pacifican-raised) his family comes west in the 1870s and he is examined at the Polytechnic rather than Cornell; his high-voltage laboratory is the Polytechnic's from the 1890s, and he builds the halls' electrical supply from 1917 (CT9), and it is his lines that carry the Sierra's power to the halls (1919–), the Columbia's to the Sierra (1924, CR-055) and Boulder's 275 miles to Los Angeles (1926). Why him: the seat in 1924 oversees transmission above all, and he is the line-builder, not a machine-builder; he is a Pacifican, not a recruit from the Union, which the seat has not had since Tesla; and he reaches sixty-eight in 1934, so the seat is held through the whole Story expansion and comes vacant again just after the book ends. Canon knock-ons (CT9, approved): CR-027's "Steinmetz as later successor" (PROPOSED) is retired, since Steinmetz dies in October 1923 (historical; canon leaves his death unchanged); the storyline's "seats pass to successors such as Steinmetz …" becomes "such as Ryan …", and CR-050's "Steinmetz builds their electrical supply" becomes Ryan's; the registry's `proposed_successor` for the power seat moves from CT-STEINMETZ to CT-HARRIS-RYAN. Beside him, PROPOSED for later use: Ezra Scattergood (born 1871) as head of the hydro board that builds Boulder and the Hawaiian board's model; Edith Clarke (born 1883), network analyst, as the obvious successor in 1934 and the first woman among *nā Kahuna*. Fact changes: none before integration (the seat's date, 1924, is already canon).

**The trade-off the author should know.** Haste moves the oil forward, not up. Primary reserves are about 13.5bn bbl whenever the fields are found (17.6bn with M8's flooding), and by 1932 Story has taken 7.9bn of them. Pacifica gets about twice history's oil in 1900–20, and less than the financial workbook's path by 1929 (255m against 300m). Keeping the late 1920s higher still needs new fields beyond the 22. The model is also generous in the peak years: at historical dates the same fields give 401m bbl in 1923 against history's 263m.

**Gas.** The dry gas of the Sacramento Valley (Rio Vista, historically 1936) and Buttonwillow come with C, and Kettleman's gas from 1923: about 110 PJ a year by 1929. The model still flares 85–125 PJ a year and runs a 230 PJ gas shortfall in 1929: pipelines from Kettleman and Buttonwillow to Los Angeles are the cheapest single fix for the late 1920s (review-3, finding 7).

Max at 3,000 is Norway's level, here for 35m people, and still an upper bound. The strain is industrial, not physical: Max needs markets for its aluminium and ammonia (Japan for the nitrogen), which the financial workbook does not yet carry and which review-3 found contradict the Japan partnership canon (hence CR-054).

**What the financial workbook now needs for Story:**
- the home-oil path with M8 (50–160m bbl a year above its present path in 1900–27, 45m below in 1929);
- about 2.15m vehicles a year by 1929;
- power investment of about $0.8–1.1bn a year in 1922–29 and electricity revenue of $1.0–1.6bn (utility receipts about four fifths);
- the CR-053 stakes' cost and dividends;
- the investment-share path and a derived industrial series (`ind_idx`), with the deflator question answered (`finance/investment-share-note.md` v2);
- the CR-054 and CR-055 champions' output and the dams' and the line's cost.

**Nuclear power and the oil gap (review-3, finding 8).** In 1929 the power stations burn about 37m bbl of oil, 68 PJ of gas and 103 PJ of coal for 26 TWh; replacing all of it with reactors cuts oil imports by about 13%. The demand is motor fuel, the navy, bunkers and lamps, and the oil railways and industry want. Canon forbids a Pacifican power station before the 1927 opening and makes Tokyo (1931) the first; from 1927 a natural-uranium gas-cooled line gives perhaps 300 MW by 1932 and 4 GW about 1940, and a 4 GW fleet holds the whole 1932 uranium stockpile. Nuclear is the 1930s answer; the bridge for 1924–32 is gas, M8, the Gulf and (CR-055) the hydrogenation of heavy residue, with the concessions of CR-053 as the 1930s' oil bought at 1920s prices.
