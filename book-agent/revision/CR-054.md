## The problem this CR solves

Pacifica's electricity per person is about two and a half times the US's (CR-052 F1) and cannot go much higher through homes, railways or dams: the remaining headroom is industrial, and the industries that absorb electricity on a national scale are aluminium, electrochemistry and electric steel. The Max case put them at export scale (180 kt of aluminium, two thirds of world output; 150 kt N of ammonia, Japan's whole fertiliser demand) and so (a) contradicted CR-031-R1 by making Japan a captive market, (b) sourced bauxite from British Guiana during the confrontation with London, and (c) made the 1931 collapse a Pacifican, not a Hilo, event. This CR keeps the industries and changes where their output goes.

## A1 — Aluminium: the grid, the fleet's aircraft, and the Concord pool (APPROVED)

**Scale (MODEL).** About 150 kt in 1929 (Story), 120 kt in 1932: Alcoa's own scale, half the world's, not two thirds. The Columbia about 100 kt (CR-055 moved the growth to the river; Wenatchee, the Rock Island reach, Rocky Reach), the Sierra electrochemical district at its 1920 size, Hilo 18 kt as canon's flagship and showcase on the hydro board's plants (CR-052 CT6).

**Where it goes (the eighth build's memo, 1929).**
1. **The grid itself — 35 kt.** Every long line of the Story expansion — Boulder to Los Angeles, the Sierra to the halls and the Bay, the Columbia's own lines and the Columbia–Sierra line (CR-055), the main-line electrification — is strung with steel-cored aluminium conductor, which Pacifica adopts a decade before it was general elsewhere. Ryan's lines are the aluminium champion's first customer. *(Historically ACSR took a fifth to a quarter of US aluminium by the late 1920s; to verify.)*
2. **The fleet's aircraft — 7 kt (10 by 1932).** Canon has carriers from 1922, the largest carrier allowance under the naval treaty, Japanese-crewed carriers in the western squadron, and fighter fields by 1932. The Concord's carrier and shore air arm is all-metal from the mid-1920s, with the airframe and engine champions in Los Angeles and (PROPOSED) a licensed line in Japan. Small in tonnes, but it is the demand that does not fall in 1931.
3. **The Concord pool — 24 kt.** Japan has no aluminium industry before the 1930s (historically Sumitomo and Nihon Soda from 1934, on imported bauxite; to verify). Under the division of labour (A3) Pacifica supplies Japan's aluminium — its navy's aircraft, its electrical industry.
4. **Civil uses at home — 38 kt** (cookware, vehicles, rail cars, the film industry's equipment, the electrical champion's castings).
5. **Export beyond the Concord — 46 kt** (Britain, Europe, the Union): the part exposed in 1931 (33 kt in 1932).

**Bauxite.** Arkansas, in the Gulf Republic since 1900, is the main North American bauxite district (historically Alcoa's). Under the Gulf compact the Republic's bauxite is shipped by the canal to the Columbia, the Sierra and Hilo; the Gulf gets the alumina refinery (PROPOSED: at Baton Rouge or Mobile Bay's Gulf side, to decide) and the royalty, as it has Spindletop's. Bintan and the Indies come later, through the Concord, in the 1930s. Guiana is not used. Fact changes: none.

## A2 — Nitrogen: the Valley and the Concord's own market, not Japan's (APPROVED)

**Scale (MODEL).** Electrolytic ammonia on the Columbia (Norsk Hydro's route: Haber synthesis on hydrogen from the river's secondary power), about 60 kt N by 1929 — from 1915 since CR-055 R0, on the BASF licence of 1913 — beside Haber–Bosch on Sacramento Valley gas for the Bay and the south (about 30 kt N). The arc plants make the war's nitric acid to 1919 and free their power for the halls as canon says, then end as in Norway.

**Where it goes.** The Central Valley, the Imperial Valley and the Columbia Basin first (irrigated, pumped, electrified farms want nitrogen more than rain-fed ones); Hawaii's and the Philippines' sugar and rice; Korea and the China trade through the Concord's shipping. **Not Japan:** Japan fixes its own nitrogen (Noguchi's electrolytic ammonia at Nobeoka, 1923, on Japanese hydro, historically; here with Pacifican turbines and Japanese capital) and the Concord's rule is that neither partner takes the other's home market in a staple. Fact changes: none. (This also settles CR-052 F9: the question was only ever the hydrogen source; "seized German patents" is dropped — the Haber–Bosch licence is bought from BASF like any other, as the 1919 peace left Germany intact.)

## A3 — The Concord's division of labour (APPROVED; the annex form PROPOSED)

CR-031-R1 makes Japan and Pacifica reciprocal industrial innovators and competitors. This CR gives the competition a written frame at the first Hayama Council (1927; PROPOSED as an annex to the Concord's economic articles, not a treaty of its own):
- **Pacifica** supplies the Concord's aluminium, magnesium, oil products, electrical equipment and heavy machinery, and the common fleet's aircraft; it does not sell fertiliser, textiles or ships' fittings into Japan's home market.
- **Japan** supplies the Concord's textiles, shipbuilding below capital ships (PROPOSED), nitrogen for its own market, and the silk and consumer trades; it does not build an aluminium or heavy electrical industry against Pacifica's before 1937 (the Concord's second term), and buys Pacifican power equipment for its hydro.
- **Both** act outward as one economic party (A6).
- Japan's gain is cheap aluminium and oil for a navy and an air arm it could not otherwise afford, and the electrical industry it builds on Pacifican equipment; Pacifica's is a market that does not fall with sterling. The Restoration officers of 1932 call this dependence; Wakatsuki and Shidehara call it the alliance paying for itself (canon's Manchuria passage already has Julian threaten the oil and the yen's convertibility — this is the same lever, named earlier).

## A4 — 1931: why Pacifica's crash stays milder (storyline sentences APPROVED)

The storyline promises a milder crash. With A1–A3 the reason is stated rather than assumed:
- **Sterling-priced aluminium undercuts Hilo in September 1931** (canon, unchanged): Hilo's 18 kt is the exposed showcase; the Columbia's and the Sierra's output is under the Concord pool's contracts, the grid's and the fleet's orders, and the Gulf's bauxite at gold parity.
- **The naval and air programme of 1931–32** (the carrier exercise, the fighter fields, dispersed command: canon) takes aluminium, steel and electrical equipment at the bottom of the slump; it is the order book the champions live on in 1932.
- **The Concord pool holds its price** inside; outside, the European cartel's quotas are what make Britain's and the Union's slump worse (A6), and Pacifica's export third — about 46 kt in 1929, 33 kt in 1932 — loses price, not volume: a champion's bad year, not a national one.
- **Nitrogen** is the Valley's and the Concord's own demand, which falls less than world trade.
Keawe's 1932 arc stands exactly as written: the price of power, the hydro board's bonds, Waimanu, the Charter.

## A6 — The two cartels (DECIDED 4 November)

The author asked which is more advantageous. The two cases cut opposite ways:

- **The nitrogen convention of 1930: join, as one Concord party.** The historical convention (Germany, ICI, Norsk Hydro, the Chilean producers) assigned home markets and quotas, and its exporters' largest outlet was Asia — Japan was the world's biggest importer of ammonium sulphate. A Concord outside it invites a European price war in exactly the market A3 reserves; a Concord inside it, as one party, gets its Asian zone recognised by London and Ludwigshafen, which is A3 made external. The beneficiary is Japan rather than Pacifica: Columbia ammonia would survive a price war, Nobeoka's would not, so joining is Pacifica protecting its partner's half of the bargain. It is also the first time the Concord acts as a single economic party in a European room.
- **Aluminium: lead the Concord pool from 1927, stay outside the European cartel of 1931.** Pacifica is the low-cost producer (Columbia hydro, canal bauxite, a domestic order book) and cartels protect high-cost producers; the Alliance would hand Pacifica a quota to shrink into. Outside it, the export third loses price in 1931 but not volume — Pacifica takes European share at the bottom — while the pool holds price inside the Concord, where two thirds of the output goes. The cost is friction with London's smelters four years after the settlement, absorbed by the Hayama annex and the debt relationship; the gain is a champion that comes out of the slump larger, the Pacifican pattern of 1932 everywhere else.

**Recommendation given:** join the one, lead the Concord pool and stay outside the Alliance.

**Decision (author, 4 November):** "I approve the nitrogen convention but want to stay out of the aluminium pool." The Concord joins the 1930 nitrogen convention as one party (APPROVED). Pacifica stays out of the European aluminium cartel, the Alliance Aluminium Compagnie of 1931 (APPROVED): its export third takes price, not quota, in the slump. The author confirmed the same day that "the aluminium pool" meant the European cartel: the Concord's own pool (A1, A3) stands. Fact changes: none; the convention seat is a ledger entry at integration.
