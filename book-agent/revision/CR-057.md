## What this CR does

It carries the Mises review into canon. Pacifica's economy keeps its state-capitalist ambition but gets a monetary and fiscal frame a hostile economist could not easily fault:
- **Falling prices.** The Reserve Bank lets prices fall as goods get cheaper, and the state works to make inputs cheap where that unlocks productivity.
- **A fixed reserve compact** ties the reserve centre's cover ratios to a treaty the government cannot change alone.
- **A sovereign wealth fund.** The Development Fund becomes one on the Norwegian and Singaporean lines, fed by rents, not by taxes above what the state needs.
- **Open patents and auctioned concessions.** Polytechnic patents are licensed openly, competitive industries carry no state stake, and concessions are auctioned. The state keeps its own estates and the canal.
- **Chartered districts at home and in four places abroad** give investors speed and cheap inputs.
- **A voluntary provident account**, deductible up to a yearly cap, rewards saving.
- **The river's spilled power** finds paying customers in 1930–32.

## B. MR-03: the reserve compact (PROPOSED)

**Ratios.** The Reserve Bank's charter fixes the domestic ratios. The reserve compact fixes the partners'.

| Liability | Minimum gold cover | Remainder | Precedent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Partners' gold reserves | 100%, earmarked | — | Mises's money-certificates |
| Partners' official dollar balances | **50%** | prime commercial bills under 90 days | above the domestic ratio: foreign official money is what runs, as Britain's did in September 1931 |
| The Reserve Bank's notes | **40%** (the workbook's rule today) | prime commercial bills | Federal Reserve Act 1913 |
| Banks' deposits at the Reserve Bank | **35%** | prime commercial bills | Federal Reserve Act 1913 (where the 35% was gold or lawful money; here gold) |

Government paper never counts as cover, whether Pacifican or foreign.

**Is 50% affordable?** The workbook holds $319m of gold above the 40% rule in 1929 and $465m in 1925, but only $49m in 1920. That would carry fractional partner balances of a few hundred million dollars in the late 1920s, not in 1920. Falling prices (MR-01) make the rule easier: the derived price level lowers the nominal money Pacifica needs and frees gold.

The money block (H1) must show three things:
- whether the 1920 cover binds, and whether it then brings on a sharp 1920–21 correction;
- how much room Bagehot lending has in 1931, when the Union rescue, gold credit to Britain and a run on partner balances all draw on the $631m above the floors;
- whether the weekly rate step would tighten in the middle of the slump.

If the balances do not fit, Pacifica accepts smaller balances; it does not lower the ratio.

**The guard against the government.** Cover laws are lifted "whenever a government deems that an emergency justifies" it (Mises XVII §12). Peel's Act of 1844 was suspended in 1847, 1857 and 1866. No rule binds a determined government. These make breaking the rule costly, slow and public:
1. **The ratios are in treaties.**
   - With Japan, the cover ratios are an agreed matter of the Concord from 1927, beside gold parity (§33). They cannot be lowered without Japan's consent, given through its party in the Concord.
   - The Philippine and Hawaiian crowns, on Julian's side of the Concord, and holders outside it (the Gulf Republic, Korea, China's reserve banks) hold their balances under bilateral compacts. Each compact can be changed only with that holder's consent.
   - The Realm's government cannot lower any ratio alone.
2. **The Treasury cannot borrow from the Bank** beyond advances repaid within the fiscal year. The Bank's holdings of government paper are capped at its own capital, and the standing debt is collateral only (MR-06). The Bank may act as the Treasury's fiscal agent, paying and receiving on its account (canon: its paymasters in Siberia, 1918–19), but never as its lender.
3. **Automatic tightening.** Below the floor, the Bank's discount rate rises by a fixed step each week until cover is restored. The Reichsbank's 1875 tax on uncovered notes paid the Treasury, which gives a government a stake in over-issue. Here the penalty is a dearer rate, so the government never gains from a breach.
4. **A weekly return**, as the Bank of England has published since 1844. The Office of Public Measures prints it and cables it to every partner capital.
5. **Unconditional redemption at par.** Partners withdraw earmarked gold without notice. Suspending convertibility is a default under the compact.
6. **Separation.** The Reserve Bank, the Treasury and the Fund have three boards. The Bank never lends to the Fund or buys its paper. Its profits go to the Reserve Fund buffer, not to the Treasury's spending or to the Fund, so no budget depends on it issuing more. Governors serve long terms and can be removed only for cause.

**The real guard.** Pacifica's power rests on its dollar being the safest money. §34 already says "the Union's savers learn which flag is safe". A government that diluted the cover would sell the order for a season's projects. **[PROPOSED]** The temptation comes in 1931–32, when the rescues and the Columbia's idle power both argue for printing. The common Finance minister (Pacifican by convention) and the Reserve Bank's governor refuse, and the Senate backs them. Who argues for printing is OPEN.

**Dates.** Partner reserves are held in San Francisco from 1886 (the peso, CR-029). Japan's currency rests on Pacifican deposits (CR-021 H). The Reserve Bank operates by 1880 (§19) and in 1897 (§25), so it is founded before 1880; the exact date and the first bilateral compact's date are OPEN. **The ratios survive the Thalassia settlement of 1932**: adjustable parities change what a dollar is worth in gold, not the share of gold behind it.

## C. MR-08: the Development Fund as a sovereign wealth fund (PROPOSED; amends CR-020 B8)

**Sources: rents, not taxes on work.** In the workbook's 1929 Budget the state's rents are:

| Rent | 1929 ($m) |
| --- | --- |
| Mineral and oil royalties (after the school of mines' share, CR-035) | 52 |
| Canal (from 1900) | 18 |
| State enterprises: the state's own estates (the Light and Matter Works, the power estate, the hydro boards) | 155 |
| Crown share | 176 |
| **Total** | **about 400 (0.6% of GDP)** |

The workbook's "other receipts" column ($28m in 1929) mixes the Land Fund with one-off events. Only the Land Fund's net margin, after the Native annuities and assisted passage (CR-033), counts as a rent.

From 1880 these rents feed the Fund, not "seventy percent of each budget surplus". E abolishes most of the Crown share, so its $176m falls to the dividends on the canal and the trunk rights of way. Auctioned concessions raise the royalties instead (E). Ordinary surpluses go to the Reserve Fund buffer and then to lower taxes.

**Investment.**
- **To about 1914, at home.** The Fund holds Pacifican state and railway bonds and mortgage paper. While the champions borrow in London (§19), investing abroad would earn less than Pacifica pays to borrow.
- **From about 1914, abroad as well.** Once Pacifica is a creditor, a diversified portfolio abroad keeps the Fund from overheating the home economy and from becoming a political lender. Norway invests its fund wholly abroad for those reasons.
- **Losses are the Fund's own.** H3 books the Russian losses of 1917–18 and the German and Latin American defaults of 1931–32 (§34) against it.
- **The commercial arm at home**, on Temasek's lines (1974), takes minority stakes only when a firm asks, never with board control. It lends beside private capital that takes the first loss, never more than half of a venture (MR-10). The counties' grid loans (CR-052) continue that way, as commercial loans.
- **Credit to the state's own estates is exempt from the private tranche.** It is a loan from one public body to another. So the Works' "Development Fund credit for electrochemical expansion, which is what the halls are" (§30, CR-051) stands, seen whole only by the sealed comptroller.

**The spending rule.** From 1900, when the Fund is large enough to spend from, the budget receives a fixed share of its value each year, earmarked for infrastructure, the Polytechnic and the research institutes: **3.5%** (MODEL). Before 1900 the rents accumulate. Norway's rule was 4% from 2001 and 3% from 2017; Singapore lets the budget take up to half of expected long-term real returns (to verify). Infrastructure also pays its own way where it can: power at cost, tolls, and project bonds sold to savers (MR-06). Each project is judged in a published benefit–cost account.

**Performing well.**
- an independent board;
- a target return over consol and Pacifican state-bond yields, published in advance;
- returns at market value in the Office's tables every year;
- managers paid on the excess over the target across several years;
- losses written off in public (MR-07).

**The trade-off, in rough numbers** (MODEL; the workbook must compute it). The workbook's yearly path of royalties, canal income and the estates' profits, without the Crown share and before any rise from auctions, compounded at 5% from 1880 with the 3.5% rule from 1900:
- the Fund holds about **$4.4bn in 1929**;
- it gives the budget about **$145m a year**.

With the Crown share as it stands today the figures would be about $7.5bn and $245m. How much auctioned royalties add is a lever (H6).

The current Fund invests about 70% of a $1.9bn surplus, some $1.3bn a year at home. So the public development flow is much smaller, and the 1929 surplus of $1.9bn (a quarter of receipts) becomes lower taxes instead. Whether output per person holds depends on two things:
- what private investors do with the money left in their hands (F raises saving);
- the Fund's net premium over the private return, about 0.18 in CR-056's mode 2 (MR-07).

That is the test H3 runs. If output falls below the story's figures, the author can raise the spending rule, widen the rent base (higher royalty rates), or accept a slower Pacifica with lighter taxes.

**The name.** Keep "Development Fund", or rename it (OPEN).

## D. MR-09: paying customers for the river (PROPOSED)

**The economics.** Spilled water costs nothing to generate, so any buyer paying more than nothing makes money. The limits are the buyer's equipment cost and the timing: the Columbia's surplus comes with the spring and summer freshet and with the slump, while the dams' firm load is in winter. The useful customers are:
- interruptible loads that run in summer;
- loads that displace imports;
- slump-time works that are worth building anyway.

Electric cooking and water heating are already in the Story case: 1,700 kWh a wired home in 1929 (CR-052 M1). The Northwest mills' electrode boilers already take the river's secondary power: 5.0 TWh in 1929 and 7.0 TWh in 1932. The 1.2–4.6 TWh spilled in 1930–32 is what is left after them. House heating is a winter load and does not absorb spill.

| Use | Why it pays | Size (MODEL, to compute in the energy workbook) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **More electrode boilers**, in the canneries, sugar refineries, chemical works and pulp mills not yet on the river, with fuel backup | The cheapest absorber: a boiler costs little, burns no fuel while water is spilling and switches back to oil or wood when the river is low. | 1–2 TWh |
| **Ferroalloys** (ferrosilicon, ferromanganese, silicon carbide) on interruptible contracts | Norway's model: furnaces stop when water is short. Ordinary steel demand falls in 1932, so the buyer is the naval and air programme of 1931–32 (§34), its armour and alloy steels, and the yards. | 50–100 kt at 8–9 MWh/t (to verify) = 0.4–0.9 TWh |
| **Irrigation pumping for new Basin farms** | A summer load that matches the freshet. New leasehold farms on Land Fund terms take settlers and returning workers in the slump. Canon has no Grand Coulee, so the water is lifted from the Wells or Chief Joseph pools, more than 100 m: about 0.6 MWh an acre-foot, about 2 MWh an acre a year. The historical Columbia Basin plan of about 1.1 million acres had its first water only in 1952, so the start here is small. | 0.1–0.2 million acres by 1932 = 0.2–0.4 TWh, rising after |
| **The rest of the Northwest's main lines electrified** (the mountain divisions already are, CR-052; half the main lines by 1929, M6) | Displaces locomotive oil at the import price and eases the Gulf ceiling (CR-053). Built in 1930–32 with cheap labour, it answers CR-056's decision 4 (hold the power estate's investment up in the slump). It is a firm year-round load, not a spill customer, and fits only because the slump also frees winter firm power. | 0.3–0.8 TWh |
| **Cold storage and quick-freezing** of the Columbia's fruit and the coast's fish | Birdseye's method (1924–30) a few years early. Wenatchee apples and salmon reach Asian and Union markets. | small in TWh, visible on the page |
| **Tacoma's carbide after rubber** | Tacoma makes about 50 kt of rubber in 1929 (CR-055, MODEL). With natural rubber collapsing after the Stevenson scheme ends in 1928, from 1930 the carbide plant turns to acetylene chemicals (acetic acid, vinyl, welding gas), with one rubber line on standby as the strategic reserve (MR-09). Cyanamide fertilizer is left out: the 1930–32 nitrogen glut is why the convention exists (CR-054). | the plant already built |

Together the boilers, ferroalloys and Basin pumps absorb about 2–3 TWh of the 1930–32 spill. The diffusion plant (150 MW in 1932, CR-055 R3) and the rising Basin take the rest as they grow.

## E. MR-11: open patents, auctioned concessions and chartered districts (PROPOSED; amends CR-020 B9 and OPEN-020-10)

**Patents.**
- From the Polytechnic's first patents, every patent it holds is licensed **non-exclusively** to any firm, foreign firms included, at a published modest royalty (MODEL: a few percent of the patented product's price) and with no equity condition.
- **Two limits.**
  - Licences to Concord partners follow the Hayama annex (CR-054): Japan builds no heavy electrical or aluminium industry, and Pacifica licenses accordingly.
  - The Works' sealed results are not patents (CR-050) and are never licensed.
- The state's return is the taxes and the spillovers of the activity.
- Patents are abundant, not scarce. The Union's complaint becomes that Pacifica gives its inventions away. Its answer (line 852 amended, G12) is tariffs, anti-dumping duties and subsidies to its own laboratories, since compulsory licensing is pointless against patents already open.
- **The champions are the opposition.** They want exclusive licences, and some of them say so in the Senate. This partly settles §5's OPEN "patent measures" (CT9).

**The state's shares.**
- **Competitive industries** (steel, motors, chemicals, electrical, film, aluminium, aircraft): no stake. The golden share survives only for a short published list of defence-critical firms (armour, propellants, the Works' suppliers), as a review of foreign takeovers.
- **Concessions** (oil and mineral leases, rail and port concessions, telegraph and power franchises): **auctioned**. The state takes the winning royalty or fee, not equity. Competitive bidding gives the privilege its market price, which Mises accepts as fair. It also raises the royalties that feed the Fund (C).
- **Equity only where the state owns the underlying asset:** the canal and the trunk rights of way (including the cable landings). These are the limited-space monopolies Mises concedes.
- **The state's own estates stay the state's:** the Light and Matter Works, chartered in 1883 with the state holding the shares (§19, CR-049), the power estate and the hydro boards. Their profits are among the Fund's rents (C), and the state's dividend on its shares left in the Works (§30, CR-051) stands.
- **Banking:** still no stake.

**Chartered districts at home.** Pacifica is already open, so a domestic district is about **speed and cheap inputs**, not opening. **[PROPOSED]** A Chartered Districts Act of the Realm, 1920. The Hawaiian crown passes its own act for its ground, where the Mōʻī takes the lease premiums (CR-020 B7), and the Philippine crown its own for Mariveles (below). A district offers:
- power at cost from the river or the hydro board;
- serviced leasehold land;
- licences within weeks;
- a bonded customs area, where components enter free for re-export, on the Hamburg free port (1888) and Hong Kong pattern;
- a Polytechnic training school;
- a five-year tax holiday for new plants (MODEL), open to any firm that locates there, so the rule is general and not a privilege for named firms.

The first districts:
- the Columbia's: Portland–Vancouver, Tacoma, Wenatchee;
- the Los Angeles harbour;
- from 1928, the Thalassia Plan's two leeward districts (CR-020 B7).

**Zones abroad: where they work to Pacifica's advantage.** Each is chartered under the host's law and sovereignty, open to the host's firms and to foreigners on the same terms, with its fees shared with the host. That is what makes them attractive rather than colonial. Ranked:

**Not recommended:**
- **Keelung and Formosa.** Pacifica already administers Formosa's revenues under a Chinese commission, and China resents it (§37). The administration becomes a Concord affair from 1928–29. A zone would deepen the grievance.
- **Korea.** Korea is independent, so any district would need Seoul's charter, and it would tread on Japan's economic predominance, which is treaty canon (§25, §26). The Hayama annex (CR-054), which divides the partners' industries, makes it worse.
- **Tsingtao.** Pacifica holds only the anchorage (§30), and Japan was denied the prize. A zone there needs China's consent and taunts Japan.
- **Batum.** Already a Georgian free port with Pacifica's own berth (CR-047).

## F. MR-12: the provident account (PROPOSED)

A voluntary tax-deductible saving account on the pattern of Switzerland's pillar 3a. Its in-world name is OPEN; "the provident account" here.

| Term | Pacifica (MODEL) | Säule 3a (2026) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who | anyone with earned income, residents and guest workers alike | anyone with AHV-liable income |
| Deductible contribution, wage earners | up to 10% of earned income, at most **$300 a year** (about a tenth of average earnings of $2,800 in 1929, CR-056) | CHF 7,258 with a pension fund |
| Deductible contribution, the self-employed and those without an employer's scheme | up to 20% of earned income, at most **$1,500** | 20% of income, at most CHF 36,288 |
| Tied until | five years before the Realm's pension age (age OPEN) | five years before AHV age |
| Early withdrawal | a home on public leasehold (CR-020 B7), at most once in five years; starting a business; leaving the Realm for good, so a guest worker carries his savings home; disability | home ownership, self-employment, permanent emigration, disability |
| Tax at withdrawal | a separate reduced rate, a fifth of the ordinary rate (MODEL) | taxed separately at a reduced rate |
| Providers | the savings bank (OPEN-020-09) and licensed banks and insurers; balances portable between them | banks and insurers |
| Investment rule | market returns under published limits, with a cap on any one issuer, **the state included**. The Fund gets the money only by competing for it. | investment rules of BVV 3 |

**Why it suits Pacifica.**
- Under falling prices (MR-01) a tied balance gains real value every year, so the incentive compounds.
- Guest workers can save in Pacifica instead of remitting at once, which slows the remittance outflow (CR-020 B11).
- Families buy leaseholds with it, the Singaporean pattern.
- **A period precedent:** Britain has given income-tax relief on life-assurance premiums since 1853, so a deduction for tied saving is not anachronistic.

**Starting date.** It starts with the Realm's income tax (date OPEN; CR-021 lists income tax among the Realm's revenues). In the workbook it raises the household saving rate (H2) and costs receipts the deduction times the marginal rate.
