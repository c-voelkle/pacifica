## What this CR does

**The strategy.** Neighbours balance against the most threatening power, not the strongest. Pacifica therefore has to be the giant nobody fears, so that Canada and Mexico stand against the Union instead of lining up with it. Its tools are information, credit, recognition, railways, cables, the canal, places at the Polytechnic, and later Kaiulani's prestige media. There is no army. In the early decades the coalition against the Union is never formalised: each neighbour has its own reasons, and Pacifica's job is to make sure each capital sees them. Later, containment turns into gravity. Canada becomes commercially Pacifican and strategically neutral; Mexico stays proud and sovereign, but its largest creditor, oil producer and rail partner is Pacifican.

**Why Canada leans west (from Macfarlane).** Canada learned to conduct its own diplomacy through fish, seals, rivers and power, where London lacked local knowledge and kept trading Canadian interests away. In this timeline most of that western business is with Pacifica, which ratifies its treaties. The eastern business is with the Union, whose Senate in our history killed Canadian agreements one after another: fisheries in 1888, inland fisheries in 1908, salmon in 1909 and 1919–20, Niagara in 1929, the St Lawrence in 1932. Canadians learn that a treaty with Pacifica is a treaty and a treaty with the Union is a draft.

**Who fuels the Union.** The Union holds none of the Gulf Republic's fields, no California and, after 1900, no Indian Territory. From about 1910 it imports oil, and by 1929 about four-fifths of what it uses comes from abroad, mostly from Pacifica's ally, the Gulf Republic. Pacifica's answer to that dependence is a doctrine: never pull the lever.

## Part I — Mexico and the early ring (1865–1871)

### NA-1 — Juárez and Querétaro (APPROVED)

Canon already has "arms and credit support Juárez as France withdraws" (§13). This CR makes it specific:
- Pacifica recognises Juárez's government and extends credit secured on the Sonora customs, on hard but fair terms.
- Rifles reach the Republic's army through Paso del Norte from Pacifican New Mexico. Selling arms to a recognised government fighting a European intervention is not the crude style Pacifica avoids with the Confederate rump (CR-048).
- Maximilian is shot at Querétaro on 19 June 1867, in the city where Elias and Alvarado signed the convention of 1847. Mexican liberals learn that Pacifica, not Washington, ended the French adventure. The Union has no land border with Mexico in this timeline and cannot repeat its historical Rio Grande pressure.
- Pacifica's first job as arbiter is restraining its own Confederate partner: Confederate exiles had courted Maximilian and settled in Mexico.
- The support also repairs part of the memory of 1847 ("Thorne knew", CR-028) ahead of the Baja purchase and the Guaymas concession.
- Juárez's own maxim of 15 July 1867 becomes the shared language of the neighbours: *"Entre los individuos, como entre las naciones, el respeto al derecho ajeno es la paz"* ("Among individuals, as among nations, respect for the rights of others is peace").

### NA-2 — BC and the CPR (APPROVED)

- A strong Canadian buffer is worth more to Pacifica than an annexed province that would provoke Britain. Pacifica encourages BC's entry into Confederation (20 July 1871); in 1866–71, it says privately in London that a Union move on Rupert's Land or BC would be "a matter of concern to the Pacific".
- It takes a quiet, minority stake in the CPR's finance through London houses (Hartnell-style agents), never in the charter. **The precedent to respect:** in 1873 the Pacific Scandal, partly over American money behind Hugh Allan's syndicate, brought Macdonald down. Pacifican money must never be visible in Ottawa.
- The CPR's western traffic and its trans-Pacific service (chartered steamers from 1887, the Empress mail liners from 1891) feed Pacifica's trans-Pacific shipping, so the British "All-Red Route" ends up serving Pacifica.

### NA-3 — Grant's war of 1870 and the spring of two treaties (APPROVED)

Canon: Grant attacks the Confederacy in July 1870 and fails; Sherman negotiates in spring 1871. In this timeline the Union is not irrational. It pursues a coherent strategy to make up for losing the West, and each neighbour meets a different part of it at a specific place:

**Canada:**
- On 13 April 1869 Sumner puts the Alabama damages at about $2.1 billion, some $2 billion of it "indirect", a sum read on both sides of the Atlantic as payable only in Canada. Historically the cession was implied more than demanded; his explicit call for the British flag to leave the hemisphere came in a memorandum to Fish in January 1871 (to verify). Here the claims are larger, because British-built raiders helped the South win.
- The Fenian raids of May 1870 (Eccles Hill, Trout River) are tolerated by Washington.
- Also in May 1870, the Union refuses to let the steamer *Chicora* through the Sault canal with troops and supplies for the Red River expedition; she later passes empty and the cargo goes round by land. Ottawa learns that the Union can cut Canada in two at the Soo.
- The St Albans raid was launched from Canada in 1864, and Montreal sheltered Confederate agents, so the Union has an account to settle in the north.
- Britain is withdrawing its garrisons in 1870–71, so Canada stands alone.

**Canada's money:** Richmond's Mississippi tolls drive Midwest grain onto the St Lawrence canals. A Union victory that frees the river would cost Montreal money.

**Mexico:**
- Grant's treaty to annex Santo Domingo, rejected by the Senate on 30 June 1870, shows Mexico a Union looking for a Caribbean naval base.
- Juárez prefers a weak Confederacy between Mexico and the Union to a reunited giant.

**During the war:**
- Pacifica coordinates nothing. It shows each capital the whole map, and each acts on its own reasons.
- Canada's militia, already under arms after the May raids, stays on the border and ties down Union troops.
- Mexico closes its Gulf ports to Union coaling.

**The spring of two treaties (May–July 1871):**
- In Washington, Britain trades Canada's fisheries away for peace with the Union in the Treaty of Washington (8 May 1871), as historically.
- In the same weeks, Pacifica grants free navigation "forever" of the Stikine, Yukon and Porcupine through Alaska, for nothing but the asking. Historically the United States granted this in the Treaty of Washington; here Alaska is Pacifica's to give.
- BC enters Confederation on 20 July.
- The grant matters at once: the Cassiar gold rush of 1873–74 runs up the Stikine through Wrangell.
- Name and place of the Pacifican instrument OPEN (proposed: a convention signed at Monterey with Britain, with a BC delegate such as Joseph Trutch in the British party).

### NA-4 — Doheny and Mexican oil (APPROVED, without the Teapot Dome scandal)

- **Canon check.** The Los Angeles City field is found by the state survey in 1882 (CR-052 C, model dates), so Doheny is not its discoverer.
- **A Pacifican by residence.** Edward L. Doheny (born 1856, Fond du Lac) prospects at Kingston, New Mexico, in the 1880s. New Mexico is Pacifican from 1865. He enters oil around 1890 in the second wave, as an independent beside the trust.
- **Mexico (1900–01).** As historically, the president of the Mexican Central Railway, A. A. (Albert Alonzo) Robinson, invites him because his locomotives need fuel oil. Prabook and a trade history both attest the invitation. Pacifica's oil-firing doctrine makes that demand earlier and stronger. The El Ébano wells of 1901 come just after the canal opens (1900, canon), so Tampico crude can also go west to Asia. Cerro Azul No. 4 (February 1916) remains his great well.
- **Company.** As historically, the Mexican Petroleum Company (incorporated December 1900) drills El Ébano, and its later Huasteca Petroleum Company (1905 or 1907; sources differ) drills Cerro Azul. Here both are chartered Pacifican oil companies, and "Huasteca" names the producing company throughout this CR. The Crown's 25% is the canon rule for chartered oil firms (storyline §25, "Industry beneath the measures").
- **Contest.** Mexican oil becomes a three-way contest: Pearson's El Águila (British; Potrero del Llano, 1910), the Union's Standard, and Huasteca.
- **Article 27 (1917).** Pacifica can live with the nation's ownership of the subsoil, because its own model already is state-owned subsoil, concessions and a Crown share. London and the Union cannot. The historical Doheny fought Article 27 and lobbied for intervention. Here Thalassia refuses him, as it repudiated the Vanderlip concession (North and Yakutia note), and makes him accept concession terms.
- **Where the crude goes:** to the Union's East Coast on long contracts (NA-12c).

### Character note — Edward L. Doheny as a Pacifican (DH-1 to DH-4, APPROVED; IR-1)

**The real man (*hist.*).** He was the son of an Irish immigrant, born at Fond du Lac in 1856. He found the Los Angeles City field in 1892, opened Mexico's oil, and by the 1920s was among the richest men in America. Then came Teapot Dome: in 1921 he sent Interior Secretary Albert Fall $100,000 in cash for a naval oil lease. Fall was convicted of taking a bribe; Doheny was acquitted of giving it. He sold out to Standard of Indiana in 1925, and in 1929 his son Ned was killed at Greystone by Hugh Plunkett, the secretary who had helped carry the cash. The Pacifican Doheny keeps the drive and the luck and loses the corruption: more successful, less criminal.

**DH-1 — Apprenticeship and the railway fortune (APPROVED).**
- **1872–1890.** He leaves school at sixteen and goes west. His first job, driving mules for a boundary survey (historically the Arizona–New Mexico survey), leads to a job with Pacifica's state survey, which from 1876 maps California's anticlines (CR-052 C). The survey geologists teach him to read rock.
- **The 1880s.** He is a miner at Kingston, New Mexico, where he makes his friendship with Albert Fall, and marries Carrie Louella Wilkins there on 7 August 1883.
- **1890–1900.** He reaches Los Angeles about 1890, after the state survey's discovery of the city field in 1882 (CR-052 C), as a wildcatter on its fringes. His real gift is selling, not discovering. Historically he signed a five-year contract in 1896 to fuel the Santa Fe; here Pacifica's oil-firing doctrine makes that the obvious business, and he wins the railways' fuel contracts. That makes him the largest independent beside the trust, before Mexico (NA-4).

**DH-2 — Never sold, and Peru (APPROVED).**
- The champions' golden share blocks a sale abroad, and there is no scandal to force one, so Pan American, his holding company with a tanker fleet sized for the canal, stays his and grows.
- **Peru is his consolation for Lago.** Thalassia bars him from Lago in 1925 (NA-12g; PROPOSED). In return he becomes the Pacifican company of CR-053 P3, which until now had no name: the Lobitos fields and the northern concessions that International Petroleum does not hold, from about 1924, with the Union's reciprocal fifth inside his company.
- By 1929 he is the second name in Pacifican oil after the trust, and among the largest fortunes in the Realm.

**DH-3 — Less criminal (APPROVED).**
- **No bribe.** Elk Hills is the Pacifican navy's reserve, and Pacifica's audit and reporting system (the Office of Public Measures) makes a quiet cash "loan" unthinkable.
- **The test is still there, and he passes it.** When Fall rises in New Mexico politics, Doheny refuses to make the friendship transactional. That refusal is the honest mirror of Teapot Dome.
- **Ned lives.** Without the scandal, Plunkett has no motive, so Ned is not killed in 1929. He takes over the business in the 1930s.
- **His death.** Doheny dies in 1935, as historically, outside the book's span. His name goes on a petroleum school at Carquinez, not on a memorial library for a murdered son.

**DH-4 — Two complications (APPROVED).**
- **The Crown's quarter.** As a chartered oil company he pays the Crown its 25% (canon) and resents it. He sits in the Senate for Los Angeles as the voice of the independents against the trust, which keeps the open contest between rivals that Pacifica's nomos values (CR-049) alive in oil.
- **Ireland.** Historically Doheny led the American Association for the Recognition of the Irish Republic and gave de Valera money. In 1919–21, with Britain inside the coalition in Russia, his Irish money is a private embarrassment to Thalassia. Thalassia tolerates it as a private matter, and London remembers it.

**IR-1 — Thalassia leans into the Irish connection (direction APPROVED; IR-2 to IR-5 APPROVED by the author on 6 November and deferred: they are not part of this CR's storyline changes and go to the Anglo-Pacifican confrontation CR).**

In the later 1920s the private embarrassment becomes a public instrument of the confrontation with Britain. It is used in Pacifica's own style: money, recognition, infrastructure and honest reporting, never arms and never a false paper. The Irish Free State is a Dominion like Canada, so the Irish lever is the same lever as the Dominion strategy of Part II, pulled in Britain's own backyard.
- **IR-2, recognition.** The Free State sent the first Dominion envoy to Washington in 1924 (*hist.*). Here a Free State legation at Thalassia is welcomed in 1926–27. Coming as it does beside Canada's growing independence and the Balfour Declaration (1926), London reads it as a signal.
- **IR-3, the Shannon.** The Shannon hydroelectric scheme at Ardnacrusha (1925–29, built historically by Siemens) goes to Pacifica's power estate instead, on Pacifican credit and standards: the AC standard and the CR-055 kind of dam, in Britain's own backyard.
- **IR-4, the press and the money.** De Valera's American fundraising for the *Irish Press* (1927–31, *hist.*) runs through Doheny and the Pacifican Irish. Thalassia lets it run as private and openly reported, Kaiulani's newsreels cover de Valera's entry into the Dáil in 1927, and no state money changes hands.
- **IR-5, the credit line.** Historically de Valera withheld the land annuities from Britain in 1932, which led to the Economic War. A standing Pacifican credit line, agreed in 1927 on ordinary commercial terms, means London knows Ireland could hold out. It is a quiet fourth weight beside the three prongs of the 1927 settlement (CR-049 §L1): debt, the Petrograd account and the demonstration at sea.
- **Limits.** No arms to Irish republicans, no Pacifican position on partition, and Ulster left alone. Pacifica's line is Dominion freedom, not rebellion.

## Part II — Canada (1871–1931)

### NA-5 — The election of 1911 (APPROVED: Borden wins narrowly)

Annexation fear makes no sense in this timeline: the Union has lost two wars and the continent holds four states. Laurier still loses narrowly, for the other reasons:
1. **The east–west economy fights reciprocity.** This is the CPR (Van Horne: "bust the damn thing"), the Toronto "Eighteen" and the Montreal banks. After NA-2, Pacifican capital and the Vancouver–Asia trade are embedded in that economy.
2. **Bourassa splits Liberal Quebec** over Laurier's naval law of 1910.
3. **The West needs Union markets less,** because its wheat can leave through Vancouver and the canal.
4. **Canadians remember that the Union cancels its deals:** reciprocity in 1866, the fisheries articles in 1885.

Pacifica does nothing visible; the toll preference first considered is dropped. Borden's win keeps him in office for the war and for the Siberian brigade.

### NA-6 — The Canadians at Irkutsk (APPROVED, no mutiny)

- **The force.** A Canadian brigade of 4,192 men under Major-General James H. Elmsley, carried from Vancouver in CPR liners. It lands at Urajio (Japanese Vladivostok) and goes up to **Irkutsk**, where its headquarters sit beside the coalition's command.
- **Duties** (OPEN; proposed): depot and command guard, the railway zone, and liaison with the Pacifican railway board west of Chita (CR-030).
- **Real ingredients:**
  - A Royal North-West Mounted Police squadron, which historically went to Siberia, polices the railway zone.
  - The Canadian Economic Commission (Siberia), created in October 1918 under Dana Wilgress, moves up the line to Irkutsk with the brigade. The Royal Bank opens its branch at Urajio, the Japanese port the brigade lands at, and keeps it open after the war for Canadian trade through the Concord's port. Historically the bank's prefabricated building sailed from Vancouver in November 1918, and the branch opened at Vladivostok in 1919 and closed that October with the withdrawal.
  - The Canadian Locomotive Company of Kingston, which built decapods for Russia in 1915–18, receives orders through the board's purchasing.
- **Why Borden stays after the Armistice in Europe:** Siberian trade, the orders, separate standing for Canada at the peace, and Pacifica paying the brigade's costs. A volunteer brigade serves through the 1919 campaign (size OPEN).
- **Québec.** French Canadians volunteer gladly. Pacifica's official languages by county, its religious freedom, and its refusal to send missionaries or to fight wars for London make it popular in Québec. There is no Victoria mutiny.
- **Afterwards.** The veterans come home in 1920 as "the Irkutsk men", a pro-Pacifica network in the militia and among the bush pilots of the 1920s.

### NA-7 — Great Bear Lake (APPROVED)

- **Why it is found early.** From 1908 the Light and Matter Works buys whole ores worldwide under cover of a radium trust (CR-050). Great Bear's veins are the five-element silver–cobalt–nickel–arsenic–uranium type of Joachimsthal, the deposit the Works knows best.
- **The clue.** In 1900 J. Mackintosh Bell and Charles Camsell of the Geological Survey of Canada noted cobalt bloom and copper near Echo Bay (one account, Wikipedia's, adds uranium). Historically, LaBine followed the cobalt bloom and found pitchblende on 16 May 1930. A Works geologist reading Bell sees cobalt bloom in a silver and cobalt country, the Joachimsthal signature.
- **The find.** A canoe expedition down the Athabasca, Slave, Mackenzie and Great Bear rivers with Camsell (born at Fort Liard in 1876; Deputy Minister of Mines 1920–36, then of Mines and Resources to 1946) finds the pitchblende about 1911–13 (year PROPOSED).
- **The deal, in the CR-050 pattern:**
  - A Canadian-chartered company on Crown land, so Canadian sovereignty is never in question.
  - Pacifican finance and an exclusive offtake, sold publicly as a radium play.
  - A free radium allowance for Canadian hospitals as the charity on show. The uranium rides along as an afterthought.
- **The Sahtú Dene.**
  - A treaty with the Dene comes with the find, as Treaty 11 (1921) historically followed the Norman Wells oil strike.
  - Historically, the Dene men who carried the ore sacks died of cancer, and Délı̨nę became known as the "Village of Widows". The Works knows the hazard, and its ore-handling code prevents it.
- **1927.** Britain needs uranium and asks Ottawa to set the contract aside for imperial priority. Ottawa keeps the contract, and Canada stays neutral. That neutrality has substance: the Empire's North American uranium is under contract to the other side.
- **Open:** company name, volumes for the uranium ledger of CR-049/050, the shipping route (river, then rail to Vancouver), and the year of the find. The Toronto-born physicist Dempster, recruited in 1914 (CR-050), is a possible Canadian thread.

### NA-8 — A western boundary-waters treaty (APPROVED)

- Before the Union's Boundary Waters Treaty of 1909 (date PROPOSED: about 1906), Pacifica and Britain, for Canada, sign a treaty on the western waters, with Canadians doing the negotiating. It covers the Columbia, Kootenay, Okanagan, Similkameen, Skagit and Pend Oreille, the Fraser salmon, and the Alaskan rivers of NA-3.
- **The commission.** Historically, Canada wanted a strong commission of equals and the United States held out for a weak one (Macfarlane, ch. 2). Pacifica gives Canada the strong one, with powers to regulate as well as to investigate. Canada's first experience of real parity is with Pacifica.
- **The Harmon Doctrine.** Pacifica rejects it. That doctrine says the upstream country owes its neighbour nothing, and on the Columbia Pacifica is downstream.
- **The Union's treaty of 1909 follows.** Canada asks the Union for what Pacifica already granted and gets the historical compromise. Its water quarrels with the Union carry on in the east: the Chicago diversion, the St Mary–Milk, Niagara.

### NA-9 — The Columbia storage compact (APPROVED)

- The Columbia rises in BC, so the firm power of CR-055's dams depends on storage upstream: the Arrow Lakes, Kootenay Lake (Corra Linn) and later Kinbasket.
- **Terms (date PROPOSED: 1922–25).** BC builds or allows the storage. Pacifica pays half of the extra downstream power, in power delivered or in gold (form OPEN), plus payments for flood control. This is the formula of the Columbia River Treaty (signed 1961, in force 1964), forty years early.
- **Dependence runs both ways.** BC's revenue is tied to Pacifica's grid and Pacifica's power to BC's storage, with Pacifica the larger partner.
- **Salmon.** CR-055's rule (no dam that salmon cannot pass) keeps the upper Columbia runs alive into BC, including the Sinixt and Ktunaxa fisheries.
- **Floods across the border.** A Kootenay dam in Pacifican Montana that would flood into BC, and the Seattle board's Skagit dams that would flood BC's Skagit valley, go ahead only with BC's consent and compensation.
- **Model:** the effect on CR-055's firm power is OPEN for the energy workbook.

### NA-11 — Fish and seals (APPROVED)

- **Fur seals.**
  - Pacifica owns the Pribilofs (Alaska, 1867) and makes no closed-sea claim of the kind the United States lost at the Paris arbitration of 1893. Instead it proposes a conservation regime early (date PROPOSED: the 1890s) and seats Canadian delegates at the table.
  - The four-power convention follows the 1911 formula: pelagic sealing is banned and Canada receives 15% of the Pribilof catch, 15% of the Commander Islands catch and 10% of the Robben Island catch.
  - From 1921 the Commanders are Pacifican too (CR-031), and Robben Island is Japanese, so all of Canada's shares are paid by Pacifica and its Concord partner.
- **Fraser sockeye.** The NA-8 commission regulates the shared runs in the Strait of Juan de Fuca. After the Hells Gate rockslide of 1913–14, caused by the Canadian Northern's railway construction, fishways are built at once, not from 1944 as historically.
- **Halibut, 1923.** Canada's first treaty signed without a British co-signature is the Pacific Halibut Treaty, signed with Pacifica (historically with the United States). The coasts involved are Pacifican Alaska and Washington.

### NA-13 — Public power (APPROVED, one line)

Ontario Hydro (1906, Adam Beck) and Pacifica's public power estate each prove the other's case against the Union's private utilities.

  - Kansas.
  - East of the divide: Salt Creek and Teapot Dome in Wyoming, and Cat Creek and Kevin-Sunburst in Montana.
  - Small amounts from Colorado's Front Range and Michigan.
- **Gone to the Union's neighbours:** Oklahoma (Sequoyah, in the Gulf Republic), Texas, Louisiana, Arkansas and California.
- **1929 supply:**
  - the Gulf Republic's non-trust crude, about 270m bbl (45% of the Gulf's 600m, CR-020);
  - Venezuela, about 110m (of 137.5m);
  - Mexico, about 25m (of 44.7m);
  - Peru and Colombia together, about 15m (Colombia produced 20.4m, most of it bound through the canal or to the Gulf);
  - its own fields, about 110m (1929, Bureau of Mines: Kansas 40.7m, Wyoming 19.1m, Pennsylvania 11.8m, Kentucky 7.8m, Ohio 6.7m, Illinois 6.3m, West Virginia 5.6m, Montana 3.8m, and smaller states).
- **The figures are rough.** Historical state output is scaled by population. A "Union fuel" row in the financial workbook should replace them (OPEN).

The Union stays a coal civilisation (Appalachia, the Illinois basin) and Pacifica an oil one. That contrast should be stated in the storyline.

### NA-12a — Standard Oil (APPROVED)

Standard survives as a giant refinery with no crude:
- **Its export trade dies.** Asia goes to the trust, and Europe to Russian and Gulf oil.
- **Its fields run down.** Illinois saves it for a decade, then declines.
- **It is barred from the Gulf's wells and pipelines** by the Republic's oil law (NA-12f).

By about 1910 it is no longer a monopoly. It is the Union's weakness concentrated in one company: refineries at Whiting, Bayonne and Bayway buying foreign crude at arm's length. Roosevelt keeps it whole (CR-020 B13; no 1911 dissolution), because there is no monopoly left to break. Instead he makes it the Union's instrument abroad, with Navy contracts and state-backed diplomacy in Mexico, Venezuela, Peru (International Petroleum, through Imperial Oil), Romania, the Dutch Indies and Mesopotamia. This is Britain's 1914 stake in Anglo-Persian, done the Union's way. It still buys Imperial Oil of Ontario (1898).

**Cracking.** The Burton process (Whiting, 1913) remains a Union invention, now with a sharper motive.

### NA-12b — The Gulf Republic's crude (APPROVED)

The roughly 45% of Gulf crude the trust does not refine is sold by the Republic's independents (a Texas Company and its peers) mostly to the Union, though not exclusively. It moves by Mississippi barge, by common-carrier pipeline from Sequoyah into Kansas and Illinois, and by sea. **The Union's largest supplier is Pacifica's ally.**

### NA-12c — Huasteca's contracts (APPROVED)

Huasteca's heavy Tampico crude goes on long contracts to the Union's Atlantic fuel-oil market and its navy. Historically about three-quarters of Mexico's exports went to the United States in 1921; that its heavy crude went mainly to the Atlantic coast as fuel oil is plausible but unconfirmed (to verify). The contracts have no political termination clause (NA-12d). The crude does not enter the Realm's energy balance; its earnings enter income from abroad in the financial workbook (OPEN).

### NA-12d — The doctrine: never embargo (APPROVED)

The Union fills about four-fifths of its oil needs from abroad. That is Pacifica's strongest lever, but only while it stays unpulled. A great power cut off from oil doesn't give in; it fights, as the US embargo of 1941 led to Pearl Harbor. The doctrine has four parts:
1. **No embargo in peacetime crises.** Pacifica never cuts the Union's oil to win a dispute, and it holds the Gulf Republic to the same line.
2. **No political escape clauses.** Pacifican supply contracts, Huasteca's above all, cannot be cancelled for political reasons. This mirrors Ottawa keeping the Great Bear contract in 1927 (NA-7); Pacifica's reputation for keeping contracts is the same asset everywhere.
3. **More than one seller.** Pacifica does not try to corner the Union's sources. The Union always has supply that neither Pacifica nor the Gulf Republic controls (NA-12g: Venezuela).
4. **The payoff.** A Union with secure oil has oil importers and Wall Street lobbying for peace (CR-020's foreign-creditor beats). The dependence works because the lever is never pulled. In a real war, the doctrine ends.

### NA-12e — Guffey Petroleum (APPROVED)

Gulf Oil was born at Spindletop, which Pacifican capital reached first (CR-020). Mellon's company is therefore **J. M. Guffey Petroleum** of Pittsburgh. As historically, it is organised in 1901 with the Mellons as its main shareholders; here it is built on Kansas and Illinois instead of Spindletop, then on Venezuela, and it buys the Barco concession in Colombia (NA-12g). Its Venezuelan company is OPEN: historically the Mene Grande Oil Company was organised in 1925, and Gulf acquired it only in 1936.

### NA-12f — The Gulf Republic's oil law, with the 55% ceiling (APPROVED)

This resolves the contradiction between "the Gulf Republic's antitrust laws keep Standard Oil out" and the trust refining 55% of the Gulf's crude (CR-020 B9).
- **The historical anchor.** Texas's antitrust law of 1889 was used to expel Standard's hidden affiliate Waters-Pierce in 1900 (upheld by the US Supreme Court that year) and again in 1907, with a fine of $1,623,500 (affirmed in 1909), for secret combination and price agreements, not for size. The common-carrier pipeline law is of 20 February 1917.
- **The Republic's law.**
  - It bans secret affiliates, rebates and price agreements.
  - From the 1900 compact, it separates the stages of the business. No refiner may own more than a minority of producing wells. Trunk pipelines are common carriers at published tariffs, as Texas historically enacted in 1917.
  - **No refiner may refine more than 55% of the Republic's crude.** The ceiling is written into the 1900 compact, so the figure is a treaty limit rather than an accident.
- **How the trust fits.** It owns refineries, tankers and canal terminals, holds minority stakes in fields, and buys crude at posted prices. It earns its share by being the best buyer, because it has the canal and the Asian markets. It sits at the ceiling.
- **Why Standard is out.** Its method was owning fields and pipelines through hidden affiliates, which is exactly what the law forbids. Like anyone else, it may buy crude at posted prices, which is what NA-12a has it doing.
- **The tension stays.** The trust's 55% offends the spirit of the law. Gulf populists, Huey Long among them, attack the ceiling as a foreign quota.

### NA-12g — The Caribbean understanding, 1921–23 (APPROVED)

- **The problem.** Colombia keeps Panama in this timeline, because the canal is neutralised under Colombian sovereignty and run by Pacifica's company (CR-028). Its natural partners are Pacifica and the Gulf Republic, and the Union has no Panama debt to pay (historically $25m in 1921). The Gulf Republic resents Union companies in the Caribbean for two reasons:
  - **Commercial:** Caribbean crude competes with its exports to the Union.
  - **Strategic:** with Pensacola and Puerto Rico, a Union presence on the Caribbean mainland would encircle the Gulf.
- **Venezuela, the Union's source.** Venezuela lies across the Caribbean from Puerto Rico, and Gómez wants many bidders. Standard, Guffey and Shell lead there, and the trust keeps its Lago minority (CR-053 P1). **Lago in 1925 (PROPOSED, follows from NA-12d and NA-12g):** historically Lago's buyer at the end of 1925 was Pan American, Doheny's own holding company, which Standard of Indiana bought the same year. Here Doheny's company is Pacifican and cannot be sold abroad (the champions' golden share), and Thalassia bars it from taking Lago, because Venezuela must stay the Union's source. Standard buys Lago directly, and Doheny is overruled a second time. The Gulf Republic earns from servicing the boom: its drilling crews, the Hughes bit and Texan roughnecks. This is the Union's supply outside the Gulf's control (NA-12d, point 3).
- **Colombia, the Gulf Republic's and Pacifica's.**
  - In 1920 **the Texas Company** buys Tropical Oil (the De Mares concession) instead of Standard's International Petroleum, with the trust's minority unchanged (CR-053 P2). Historically the Texas Company did enter Colombia later: it bought the Barco concession with Socony-Vacuum in 1936.
  - Guffey buys the Barco concession in 1926 as the Union's one toehold, so Colombia is not closed to the Union. As historically, Colombia revokes it the same year and restores it in 1931, so the toehold is real but insecure.
  - Most of Cartagena's crude moves through the canal; the Texas Company sells some of the rest to the Union.
- **The market sorts itself out.** Caribbean and Mexican heavy crude supplies fuel oil to the Atlantic seaboard (plausible historically; to verify). Gulf and Sequoyah crude goes by pipeline and barge to Midwest refineries. So the Gulf Republic keeps the Midwest, and the competition is smaller than it fears.
- **No bases.** The Cuban principle of 1898 (canon: no foreign bases) is extended to the Caribbean mainland, and Pacifica is bound by it too. Its canal rights in Panama and Nicaragua are not bases. Union companies may own oil on the mainland; the Union Navy may not station ships there. The Union's existing bases at Puerto Rico and Pensacola are untouched.
- **What Pacifica gains:**
  - It becomes the arbiter, owed by both the Union and the Gulf Republic.
  - Colombia moves into its orbit.
  - Its Venezuelan minority gives it information and crude.
  - The Gulf Republic's service income stays within Pacifica's circle.
  - Colombian crude bound for the Pacific pays canal tolls.
  - The Caribbean is demilitarised, which suits a power whose leverage is money, not bases.
  - The Union's oil is secure, so the Union is less dangerous.

### NA-12h — Story consequences (APPROVED)

- **The Gulf Republic has two customers.** Its independents depend on the Union and want good relations with it. That makes the Republic a bridge, and it adds a quiet strain inside the partnership over the trust's 55%.
- **The Union Navy converts to oil late.** It goes oil-fired around 1916 instead of 1912, stockpiles at its Wyoming reserves (Teapot Dome, Salt Creek), and its fleet stays tied to the Atlantic and the Caribbean.
- **The Union has rational reasons to be assertive in the Caribbean.** Its oil is there. This is the non-fanatic Union of NA-3, again.
- **A Union–German coal-oil axis.** In 1927 Standard licenses IG Farben's process for making oil from coal, as Standard of New Jersey historically did in 1927–29, but here out of necessity. It fits canon's Union loan book in Germany (CR-020 B6).
- **Mexico, 1927.** Article 27 and the Union's need for oil make intervention tempting, in the same year as the confrontation with Britain. Pacifica defuses it with the Huasteca supply contracts and a Morrow-style settlement (Dwight Morrow, ambassador from 29 October 1927; the Calles–Morrow settlement of 1927–28, with Mexico's oil-law amendment of December 1927). Wall Street's peace lobby backs it.
- **Canada.** Ontario and Quebec hydro sold to the Union is a second energy lever over the Union, held by a Canada in Pacifica's orbit (NA-13).

## Part IV — Villa, the Gulf and Patton (1910–1917)

This part answers the "Villa question" CR-051 left for the military CR, and the circumstances of Patton's 1916 raid that CR-044 C5 left OPEN. Canon already fixes the frame: New Mexico and Arizona are Pacifican, Texas belongs to the Gulf Republic, Pacifica is at war with Germany from 23 August 1914, and the Columbus raid happens only if Pacifica does what Wilson did, recognising Carranza and letting his troops cross its railways to Agua Prieta (CR-051). In this answer it does, for its own reasons, and Patton's distinction comes from fighting Villa's bands, not Mexico.

### MX-1 — Waiting, then recognising Carranza (APPROVED)

- **1910–1915.** Pacifica protects its nationals and property and backs no faction (CR-051 C5: a border force after the Magonista rising on the Baja border in 1911; the navy at Guaymas and Mazatlán in 1913–15).
- **1913.** It refuses to recognise Huerta after Madero's murder in February 1913.
- **The Sonorans.** Its natural partners are the men next door: Obregón, Calles and Adolfo de la Huerta, whose world already runs on Pacifican copper, the Guaymas railway and the Sonora customs.
- **1915.** Once Obregón has broken Villa at Celaya (April 1915), the winner is clear. In October 1915 Pacifica and the Gulf Republic recognise Carranza together, with the Sonorans as Pacifica's real partners.
- **No arms.** Recognition carries rail transit (MX-4), not weapons. Pacifica's rule of no arms to either side in a civil war (CR-048 A2) stands; NA-1's rifles for Juárez went to a recognised government fighting a foreign intervention.

### MX-2 — The German hand (APPROVED)

Pacifica has been at war with Germany since August 1914, and German agents are working Mexico.
- **Huerta's comeback.** On 27 June 1915 Huerta is arrested at Newman, New Mexico, just north of El Paso, on his way to a counter-revolution financed with German money. Historically US agents arrested him; here Pacifican border agents do. Orozco escapes and is killed on 30 August by a posse of ranchers, deputies and soldiers in Culberson County, in the Gulf Republic. How large and direct the German role was is argued by historians (Katz, von Feilitzsch); here Pacifica's intelligence takes it as proven.
- **Villa's arms buyer.** Felix A. Sommerfeld was Villa's main arms buyer north of the border and also worked for the German naval attaché, Karl Boy-Ed. Pacifica's wartime intelligence knows it, so Villa's supply line now runs through the enemy.

### MX-3 — The Plan de San Diego in the Gulf Republic (APPROVED)

- The Plan de San Diego is dated 6 January 1915. It called on Mexican Americans, African Americans, Native Americans and Japanese Americans to rise in Texas, New Mexico, Arizona, Colorado and California. Its raids, from July to September 1915, fall on south Texas, which is the Gulf Republic's. Here its appeal to the Japanese reads in Thalassia as a German provocation aimed at the Japanese partnership, and Japanese Pacificans ignore it.
- Historically the Texas Rangers and vigilantes killed Tejanos in reprisal: federal estimates say more than 300, and the 1919 Canales inquiry gave anything from 300 to 5,000. The Gulf answers with courts instead: it prosecutes vigilantes and keeps its homicide numbers honest, as it does with the levee camps in 1927 (canon §25).
- Historians argue that Carranza sponsored or tolerated the raids as leverage for recognition, and they slowed sharply once he had it, though they revived in 1916. That gives the Gulf its own reason to join the recognition of October 1915.

### MX-4 — The transit and Agua Prieta (APPROVED)

- General Benjamín Hill's troops cross from Piedras Negras to Eagle Pass and ride on Gulf rails, then through New Mexico and Arizona on Pacifican rails, to Douglas, to reinforce Calles. Historically some sources start the trains at Laredo or El Paso instead. The Gulf Republic is party to the decision because the transit starts on its lines (CR-051).
- Villa's attack on Agua Prieta fails on 1–3 November 1915; the main night assault comes in the early hours of 2 November.
- Historically Villa blamed searchlights that blinded his men and believed they were American. The sources still disagree: most put them on the Mexican side, one says US Army engineers supplied them, and Agua Prieta's electricity came from Douglas. Here that would be Pacifican current. Nobody ever settles it.

### MX-5 — Villa's revenge on Pacifica (APPROVED)

**Why Pacifica and not the Gulf.**
- El Paso was Villa's commercial capital, his family lived there, and the Gulf's Tejanos are his public.
- Pacifica is the paymaster behind Carranza.
- Pacifica is also the power that took California in 1847 and bought Baja in 1867 (CR-028: "Thorne knew"). Villa's Naco manifesto of November 1915 accuses Carranza of selling Mexico to the foreigner who paid for his victory. Here the foreigner is Pacifica, and the manifesto says the Thornes now want Sonora. Many Mexicans believe it.

**What he does.**
- **Santa Isabel, 10 January 1916.** Pablo López's men stop a train in Chihuahua carrying the staff of a reopening mine at Cusihuiriachi (historically ASARCO's; here a Pacifican company) and shoot them. Historically about eighteen died, seventeen of them Americans; one, Thomas Holmes, survived. Here the dead are Pacifican engineers, Carquinez graduates.
- **Columbus, 9 March 1916.** He raids Columbus, New Mexico, a lightly held Pacifican border town where he believed a merchant, Sam Ravel, had cheated him. Historically about eighteen Americans died, soldiers and civilians, though counts run from 15 to 19.

### MX-6 — The 90-day expedition (APPROVED)

Thalassia reads the raid as a German trap meant to tie Pacifica down in Chihuahua, so it does the opposite of an occupation:
- **Mexico's consent first.** The legal basis is a reciprocal pursuit clause in the Mexican convention that ended the scalp bounties after the Apache peace of 1865 (canon). The clause itself is PROPOSED. Its real model is the US–Mexico agreement of 29 July 1882, which let regular troops cross unpopulated border country in pursuit of "savage hostile Indians". Carranza depends on Pacifican credit and fears a Union-style occupation, so he consents formally. Historically he gave only grudging, informal consent, and no crossing protocol was ever finalised.
- **Mexican liaison.** Obregón's War Ministry attaches officers to the columns. Historically Pershing's men fought Carranza's own troops at Parral (12 April 1916) and Carrizal (21 June) and nearly started a war; with liaison, that never happens.
- **A 90-day limit, kept to the day.** The expedition withdraws on schedule whether or not Villa is caught.
- **The Gulf pact's first use.** A Gulf cavalry brigade rides with the expedition under the 1900 mutual defence pact, and supply runs by rail through El Paso to the Columbus railhead.
- **Command.** Frederick Funston, already Pacifican in canon (CR-051), commands in the field (MX-11).
- **Pacifica's edge.** Its motor trucks, Holt tractors and aircraft work where Pershing's eight Curtiss JN-3s, the US Army's entire air force, were defeated by the Sierra Madre and wrecked by the end of April.
- **The result.** Villa is not caught. His Columbus bands are broken, and Candelario Cervantes, one of the raid's leaders, is killed on 25 May, as historically.

### MX-7 — Patton at San Miguelito (APPROVED)

- On 14 May 1916, at the San Miguelito ranch near Rubio, Chihuahua, Patton leads three touring cars with about ten soldiers, plus civilian guides and drivers, and kills Julio Cárdenas, commander of Villa's Dorado bodyguard, and two other men. He straps the bodies to the bonnets.
- It is the first motorised raid in his service's history (CR-044 C5); historically it is called the first US motorised military action. The historical cars are widely reported as Dodges; here they are Pacifican-built.
- He is Funston's aide, as he historically lobbied his way onto Pershing's staff.
- The lesson he takes from the raid (speed, engines and nerve) is what puts him on the armoured trains in 1918 and at Kotelnich in 1920 (canon).

### MX-10 — The size of the expedition (APPROVED)

About 6,000 men in all. Historically Pershing had about 4,800 in the field at first, and more than 10,000 men were committed at the peak, counting the base at Columbus.
- **The Pacifican column, about 4,000:**
  - a cavalry brigade of two regiments;
  - a battalion of truck-borne infantry holding the supply line from Columbus;
  - an aero squadron of about a dozen machines;
  - a motor train of about 150 trucks with Holt tractors. Historically the Army ordered 147 four-wheel-drive trucks for the expedition.
- **The Gulf brigade, about 2,000:** two cavalry regiments. One is raised in south Texas from Tejanos who sat the Defense Law test in Spanish, as canon allows (§25). Villa and the Plan de San Diego both called on Tejanos to rise; instead they ride against Villa as citizens of the Gulf Republic.
- **Why smaller works:**
  - the mission is narrow: break the bands that raided Columbus, not occupy Chihuahua;
  - Mexican liaison officers supply the intelligence Pershing never had;
  - the aircraft do the scouting.
- Amounts MODEL.

### MX-11 — Funston in the field (APPROVED)

- The expedition is small and lasts 90 days, so the border commander leads it in person. Historically Funston urged an immediate pursuit into Mexico, and Wilson gave the command to Pershing, who commanded the district at Fort Bliss.
- It suits a man who made his name in the field, in the Philippines in canon.
- Patton, his aide, rides at headquarters, which makes the San Miguelito errand natural. Historically it was a corn-buying trip.
- **The southern border command sits at Tucson (decided 6 November, author's delegation).**
  - Tucson lies on the main line from California to New Mexico, midway along the Mexican border, with the telegraph and the university.
  - Fort Huachuca, the cavalry post in the hills south-east of the city, is its field base.
  - Columbus is the expedition's railhead.
  - El Paso and San Antonio are the Gulf Republic's, so a Pacifican command has no business there. Historically the Southern Department sat at San Antonio.
- **The cost.** The Sierra Madre is hard on a man with a weak heart. Canon keeps his death in February 1917 (CR-051), so Chihuahua is his last campaign.
  - He dies of a heart attack in the Santa Rita Hotel at Tucson, the town's grand hotel since 1 February 1904, instead of the St. Anthony at San Antonio. Historically he lay in state first at the Alamo; here there is no Alamo stop.
  - His body goes home by rail to San Francisco, the city he held together after the earthquake of 1906 (canon: the army leads the relief). He lies in state at City Hall and is buried at the Presidio, as historically.
  - The day is 19 February 1917, as historically, unless the author moves it.

### MX-12 — The Columbus garrison (APPROVED, officer unnamed)

- **The regiment.** A squadron of the 1st Border Cavalry, the regular regiment of the Border Force raised after the Magonista rising on the Baja border in 1911 (CR-051), headquartered at Columbus.
- **Its strength.** Its roll is about 350 men, close to the historical 13th Cavalry's 353, and as historically about half are out on patrol on the night of 9 March.
- **Its men.** Many troopers are Hispanos from New Mexico and Arizona, from counties whose official language is Spanish. That makes the liaison with Obregón's officers work, and it gives the raid a bitter edge: Villa's men are fighting Spanish-speakers on both sides of the line.
- **The pursuit.** The squadron's officer, unnamed, pursues into Mexico at once. Historically Major Frank Tompkins took two troops about fifteen miles south that day, fought the rearguard four times and turned back short of ammunition and water.

### What Part IV does for the story

- **Mexico learns that Pacifica keeps its word.** It came with consent, fought only Villa's bands, and left on day 90. That builds trust with the Sonorans, who rule from 1920 (Obregón, then Calles), and it is the groundwork for defusing the Mexico crisis of 1927 (NA-12h).
- **Germany's trap fails.** The German offer to Washington of January 1917, already in canon, becomes Germany's second attempt to use America against Pacifica after Mexico didn't work.
- **The Gulf partnership is tested and holds.** The two allies decide the transit together and fight together under the pact.
- **An honest cost.** Villa is never caught. He surrenders at Sabinas on 28 July 1920 under Adolfo de la Huerta's amnesty, takes the Canutillo hacienda, and is murdered at Parral on 20 July 1923, as historically, with Pacifica staying out. The ballads sing of his raid on the *californios*, and in Mexican popular memory Pacifica remains the neighbour who took California. That is a counterweight to the elite friendship.

Not taken: MX-8 (Thalassia ordering Huasteca to stop paying the Tampico warlord Peláez). MX-9 was a process choice, where to record this part; the answer is Part IV of this CR.
