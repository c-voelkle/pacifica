## What this CR does

**The outcome.** Oregon is never a state of the Union. It is occupied in 1861 as a territory, governs itself under a civil compact, and in October 1866 chooses by its own vote to join Pacifica. The 1867 treaty transfers the United States' title. Because Oregon was never admitted, the Senate cedes a territory, not a state (Nevada's case is CT9).

**The causal chain, made explicit on the page.** On 12 February 1859 the House of Representatives historically passed Oregon's admission by 114 to 103. Here it fails, 106 to 109. Every lost vote traces to California's independence or to California's Oregon policy:
- Two of the historical yeas are California's own members, who do not exist here.
- Two Mississippi Democrats who meant to stand by Lane vote no. Kansas has been held to the English bill's population ratio since its voters refused the English terms in August 1858, and the free states took the Senate's lead only in May, after eight years of the parity California's absence preserved. They will not admit a second free state at half the ratio.
- Two Ohio Republicans, leaving the House in March, follow John Sherman's security argument. Sherman builds it from Pacifica's renewal note and his brother's letters: a state beside a foreign treasury that has shown how a legislature leaves.
- Two Maine Republicans from the shipping coast follow the same argument for their own reasons: Oregon's trade already moves in bond through San Francisco, and the Superintendency's paid passages are winning the race for settlers.

The right argument, "admit Oregon to bind it," is made on the floor by Hughes, Thayer and Stephens, as historically. It loses because the man asking is Joseph Lane, the Lecompton man.

**Why Oregon chooses Pacifica.**
- **The exclusion clause.** The Union's 1861 Enabling Act forbids it, and Senate Republicans refuse to drop the condition. Pacifica's 1856 Articles leave residence and the franchise to the member states, a power Hawaii demanded, so Oregon may keep a limited clause. Exclusion is the occasion.
- **Self-government is the argument.** Salem's Douglas Democrats get the popular sovereignty Douglas died defending: an entrenched charter that cannot be changed without Oregon's own consent.
- **The Idaho trade.** Idaho is Pacifican before Oregon votes: in fact from the Walla Walla convention of summer 1862 and the mining compacts of 1863, in the treaty signed in 1865 (ratified 1867), and by its own consent in June 1866. A Union Oregon would be an exclave with its hinterland behind a customs line.
- **Five years of evidence.** The compact's record and Pacifica's gold make the case.

**Who is party.** The Native nations whose lands, treaties or fishing places lie in Oregon are parties to the accession, not delegates at Salem. Their inclusion costs Pacifica votes in the south and gains some in the east.

**Working result (MODEL):** accession by about 51 to 49, within a range of about 50–53 depending on which optional levers the author adopts. The model is calibrated on the historical 1866 governor's race: 20,355 votes, with Woods (Union party) beating Kelly (Democrat) 10,316 to 10,039, a margin of 277. Accession needs to hold nearly all of the Democrats' 49.3 percent and win one to three points from voters who historically voted with the Union party.

## Part I — California's Oregon policy, 1848–1858 (P1–P11)

**Four structural facts by 1859, all canon:**

| | Fact | Bearing on Oregon's admission |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | California never joins the Union (CR-028) | Two House members and two senators, Democrats who historically voted for Oregon, do not exist |
| S2 | No free state is admitted in 1850 | Senate parity of fifteen to fifteen lasts until Minnesota in May 1858; Oregon would be the second free state in nine months |
| S3 | The Confederation of 1856: a sovereign legislature, Hawaii's, voted itself into a Pacific union | The "Pacific republic" has a capital, a treasury, a conscript army and a precedent for accession by a legislature's vote |
| S4 | The Union's only sovereign Pacific harbours are in Oregon and Washington; its squadron provisions at Sausalito under a revocable privilege expiring in 1860 | The Columbia and Puget Sound are strategic as they never were historically |

**The policy.** It is commerce and nothing more (P1–P5). P5 is load-bearing. Querétaro, the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention and the sectional lock depend on Washington believing the Republic covets nothing north of the 42nd parallel. The moves of P7–P11 are therefore all private, commercial or open to anyone. Washington sees business, not policy.

**Who decides, and when.**
- **Until February 1859: restraint.** P1–P4 and P7 are general policy, not aimed at Oregon. Every miner gets the same licence and coinage, every examinee the same examination, every emigrant family the same passage.
- **February 1859: the turn.** Sherman's speech tells Washington what P5 hid, and Buchanan's demands in the 1859–60 renewal talks follow (canon). Elias concludes that restraint no longer buys Washington's trust. He changes nothing in public. Private men move first: Ralston's stakes in the OSN (P8) and the merchants' fund (P9) are their own ventures, which Elias knows of and does not discourage. If adopted, the Hudson's Bay Company rights (P10) and the scrip (P11) are his own decision, carried out through the Superintendency's London and San Francisco correspondents.
- **From May 1861: the Protector's policy.** W5–W10 and I1–I11 are carried out by the occupation command and the Confederal Treasury.
- **1865–66: the Confederal government.** V7, V12, V14, V16 and I11 are its levers. The push in the House (V7 option) would go through Pacifica's chargé in Washington (name OPEN).

**The levers and their purposes:**

| Lever | Effect by 1866 |
|---|---|
| L1 (P2) | A generation of Oregon men who made their stake under Californian courts; Oregon's money is Californian |
| L2 (P7) | Oregon's young surveyors, engineers, doctors and lawyers trained in Pacifica, among them some of the convention's younger delegates. Any named scholar must be a historical person |
| L3 (P8) | The OSN's board and Ladd's bank have Pacifican partners at the table |
| L5 (P9) | Blunts the clergy's fear of a "Catholic, Mormon republic" |
| L4 (P10, option) | Fort Vancouver, Cowlitz and Nisqually in friendly hands in 1861; French Prairie's old HBC families have a Pacifican-linked landlord. Risk: Washington objects if it is traced |
| L8 (P11, option) | The 1861 act appropriated less than half of what was claimed and paid in bonds after audit (Hist.), so in this timeline Oregon's claimants hold three kinds of paper: audited claims awaiting bonds, disallowed claims, and the few bonds issued before the war. Brokers buy the first two at a discount. Issued bonds are federal securities and give no lever. By 1866 most unsettled claims sit in San Francisco, and the Union's promise to complete the act pays Pacifican holders. In 1867 they are set off against the cession price. **Tension with CR-033's 1851 refusal** (CT5). Historically the scrip traded at 12½–37½ cents before the act |

### Who changed their mind and why

**Barksdale and McRae: the ratio and the balance.** The Senate passed Oregon in May 1858, a week after Minnesota and before Kansas voted in August to refuse the English bill's terms (Hist.). From then on, the English bill's population ratio is the South's only hold on Kansas. Admitting Oregon at half that ratio would break it, and would give the free states a lead of two, nine months after the parity that California's absence had kept since 1850. The argument is already on the floor, historically: Millson of Virginia says the English bill gave Kansas notice, so the ratio binds from now on, and Hill of Georgia says he would put Oregon "upon the same ground" as Kansas. Historically it moved few Democrats, because the balance was already lost; here it is not lost yet. Barksdale and McRae (McRae newly seated in Quitman's place) came meaning to stand by Lane. Sherman's speech makes Oregon a question of loyalty, not party: a state whose purse is in Monterey is no sure pair of Democratic votes. They vote with the Alabama and South Carolina members. Lamar, who had needled Millson about Kansas, votes yes and is left arguing alone in the delegation. *Link: S1, S2, H4.*

Stephens answers this in his speech of 12 February as historically: the South's security rests "upon principle, rather than upon numbers", and the balance "is already gone", with "no prospect of its ever being regained". Here the second claim is false, and Barksdale knows it: without California the balance is still even. Stephens's historical precedent, that Florida and Texas came in without a census ("So was California"), loses its last example.

**Horton and Leiter: security.** John Sherman, who historically did not vote, makes an argument nobody could make historically:
- He moves for the correspondence. Buchanan, who signed the 1848 convention, sends it.
- He reads the renewal note's schedule of Oregon produce and Astoria port charges to the House.
- He reads his brother's letters: a republic that drills every young man, coins Oregon's money, carried Portland's banks in 1855 and in 1856 took a kingdom into its confederation by that kingdom's own vote.
- Lane advised Oregon in 1858 to act as a state without waiting for Congress. Admit it now and he gets a legislature, a governor and a militia beside a foreign treasury that holds Oregon's purse and has shown how a legislature leaves.
- A territory is governed from Washington. Keep Oregon one until it has a constitution Congress can accept, and Republicans expect to win in 1860.

As members leaving Congress, the two owe Oregon's Democrats nothing. Their colleague Nichols, also leaving, says on 10 February what he said historically: admission should not take "a purely partisan cast", and Ohio, Indiana and Illinois have their own laws against free Black immigrants. He votes yes and loses. *Link: S3, S4, P1–P3, P6, William Sherman in Pacifica (canon §8).*

**Wood and Foster: the coast and the settlers.** Sherman's last paper is the Superintendency's passenger returns. Oregon's donation claims closed in 1855, and Pacifica is still paying passages and granting married couples land on Oregon's own terms without a colour bar. Its produce moves in bond through San Francisco, and its lumber and spars go to the Republic's yards. For two members from Maine's shipping coast this is a commercial fact, not a theory: Oregon is already in Monterey's trade, and a state would be a port of that trade with two senators. Fill Oregon with free labour first, then admit it. *Link: P2, P4.*

**Thayer: the argument that loses.** Thayer, the man who won Kansas by emigration, gives his historical speech of 11 February (Globe pp. 975–977). He answers his colleague Dawes, who cannot see "how an honest Representative" of Massachusetts can vote for Oregon. He calls Oregon's people "the pilgrims of the Pacific coast" and says they will "contaminate this Confederacy" just as much "as their mountain streams will contaminate the Pacific ocean". He has "no faith" in government by "a non-resident Government" over Anglo-Saxons "anywhere". Here that line is the whole case of 1866 stated seven years early, and in 1866 Oregon's papers reprint it. *Link: V-section, the campaign.*

## Part III — Deadlock and the Enabling Act, 1859–1861 (E1–E3)

- **The limbo becomes permanent.** Oregon keeps a governor-elect, a legislature elected in 1858 and two senators-designate, none with an office. The state legislature's cancelled session of December 1858 (Hist.) is never revived.
- **The 36th Congress** deadlocks (E1). The Carson Convention of autumn 1860 renews only the commercial articles (canon). Buchanan's demand for a stronger Sausalito privilege, which follows Sherman's reading of the correspondence, is refused.
- **Lane** is Breckinridge's running mate in 1860 (Hist.), now as the man whose state was refused.
- **The Enabling Act (E2).** The departure of the Southern senators clears the way. The act is a standing statute: it remains federal law through the war and is the legal basis of the Union's offer in 1866.

## Part IV — War and the compact, 1861–1865 (W1–W12)

**The electorate in 1861.** The occupation matters politically because of who Oregon's voters are:
- many Southern-born Democrats who hate Republican Washington more than they love slavery;
- commercial Portland, which depends on Monterey's credit;
- Union Democrats (Bush, Nesmith) and a small Republican party, who oppose the occupation.

These are canon §10's three factions, and they are the electorate of 1866.

**Whiteaker's line in §10 keeps its words.** He "negotiates civil protections without conceding a governor's right to transfer Oregon's allegiance," but he is governor-elect of a state never admitted. He has no allegiance to transfer.

**The levers W5–W10** are the occupation's argument. The most important is **W7 together with V2**:
- From 1863 thousands of Missouri refugees, Confederate deserters and draft evaders came west on the Oregon Trail (Hist.).
- Those who settle the Grande Ronde, Powder River and the Willamette before spring 1865 are U.S. citizens, so they are electors.
- They vote against Washington. They are part of Oregon's historical wartime growth and already inside the Democrats' 49.3 percent of 1866, not an addition to it. **The lever is defensive:** the armistice date keeps them on the rolls, where Washington's 1861 rolls would have struck them off. How many settled in Oregon and could vote is unquantified (research task 25; the NPS study *Overlanders in the Columbia River Gorge, 1840–1870* has the migration table).

**W6 has a sneaky edge.** The officials who will run the 1866 polls are paid from Oregon's own customs. The Union will object. Pacifica answers that it is Oregon's money, and the protocol's observers and the returns commission answer for the count.

**The irony to show, not explain.** Pacifica shelters white men fleeing a draft in 1863 and returns Black men fleeing slavery from 1871.

**Why the Missouri route fails.** Canon (CR-015 H4) says it is "cut in wartime." The mechanism:
- Fort Benton is a seasonal riverhead 2,000 miles from St Louis.
- The Lakota wars of 1862–64 make the upper river dangerous (Hist.).
- Halleck needs every regiment in the East.
- The Coeur d'Alene and Spokane refuse Union troops passage (I5).

### The chain in brief

1. The Lewiston lease (I1).
2. The interior concentration winters on Wright's stores in 1861–62, and cannot buy or carry in a second winter's supply in 1862 (I2).
3. The 1862 recovery attempt on the lower Columbia fails (canon §10).
4. The Walla Walla convention (I3).
5. The Boise Basin rush inside Pacifica's economy (I6). Idaho City reaches about 7,000 people in 1864, the largest town in the Northwest, bigger than Portland (Hist.).

8. Unconditional transfer (I9).

**Why Elias tolerates Wallace.** In the moment, the reasons are legal. Idaho's camps are supplied, not occupied. The Basin's Southern Democrats have their own reason to use the Union's organic act: it lets them govern themselves against Lincoln's appointees. ## Part VI — The question, the offers and the vote, 1865–1866 (V1–V17)

### Three bodies frame the vote

| Layer | Body | Decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The treaty's Oregon article, signed late 1865 | What the vote means; the threshold; the electorate; the protocol (V1–V4) |
| 2 | The convention of the 1861 delegates | The ballot's text, the separate article and the note (V5–V6) |
| 3 | The voters | Pacifica or the United States |

**Why Washington wants a vote at all.** A vote lets the Union lose Oregon without signing it away. Senators can tell their voters that Oregon left by its own choice.

**Elias's price.** He accepts the risk of a vote on terms. If Oregon votes for the United States, it stays a U.S. territory but neutral and demilitarized: no U.S. naval station, fortification or garrison beyond a customs guard; the Columbia's navigation free to Pacifica; and a smaller vendor credit (MODEL). The Union would keep a commercial port it could not use for war, so canon's "Union without a Pacific port" (C-1867-WESTERN-LINE) holds because Oregon accedes. The lower price also gives Washington its own reason to accept a vote it may lose.

**What "remain a territory" means.** It is not on the ballot. It is what a Union vote means for anyone who also wants exclusion, because Congress would reject that constitution. The ballot note makes this visible.

### Why the Union cannot match the offer (V7)

Senate Republicans kill the House's amendment for three reasons:
- **Principle:** Sumner, Wade and Bingham's cause.
- **Party:** two more Democratic senators.
- **Riddance:** some would rather lose a Democratic exclusion state than admit one. John Sherman's 1859 argument becomes "let Oregon choose."

A further charge for Oregon's Democrats: the Union that asks them to admit free Black people still keeps slaves in Kentucky and Delaware, because the Thirteenth Amendment failed (canon §12; border-state status to confirm, open decision 38).

**The 1860 county returns matter (Hist.).** The Willamette was not one Douglas bloc. Marion (Salem) voted Douglas 864, Lincoln 598, Breckinridge 286. Linn voted Breckinridge 671, Lincoln 580, Douglas 312, and Lane voted Breckinridge 555, Lincoln 492, Douglas 166. Linn and Lane, the county named after Joseph Lane, belong to the Breckinridge base. **The decisive shift** is in Marion County, among Salem's Douglas Democrats. Nesmith and Bush, partners in the Salem Clique, campaign against each other in their own county. Bush is the sharper case: he opposed slavery and supported exclusion (Hist.), and he still votes for a Union that demands exclusion's repeal. Exclusion is the occasion; self-government is the argument.

**Why Nesmith's reversal is credible.** Historically Nesmith and Bush helped keep Oregon in the Union, so a speech alone could not turn him. Here, though, circumstances have changed four times:
- In 1859 the Republicans refused Oregon admission.
- In 1860 Nesmith did not go to the Senate. Historically he was elected that year with Edward Baker by a Douglas–Republican fusion, but here there is no state to elect him, and he spends 1861–66 in Oregon working the compact.
- The war is over, and the choice is between two unions after an armistice, not loyalty against secession.
- In spring 1866 the Republican Senate refuses Oregon the constitution its voters chose (V7a).

He turns on the last of these, and the page shows it.

### §10, around the Whiteaker sentence (kept verbatim)

<!-- CR-059 -->
> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** The Oregon Enabling Act of 2 March 1861 offers statehood under a new constitution without slavery or exclusion, and Oregon elects its delegates in June. The convention never meets. When Marcus takes Portland, Oregon is still a territory of the United States. Its territorial legislature and county commissioners negotiate a civil compact with the occupation command. They choose Whiteaker, whom Oregon elected governor in 1858 for a state never admitted, to chair it. John Whiteaker negotiates civil protections without conceding a governor's right to transfer Oregon's allegiance. The legislature's acts take effect without the absent federal governor, subject to the compact's court. Courts and county governments continue, requisitions are paid and disputed in civilian courts, no one swears allegiance, and Oregon's men, ports and revenues serve no military purpose outside Oregon. The Puget Sound counties sign a parallel compact, and Matthew Deady, a territorial justice, becomes the compact's court. In the Willamette flood of December 1861 an officer seizes a relief steamer for the garrison. Deady orders it released and the command refuses. Victoria puts compliance before Hawaii's legislature as the price of its assent to renewing the Protectorship, and the officer is overruled. From 1862 the customs collected at Astoria and Portland go into a published Oregon account that pays Oregon's courts and county officers.

### §10, after "Idaho's mining districts turn toward the Northwest in 1863"

<!-- CR-059 -->
> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** In October 1861 the War Department orders every regular on the coast east. The regulars Wright carried up to Fort Walla Walla can no longer go down the river, and the Mullan Road over the Bitterroots is closed by snow until summer. They winter in a valley stripped bare by the worst winter anyone remembers. There is no flour to buy, no hay, no barley, and the cattle are dying in the fields. Their requisitions wait for the river to open, and when it opens it is Pacifica's. Washington has no volunteers to send, no recruits and no gold. The valley's farmers sell on Portland's credit for coin and take the quartermaster's drafts only at a discount the post cannot pay. The Coeur d'Alene and Spokane, who remember Wright's hangings of 1858, will let no Union column come west. After the attempt on the lower Columbia fails, the Walla Walla convention of July 1862 lets the regulars carry out their own government's order: they march east with the honours of war, guided by John Mullan on the road he built. Mullan resigns his commission in the spring and comes back to his property in Walla Walla to carry Pacifica's mail on his road. Lewiston stands on a townsite the Nez Perce lease through the Confederation. The Boise Basin rush of 1862 is fed by Portland's steamers, Salt Lake's freighters and California's roads, and its gold is coined by Pacifica's assay office at Idaho City, by 1864 the largest town in the Northwest. Boise grows beside a Pacifican post. Washington organizes Idaho Territory and sends a governor by way of Fort Benton. The Protector's mandate forbids annexation by decree, and Hawaii's legislature renews it each year, so Pacifica lets him govern what he can. The Basin's Southern Democrats use his organic act to govern themselves. His successor's accounts are later found short by the money Congress voted for a Nez Perce treaty that was never made.

### §12, after the 1865 peace paragraph

<!-- CR-059 -->
> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** The peace transfers Idaho outright. It transfers Oregon only if Oregon votes for it. Elias accepts the vote on terms: a Union Oregon would be disarmed, and Pacifica would pay less. The Union's Enabling Act still offers statehood, and the Senate's Republicans refuse to drop its ban on excluding free Black people. Pacifica's Articles leave residence to the member states, as Hawaii demanded in 1856, so Oregon may keep its exclusion clause. In Hawaii's ratifying session Victoria limits it to people who are not citizens of a member state. The delegates Oregon elected in June 1861 reconvene at Salem under Deady and put two questions: Pacifica or the United States, and the exclusion article, which takes effect only under Pacifica. In June 1866 Idaho's voters approve its charter of accession, the Confederal chambers and both founding states entrench Oregon's, and the Treasury publishes the customs houses a Union Oregon would face. Bush asks the Willamette why a people that refused Buchanan's appointees should take a Providentor for life; Nesmith answers with a charter no cabinet can touch. In October 1866 Oregon chooses Pacifica by a narrow majority. Salem's Douglas Democrats are won by the popular sovereignty their own country refused them, and Portland by a hinterland that is already Pacifican. The nations whose land it is have no ballot. They have signed their own compacts, to take effect only if Oregon accedes.
