## What this CR does

**The outcome.** Oregon is never a state of the Union. It is occupied in 1861 as a territory, governs itself under a civil compact, and in October 1866 chooses by its own vote to join Pacifica. The 1867 treaty transfers the United States' title. Because Oregon was never admitted, the Senate cedes a territory, not a state (Washoe's case, the would-be State of Nevada, is CT9).

**The causal chain, made explicit on the page.** On 12 February 1859 the House of Representatives historically passed Oregon's admission by 114 to 103. Here it fails, 106 to 110. Every lost vote traces to California's independence or to California's Oregon policy:
- Two of the historical yeas are California's own members, who do not exist here.
- John Sherman, who historically did not vote, is persuaded by his brother William's letters from Monterey and votes no.
- Two Mississippi Democrats who meant to stand by Lane vote no. Kansas has been held to the English bill's population ratio since its voters refused the English terms in August 1858, and the free states took the Senate's lead only in May, after eight years of the parity California's absence preserved. They will not admit a second free state at half the ratio.
- Two Ohio Republicans, leaving the House in March, follow John Sherman's security argument. Sherman builds it from Pacifica's renewal note and his brother's letters: a state beside a foreign treasury that has shown how a legislature leaves.
- Two Maine Republicans follow the last part of Sherman's case, the race for settlers: the Superintendency's paid passages are filling Oregon's valleys, so fill Oregon with free labour first and admit it after. It is the Emigrant Aid logic that won Kansas, turned against New England's own cause.

The right argument, "admit Oregon to bind it," is made on the floor by Hughes and Stephens, as historically. It loses because the man asking is Joseph Lane, the Lecompton man.

**Why Oregon chooses Pacifica.**
- **The exclusion clause.** The Union's 1861 Enabling Act forbids it, and Senate Republicans refuse to drop the condition. Pacifica's 1856 Articles leave residence and the franchise to the member states, a power Hawaii demanded, so Oregon may keep a limited clause. Exclusion is the occasion.
- **Self-government is the argument.** Salem's Douglas Democrats get the popular sovereignty Douglas died defending: an entrenched charter that cannot be changed without Oregon's own consent.
- **The Idaho trade.** Idaho is Pacifican before Oregon votes: in fact from the Walla Walla convention of summer 1862 and the mining compacts of 1863, in the treaty signed in 1865 (ratified 1867), and by its own consent in June 1866. A Union Oregon would be an exclave with its hinterland behind a customs line.
- **Five years of evidence.** The compact's record and Pacifica's gold make the case.

**Who is party.** The Native nations whose lands, treaties or fishing places lie in Oregon are parties to the accession, not delegates at Salem. Their inclusion costs Pacifica votes in the south and gains some in the east.

**Working result (MODEL):** accession by about 51 to 49 with the levers the author chose on 7 November; Elias expects about 55 (Part VI, "Why Elias takes the risk"). The model is calibrated on the historical 1866 governor's race: 20,355 votes, with Woods (Union party) beating Kelly (Democrat) 10,316 to 10,039, a margin of 277. Accession needs to hold nearly all of the Democrats' 49.3 percent and win one to three points from voters who historically voted with the Union party.

Querétaro, the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention and the sectional lock depend on Washington believing the Republic covets nothing north of the 42nd parallel. **Who decides, and when.**

### Who changed their mind and why

**Barksdale and McRae: the ratio and the balance.** The Senate passed Oregon in May 1858, a week after Minnesota and before Kansas voted in August to refuse the English bill's terms (Hist.). From then on, the English bill's population ratio is the South's only hold on Kansas. Admitting Oregon at half that ratio would break it, and would give the free states a lead of two, nine months after the parity that California's absence had kept since 1850. The argument is already on the floor, historically: Millson of Virginia says the English bill gave Kansas notice, so the ratio binds from now on, and Hill of Georgia says he would put Oregon "upon the same ground" as Kansas. Historically it moved few Democrats, because the balance was already lost; here it is not lost yet. Barksdale and McRae (McRae newly seated in Quitman's place) came meaning to stand by Lane. Sherman's speech makes Oregon a question of loyalty, not party: a state whose purse is in Monterey is no sure pair of Democratic votes. They vote with the Alabama and South Carolina members. Lamar, who had needled Millson about Kansas, votes yes with Reuben Davis and Singleton and loses the argument in the delegation. *Link: S1, S2, H4.*

Stephens answers this in his speech of 12 February as historically: the South's security rests "upon principle, rather than upon numbers", and the balance "is already gone", with "no prospect of its ever being regained". Here the claim is nine months old and true by a single state, and Barksdale knows it: without California, parity ended only with Minnesota in May 1858, and Oregon would make the free states' lead two. Stephens's historical precedent, that Florida and Texas came in without a census ("So was California"), loses its last example.

**John Sherman: the brother's letters.** Historically Sherman did not vote, and he sat on the naval investigating committee that winter. He is the clearest case of a mind that changes. His brother William is in Monterey (canon §8), and William's letters describe a republic Washington has never seen. Sherman comes to the debate as a Republican who would let Oregon in and leaves it as the man who moved for the correspondence and voted no.

**Horton and Leiter: security.** Sherman makes an argument nobody could make historically:
- He moves for the correspondence. Buchanan, who signed the 1848 convention, sends it.
- He reads the renewal note's schedule of Oregon produce and Astoria port charges to the House.
- He reads his brother's letters: a republic that drills every young man, coins Oregon's money, carried Portland's banks in 1855 and in 1856 took a kingdom into its confederation by that kingdom's own vote.
- Lane advised Oregon in 1858 to act as a state without waiting for Congress. Admit it now and he gets a legislature, a governor and a militia beside a foreign treasury that holds Oregon's purse and has shown how a legislature leaves.
- A territory is governed from Washington. Keep Oregon one until it has a constitution Congress can accept, and Republicans expect to win in 1860.

As members leaving Congress, the two owe Oregon's Democrats nothing. Their colleague Nichols, also leaving, says on 10 February what he said historically: admission should not take "a purely partisan cast", and Ohio, Indiana and Illinois have all "interposed obstacles to the immigration of free negroes" (Ohio repealed its laws in 1849; Indiana and Illinois still had them). He votes yes and loses. *Link: S3, S4, P1–P3, P6, William Sherman in Pacifica (canon §8).*

**Wood and Foster: the race for settlers** (chosen, author, 7 November). Sherman's last paper is the Superintendency's passenger returns. Oregon's donation claims closed in 1855, and Pacifica is still paying passages and granting married couples land on Oregon's own terms without a colour bar. Whoever fills a territory owns it: that was the lesson of Kansas, and New England taught it, through the Emigrant Aid Company. Wood and Foster draw the lesson. Oregon's settlers are arriving on Monterey's tickets, so fill Oregon with free labour first and admit it after, when the next census shows whose people they are. Two of New England's votes go against admission on New England's own argument. (The earlier draft gave them a shipping motive. It is dropped because admission or territorial status does not change who may carry Oregon's trade.) *Link: P4.*

**Thayer** is cut from the scene (author, 12 November 2026). His historical speech and vote for admission stand in the record and are not shown.

## Part IV — War and the compact, 1861–1865 (W1–W15)

**The electorate in 1861.** The occupation matters politically because of who Oregon's voters are:
- many Southern-born Democrats who hate Republican Washington more than they love slavery;
- commercial Portland, which depends on Monterey's credit;
- Union Democrats (Bush, Nesmith) and a small Republican party, who oppose the occupation.

These are canon §10's three factions, and they are the electorate of 1866.

**Whiteaker's line in §10 keeps its words.** He "negotiates civil protections without conceding a governor's right to transfer Oregon's allegiance," but he is governor-elect of a state never admitted. He has no allegiance to transfer.

**The levers W5–W10** are the occupation's argument. The most important is **W7 together with V2**:
- From 1863 thousands of Missouri refugees, Confederate deserters and draft evaders came west on the Oregon Trail (Hist.).
- Those who settle the Grande Ronde, Powder River and the Willamette before spring 1865 are U.S. citizens, so they are electors.
- They vote against Washington. They are part of Oregon's historical wartime growth and already inside the Democrats' 49.3 percent of 1866, not an addition to it. **The lever is defensive:** the armistice date keeps them on the rolls, where Washington's 1861 rolls would have struck them off. How many settled in Oregon and could vote is unquantified (research task 25; the NPS study *Overlanders in the Columbia River Gorge, 1840–1870* has the migration table).

**W6 has a sneaky edge.** The officials who will run the 1866 polls are paid from Oregon's own customs. The Union will object. Pacifica answers that it is Oregon's money, and the protocol's observers and the returns commission answer for the count.

**The irony to show, not explain.** Pacifica shelters white men fleeing a draft in 1863 and returns Black men fleeing slavery from 1871.

**Why the Missouri route fails.** Canon (CR-015 H4) says it is "cut in wartime." The mechanism:

**Why Elias tolerates Wallace.** In the moment, the reasons are legal. Article XIV forbids annexation by decree and limits the Protector to what operations require, and Hawaii's legislature, which renews the Protectorship each year, would make an arrested governor its question. Idaho's camps are supplied, not occupied. The Basin's Southern Democrats have their own reason to use the Union's organic act: it lets them govern themselves against Lincoln's appointees. | Mechanism | Who it moves |
|---|---|
| The customs map (I11): Oregon's flour, beef and freight to Idaho would pay Pacifica's tariff; the treaty's transit clause covers through-shipment, not sale | Portland, the OSN, The Dalles, Umatilla Landing, Willamette millers and stockmen |
| The exclave made concrete | Marion's Douglas Democrats |

| Lyon's theft reported in Oregon's papers (W8) | Everyone |
| The refugees protected on the Snake road (W7) | Eastern Oregon |
| Idaho's gold reaching Portland as Pacifican coin | Portland's banks |

**Idaho is load-bearing.** As a scenario assumption, not a historical estimate, it is worth several points (4–6 in the working model). Without it, accession probably loses. Research task 19 (7 November; `outputs/sessions/20261107-cr059-oregon/research-idaho-trade.md`) grounds the size in the trade's scale (Hist.): Idaho produced about $6.5–8 million of gold a year in 1863–66 (Lindgren 1898, p. 652), the Boise Basin $3–5 million of it (p. 655); Wells Fargo shipped $5.4–6.2 million of treasure a year from Portland in 1864–66, against Portland's own merchandise exports of $457,967 in 1866; the river company's traffic rose from 6,290 tons and 10,500 passengers in 1861 to 21,834 tons and 36,000 passengers in 1864, and it returned 48 per cent on its original shares in its first year, 1861 (Johansen 1941, p. 182). The Columbia, through Umatilla Landing, supplied most of the Boise Basin in 1862–64; Salt Lake sent mainly flour. From 1864 California's wagon roads took a growing share: by October 1865 three-fourths of the trains entering Idaho came from California (*Idaho World*, via Trimble and Throckmorton 1961, p. 262), and much of the Basin's gold went out overland by the Humboldt route (Throckmorton p. 300). Before any Pacifican policy, Portland was already losing southern Idaho to routes that in this timeline are Pacifica's. The conversion into votes remains MODEL.

**The summer of 1866 (PROPOSED, from task 19).** Historically, in 1866, California's railway, river and teamster interests underbid Portland for the Owyhee and Boise trade, with free freight to Chico landing and wagons to Boise at 11–12 cents a pound, while the river company's earnings fell and the Basin's output passed its peak. Here those California routes, and Salt Lake's, are Pacifica's. The customs map (I11) is published in the same summer as California's rate war: a Union Oregon would face a tariff line and a cheaper rival at once, and Portland votes to keep a shrinking trade rather than to win a growing one. **The softened slump (author, 7 November 2026; amounts MODEL).** Historically a slump struck Portland and the coast in 1865–66 (Throckmorton 1961, pp. 289–290), from four causes: the fall of gold from about 258 (July 1864) to about 136 (May 1865), which caught merchants who had bought in New York on currency credit; dear credit at 1½–2 per cent a month; the waning of Idaho; and the loss of Montana to St. Louis. Here the first two are softened and the last two stand:
- **Less of the currency swing.** Oregon has been inside the customs union and on Californian coin since 1861 (P2) and buys more through San Francisco in gold, so less of its stock is bought on New York currency credit. Softened, not spared.
- **Cheaper credit.** The Superintendency's credit of 1855 (P3), Pacifican partners at Ladd's bank (L3) and the Reserve Bank (1857, PROPOSED in CR-057) keep Portland's sound houses in credit through 1865.
- **Wages in the south.** The Siskiyou railway contract (W9) pays Jackson County men in gold through 1863–66, in the slump years and in the county where accession is weakest.
- **What stands.** Idaho's decline is geological (both Lindgren's and Bolino's series peak in 1865–66), though its trade now runs on Pacifica's own carriers; Montana goes to St. Louis either way. Portland still fears for a shrinking trade, and the vote still turns on that fear and on Elias's own lines, not on hard times.

**The debtor's 29 per cent.** Without a dull coast, the historical argument for the national currency (Bowles, August 1865: opinion in Oregon turning toward it because the East boomed on paper while the coast stagnated; Throckmorton p. 290) shrinks to a debtor's sum. Greenbacks averaged about 71 cents in gold in 1866 (Throckmorton p. 273). A Union Oregon with paper as legal tender would let a mortgaged farmer repay a debt without a gold clause at about 71 cents on the dollar, and cost Portland's lenders the same 29 per cent on their books. The money row (Part VII) moves Ladd, Failing and the river company further toward accession and wins the Union a few indebted farmers in the valley; it no longer argues that paper means prosperity. Material for the greenbacker notices (research note, section 7, no. 4).

**The neighbour that slumps.** British Columbia's slump of 1865–66, as the Cariboo faded and the colonies' debt pushed the union of Vancouver Island and British Columbia in 1866 (Hist., to verify), shows across the water what Oregon was spared. If Colfax's tour of 1865 is used (set piece 6), Bowles writes home about a coast that is not dull under Pacifica, an awkward witness for the Union's campaign.

**For the dynamic model layer:** the 1865–66 slump, harder in the Union's currency zone than in Pacifica's, is a first test case for credit and wages as second-order effects.

## Part VI — The question, the offers and the vote, 1865–1866 (V1–V17)

### Three bodies frame the vote

| Layer | Body | Decides |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The treaty's Oregon article and the armistice protocol signed with it, late 1865 (CR-062 O1, confirmed by the author on 12 November 2026; CT14) | What the vote means; the threshold; the electorate; the polls (V1–V4) |
| 2 | The convention of the 1861 delegates | The ballot's text, the separate article and the note (V5–V6) |
| 3 | The voters | Pacifica or the United States |

**Why Washington wants a vote at all.** A vote lets the Union lose Oregon without signing it away. Senators can tell their voters that Oregon left by its own choice.

**Elias's price.** He accepts the risk of a vote on terms. If Oregon votes for the United States, it stays a U.S. territory but neutral and demilitarized: no U.S. naval station, fortification or garrison beyond a customs guard; the Columbia's navigation free to Pacifica; and a smaller vendor credit (MODEL). The Union would keep a commercial port it could not use for war, so canon's "Union without a Pacific port" (C-1867-WESTERN-LINE) holds because Oregon accedes. The condition sits in the protocol signed with the treaty in December 1865 as a supplement to the armistice convention, and is spent before the Senate ratifies in 1867; the treaty's article carries no condition of its own (CT14; CR-062 O1, confirmed by the author on 12 November 2026). The lower price also gives Washington its own reason to accept a vote it may lose.

**Why Elias takes the risk** (chosen, author, 7 November, with CT14). CR-015 A6 calls the Union's full exit from the coast the indispensable term, and a man who believes that does not accept an even chance. Elias expects about 55 per cent (MODEL): the county officials paid from Oregon's customs account (W6), the river company's agents and Portland's banks all report a comfortable majority, and Idaho's June vote seems to confirm it. The margin narrows to about 51–49 because of the lines Elias himself insists on: the Chinese floor and the Native article cost about two points in the south and on the coast (Part VII, Part VIII). He nearly loses Oregon to his own principles. The narrow result is a character beat, not luck, and his estimate holds until August, when a report after Roseburg shows the margin closing (open decision 49); the count in Jackson County confirms it. **A third reason (CR-068 CT9; approved there, 11 November 2026):** Lincoln's message of 1861 made the Union's own President the author of the test, that the right of revolution belongs to a people who use it themselves, and Elias printed that test as the second head of the Terms of Monterey in August 1863. He cannot refuse in 1865 a vote he has offered in print for two years.

**What "remain a territory" means.** It is not on the ballot. It is what a Union vote means for anyone who also wants exclusion, because Congress would reject that constitution. The ballot note makes this visible.

### Why the Union cannot match the offer (V7)

Senate Republicans kill the House's amendment for three reasons:
- **Principle:** Sumner, Wade and Bingham's cause.
- **Party:** two more Democratic senators.
- **Riddance:** some would rather lose a Democratic exclusion state than admit one. John Sherman's 1859 argument becomes "let Oregon choose."

A further charge for Oregon's Democrats: the Union that asks them to admit free Black people still keeps slaves in Kentucky and Delaware, because the Thirteenth Amendment failed (canon §12; CT10, confirmed).

### Who decides, and what moves them

**Baseline electorate.** About 20,000 voters, calibrated on the historical 1866 governor's race (20,355 votes; Union party 50.7, Democrats 49.3). The 1860 presidential split in Oregon was Lincoln 36.2, Breckinridge 34.4, Douglas 28.0 percent (Hist.). Accession needs nearly all the Democrats and one to three points of voters who historically voted with the Union party: Portland's river interest and Nesmith's Douglas men.

**The 1860 county returns matter (Hist.).** The Willamette was not one Douglas bloc. Marion (Salem) voted Douglas 864, Lincoln 598, Breckinridge 286. Linn voted Breckinridge 671, Lincoln 580, Douglas 312, and Lane voted Breckinridge 555, Lincoln 492, Douglas 166 (figures from the author's research audit; Wikipedia's county table differs slightly, e.g. Marion Douglas 865, Linn Breckinridge 672, Lincoln 582, Douglas 308, Lane Breckinridge 565, Lincoln 507, Douglas 155; to reconcile against the official abstract; the pattern is the same). Linn and Lane, the county named after Joseph Lane, belong to the Breckinridge base. Marion is the Douglas county, and the home of the *Statesman*, which Bush sold in 1863, and of the Salem Clique.

**The decisive shift** is in Marion County, among Salem's Douglas Democrats. Nesmith leads for accession in his own county. Against him stands the *Oregonian*'s Union-party campaign, built on Lincoln's letter (the author left the choice to the drafter, 12 November 2026; PROPOSED). Exclusion is the occasion; self-government is the argument.

**Why Nesmith's reversal is credible.** Historically Nesmith and Bush helped keep Oregon in the Union, so a speech alone could not turn him. Here, though, circumstances have changed four times:
- In 1859 the Republicans refused Oregon admission.
- In 1860 Nesmith did not go to the Senate. Historically he was elected that year with Edward Baker by a Douglas–Republican fusion, but here there is no state to elect him, and he spends 1861–66 in Oregon working the compact.
- The war is over, and the choice is between two unions after an armistice, not loyalty against secession.
- In spring 1866 the Republican Senate refuses Oregon the constitution its voters chose (V7a).

He turns on the last of these, and the page shows it.

### §8, after the 1858 paragraph

### §10, around the Whiteaker sentence (kept verbatim)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** The Oregon Enabling Act of 2 March 1861 offers statehood under a new constitution without slavery or exclusion, and Oregon elects its delegates in June. ### §10, after "Idaho's mining districts turn toward the Northwest in 1863"

<!-- CR-059 -->
> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** In October 1861 the War Department orders every regular on the coast east, except Casey's companies holding Port Townsend. ### §12, after the 1865 peace paragraph

<!-- CR-059 -->
> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** The peace transfers Idaho outright.
