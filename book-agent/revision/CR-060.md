## What this CR does

**The outcome.** Between 1861 and 1864 the Union's hold on the interior west of the divide dissolves without a battle that Washington could have won, and by 1867 every organized community there has consented to Pacifica on its own terms. Deseret is associated with Pacifica in December 1862 and becomes a member state only when the treaty is exchanged in 1867. Washoe is retaken in the spring of 1864, before a State of Nevada can be made, so the peace cedes no state. Idaho's and western Montana's camps live inside Pacifica's freight, coin and courts from 1863–64, while Lincoln's governors hold offices with little under them. Southwest Wyoming is tied to Salt Lake by the post and the stage; the Western Slope is Ute country, and Pacifica's title there rests on the treaty and a Ute agreement.

**The causal chain.**
1. **The Union withdraws itself.** Camp Floyd is abandoned in July 1861 (Hist.) and the October 1861 recall takes the coast's regulars east (CR-059 I2). The only force that historically re-garrisoned Utah was the Third California Infantry, raised in California, which here is Pacifica's.
2. **The Union cannot come back.** A garrison at Salt Lake supplied from Fort Leavenworth must eat Mormon flour: the whole Leavenworth–Salt Lake shipment of FY1862–63 (662,720 lb over 1,837 miles, Hess ch. 6) is about 600 man-years of rations and nothing for the animals (MODEL). The Morrill Act of July 1862 makes the flour unavailable.
3. **Pacifica can come.** Its road garrison eats Deseret's wheat bought in gold and needs from California only what is light: ammunition, clothing, medicine and coin (Part V).
4. **The interior's people choose supply over flags.** The Mormons choose protection from the Morrill Act; the mining camps choose freight, coin and courts; Washoe's miners reject the Union's first constitution over the mine tax (Hist.).
5. **Lincoln answers with law.** He keeps the claim with paper governments (Idaho 1863, Montana 1864) and tries to lock the Basin into the Union by making Nevada a state. Elias answers by making the convention impossible.

**The Shoshone (author's decision, 8 November 2026).** Bear River is not a Pacifican massacre. The column that marches in January 1863 goes with warrants and written orders, fights a short, limited action at the ravine when the bands refuse to give up the Snake road killers, and does not touch the camp; the Box Elder compact follows in the summer. The stain the friendship doctrine needs (CR-015 H6) moves to the Owyhee country in 1865–66, where Idaho's miners' companies do what Connor's volunteers did historically, and Pacifica prosecutes them (M8–M10, CT5).

**The Union is rational throughout.** Holding a legal claim costs nothing; soldiers spent in Utah are soldiers lost at Shiloh. Washington keeps every claim it can keep by statute and gives up only what it cannot feed.

The historical interior garrisons of 1862–65 were raised in California and Oregon. Here they are Pacifica's, so the Union's interior position after 1861 is a set of claims without soldiers: three territorial governors, one weak fort in Washoe and the Platte road behind the divide.

**Why Lincoln does not send regiments.** Hess's own comment on the Leavenworth shipments is that they were "regular supply shipments for small garrisons" (ch. 6). Even a small garrison at Salt Lake would buy its flour from the Mormons, as Camp Floyd did (Hist.), and after the Morrill Act it cannot count on that. Lincoln's historical policy toward the Mormons was to leave them alone if they left him alone (Hist.; to verify). It is the right policy for a Union with a California regiment camped above the city. Here the regiment on the road is Pacifica's, and it does not camp above the city.

## Part II — Deseret (D1–D12)

**Who decides, and when.**
- **January–July 1862:** Young. He elects himself governor of a state that does not yet exist, keeps the channel to San Bernardino open and waits on Washington.
- **July–October 1862:** Elias. The Morrill Act gives Young a reason to ask; Elias sends a garrison before he sends terms. Elias does not send Connor and does not camp above the city: a garrison that overawes the Temple Block makes Deseret a conquered province before it is an associate, gives the Union's press its sermon while Oregon is being asked to consent, and tells Hawaii's legislature what the Protectorship is for. He sends Hays, who has made one Great Basin peace already, Beale, who has made reservations, and Jefferson Hunt, whom Young has known since the Battalion, and he posts the garrison on the road at the old Camp Floyd, forty miles off. Pacifica offers protection from the Morrill Act with a garrison whose orders are to buy the road and whose soldiers enter the city unarmed. The leverage is not a gun on the Temple Block; it is the Morrill Act itself, the gold market for Deseret's wheat and the fact that Pacifica is the only power that can feed a garrison in Utah at all (Part V).
- **December 1862:** both. The Compact is the bargain both can sign. Young gets protection without giving up his state; Elias gets the road, the wheat and the Legion without asking Hawaii or the Assembly for a vote.
- **1863:** the price. Lee is surrendered under the travellers' article, which everyone knows was written for Mountain Meadows. It reaches back to 1857 because Deseret has consented to it.

**Why the Compact is lawful.** Canon's own wording settles it: the Protectorship forbids "annexation by emergency decree", and §10 says "recognition and later association require peace and constitutional agreements, not emergency annexation." An Article XIV agreement about passage, supply and defence is what operations require. Accession is a constitutional amendment, and Elias has four reasons not to attempt one during the war (D11). The Assembly majority that threatens his emergency measures in 1864–65 (§12) would use a polygamous member state against him; the Protestant American-born third and the Kentucky pastor already do (CR-015 H3).

**Bear River (author's decision, 8 November 2026).** The Compact promises safe passage on Pacifica's roads, and a month later the road is bloody: the killings of 1862 on the Snake and of the Bannack miners in January are history, and so are the warrants. What Pacifica cannot do is what Connor's volunteers did. It has refused since 1851 to pay volunteer campaigns (CR-033); it prosecutes settler massacres by 1865 (CR-015 H6); Hawaii's legislature is renewing the Protectorship that spring; and Oregon's voters are being asked to believe in consent before title. A massacre at Bear River hands the Union a sermon and Victoria a question. So the column marches with warrants and written orders, fights the morning it is refused, and leaves the lodges standing; the road is opened by the Box Elder compact, paid for out of the road's own fees, and held by Shoshone scouts. The doctrine is still "a choice rather than a given" (CR-015 H6): the choice is made at the ravine, when Hays obeys an order Connor would not have, and it is tested again on the Owyhee (Part IV). CR-059 I7's "price of the Snake road" is the compact's annuities, not Bear Hunter's people.

**Why the vote waits until December 1866.** Oregon votes on 1 October 1866 (CR-059). Holding Deseret's charter until after that vote keeps Oregon's Protestant clergy from voting against a Mormon fellow member state (CR-059 W10, L14). Utah's legislature historically met in December (Hist.; to verify), so the date needs no contrivance.

## Part III — Carson and Washoe (C1–C11)

**1861–63: the enclave Pacifica feeds.** Washoe is Union territory under the Carson Convention (canon §8). Its silver, capital and workforce are San Francisco's (canon §8). Elias could take it in 1861 and does not, for four reasons:
- the 1860 defeat, when Hawaii withheld its assent;
- the yearly renewal of the Protectorship by the same legislature;
- the Carson guarantees, which protect Pacifican investors as well as American ones;
- the fact that the silver comes to San Francisco anyway.

Nye's volunteers at Fort Churchill eat California's flour, bought with drafts Washoe's merchants discount. After December 1862 the garrison has no line to the Union except through Deseret.

**1864: Lincoln's lock, and Elias's answer.** In 1859 the House refused to admit Oregon to bind it (CR-059). In 1864 Lincoln does with Washoe what the House would not do with Oregon: he makes a state in order to bind it. The voters' rejection of the first constitution in January (Hist.) shows that Washoe will not pay the Union's tax. The Enabling Act of March is Lincoln's second attempt. Elias reads it as a lock on the peace, because no Senate will cede one of its own states. He does not argue. He makes a convention impossible, in the first weeks the Sierra roads are open.

**The terms.** The retaking is the cheapest operation of the war and is written to be the cleanest:
- the garrison leaves armed, or goes home;
- Nye goes east;
- the county officials sign a compact on the Oregon model;
- the mine tax is lighter than the one Washoe just rejected;
- Carson's guarantees stand.

Sherman, who opposed crossing the border in 1860 (character bible), accepts this crossing because it ends a garrison's slow starvation and annexes nothing. Victoria writes the conditions into Hawaii's assent. In 1860 she stopped the war over Washoe; in 1864 she sets the terms of its return.

**Washington's response.** Lincoln protests. Washoe's silver had never reached his Treasury (canon §9), and the loss falls in the spring of 1864, among the failures the election will weigh (CR-065). Its electoral weight is small.

## Part IV — Idaho, Montana and the divide (M1–M7, R1–R5)

**Supply, not flags.** CR-059 makes Idaho Pacifican in fact by the river, the Lewiston lease and Camp Boise. This CR adds the other two doors: the Montana Trail from Salt Lake and the Snake road from the Bear River crossing. The compacts offer what the camps cannot get from Washington: freight at published rates, coin rather than greenbacks (CR-059 I2's notes at two-thirds of face), assay and courts.

**The courts and the rope.** Elias faced vigilance committees in San Francisco in 1851 and 1856 (canon §8). In Montana the committee acts in the one season when no court can cross the divide, and it hangs a sheriff who once wore a Pacifican marshal's badge. The miners' argument against Pacifica's courts is therefore personal: your courts let him go. Pacifica's answer is a circuit judge in the spring, and the committee goes on hanging men beside him. The promise of courts is made, and from 1864 it is tested.

**Fort Benton.** Canon says the Missouri route is "cut in wartime" (CR-015 H4). It is cut for the army: the Lakota wars and the June rise make it useless for military supply (CR-059 Part V). Civilian steamers still come up in the high water, and the camps buy from St Louis as well as from Salt Lake and Portland. That two-way trade is what makes the 111°W line defensible in Washington in 1867 (plan CR-B; CR-062's named civilian route from Fort Benton).

**Colorado and Montana (CO1–CO9).** The author asked that the line be drawn where it is defensible, not where the water divides. Montana already is: the camps are on the Missouri side of the divide and Pacifican because Salt Lake and Walla Walla feed them and Fort Benton cannot (CO2). In Colorado the question is who can hold what in 1863–67. The Union's Colorado is Denver and the Front Range mineral belt — Central City, Black Hawk, Idaho Springs, Boulder — 25,000 people, the regiment that won Glorieta, and 600 miles of wagon road to Leavenworth that the Cheyenne and Arapaho actually cut in the winter of 1864–65 (Hist.; to verify). Pacifica's reach is Salt Lake, 500 miles from Denver, and Santa Fe by the San Luis Valley and Poncha Pass, 300 miles — once Fort Garland falls (CO3). Between them lie the parks, South Park and the upper Arkansas, almost empty, reachable from the valley in summer and from nowhere in winter. So the 1865 line takes every piece of the Rockies Pacifica can garrison or snow in — the Western Slope, the parks, South Park, the upper Arkansas, the San Luis Valley — and leaves the one piece it cannot take, hold, feed or ask (CO4), with the Colorado article (CO5) as the lawful door. The Front Range mineral belt then comes the way Deseret and Washoe came: Pacifica is the only power that can feed it. Its gold fails in 1864–67, its statehood is refused, its road is cut; Pacifica's smelter buys its ores in coin; Pacifica's rails reach Leadville's silver on Pacifica's side of the crest while the Union's stop at Denver's foot; and the districts vote their freight bills in 1878–80 (CO6–CO7). The line is decided twice — by arms and snow in 1863–65, by freight and silver in 1877–82 — and the Union's revanchists are right to call the second decision what it is (CO9). What is left to the Union is Denver and the plains, and the name the author wants on the page is **Denver, Kansas** (CO8).

**The divide.** Fort Bridger, the Green River and South Pass are Salt Lake's hinterland. The stage road crosses the line at the divide's eastern branch, between Fort Halleck and Fort Bridger. On the Pacific side the emigrants of 1863–65 find published terms and no recruiting sergeant. The Western Slope has almost no settlers to consent. Its title needs the Utes, and the agreement that makes it Pacifican makes it unsellable: the Uncompahgre lease of 1881 (§19) is the same agreement working.

---

**The Owyhee (M8–M10).** Idaho is Pacifican by supply before it is Pacifican by law (CR-059), and that is the gap the stain comes through. The districts arm themselves because the mounted police are a dozen men on four hundred miles of road; the Silver City press preaches extermination because the Snake War's raids are real; and the company that kills the Paiute camp is made of men who voted for Pacifica's assay office and its coin. Pacifica did not order it and could not have prevented it, and both are true of the Union at Sand Creek. The difference is what follows: the court at Boise, the Paiute witnesses, the hangings, and a Paiute agreement signed because the hangings happened. The Union's critics will say in 1890 that Pacifica hanged miners to make a point; the Owyhee districts will say so in 1866. Both are partly right, which is what makes it a stain and not a parable.

### CT8 — What "Washoe" covers, and the Salt Lake column's road (D4; CR-015 H2; C-1860-CARSON; added by the set review, 8 November 2026)

**Decision (author, 12 November 2026):** route (a). "Washoe" is the district the Carson Convention covered; the column goes by Honey Lake and the Humboldt, north of Carson.
