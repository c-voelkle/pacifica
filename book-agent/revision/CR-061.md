## What this CR does

**The outcome.** Pacifica holds the Rio Grande from Mesilla to Santa Fe from the autumn of 1862 with a garrison of about 1,800 men. The Union holds Fort Union, Las Vegas and Raton Pass. The front freezes at Glorieta Pass and stays there through the 1865 armistice. The treaty ratified in 1867 gives Pacifica the whole territory to 103°W, and Fort Union's garrison marches out under a timetable after the exchange. Fort Union is never besieged.

**Why the front freezes where it does.** This is the review's blocker B4, answered with numbers. A wagon carries about a ton. Each animal eats 26 lb a day, against a man's 3. A loaded train makes 15 miles a day. Beyond about 100 miles a supply train that carries its own fodder delivers nothing (Hess ch. 6; H&J p. 78 n. 14). Each side therefore lives on the valley it can buy from, and moves the rest a few wagons at a time:
- The Union's valley is the Mora and Las Vegas country behind Fort Union, which is fed by six million pounds a year from Fort Leavenworth, 735 miles away.
- Pacifica's valley is the Rio Grande, from Mesilla up to Santa Fe, where the column pays gold for grain that Sibley's men took.
- Glorieta is the defile where the two radii meet. A force big enough to take the other side's depot could not be fed in front of it (Part II).

**Who commands.** Caleb Vance, recommended (C2). He was a prisoner on this road in 1841. At Mesilla his soldiers kill Henry Pruett, a fellow captive and the man raising Elias's son. That makes CR-022's watch his errand: "the men whose soldiers killed the father who raised him" are Caleb and Elias.

**The towns.** In 1863 Pacifica makes the Rio Grande compact with the members of the territorial legislature, the probate judges and the town councils. It offers elected local government, bilingual courts, land-grant confirmation on CR-033's model and the 1850 institutions. The signatories accept one clause unwillingly: peonage and the captive trade end when the peace comes. In 1865 the Navajo homeland treaty requires the captives' return. That brings the clause forward, and abolition with compensation in 1866 provokes the short Hispano revolt (APPROVED canon). The compact is tested by the promise it made least willingly.

**The southern door.** From 1865 rifles and credit reach Juárez at Paso del Norte. They go by a west-bank road and a customs post agreed with Mexico, which never touches Confederate El Paso (CR-058).

## Part I — The column and its commander (C1–C10)

### Why the column marches when it does

Pacifica is at war with the Union from the summer of 1861, and Sibley's Texans are Richmond's men. Elias has three reasons to wait until April 1862:
- **The army is in the Northwest.** Marcus has the regulars on the Columbia (canon §10).
- **The desert.** The Yuma–Gila crossing is passable for a column only in spring, on the season's grass and water. Carleton's historical column left on 13 April and moved in detachments (Boatner).
- **Policy.** A Confederate army in New Mexico is a partner's venture. If it fails, it opens the valley to Pacifica without a quarrel with Richmond, and Elias's terms for Arizona become credit and a railway, not a war.

Sibley fails in March (Boatner: Valverde 21 February; Santa Fe taken 23 March; Glorieta 26–28 March, "indecisive", with Slough fighting against Canby's orders; Sibley withdraws "being unable to resupply his forces"; Canby, with 860 regulars and 350 volunteers, "let him go"; Sibley reaches San Antonio "with 1,700 fewer men"). When the column leaves Fort Yuma in April, the valley it is marching into has been fought over twice and stripped.

### March 1862: Glorieta in this timeline (C0)

The author's question of 8 November 2026 was whether the battle happens at all, since the Confederates have no California to conquer. It happens, and it matters more.

**Why Richmond marches.** Sibley's plan (approved July 1861; Hist., to verify) aimed at New Mexico, Colorado's gold and the road to California's ports and gold. Here the third aim inverts rather than vanishes: California is the Confederacy's supplier, and Pacifican credit and arms already come round the Horn to Matamoros (canon §11; CR-015 F1), seven hundred miles of Mexican wagon road from any Confederate depot. A Confederate Rio Grande and Gila is the land bridge to that supplier, by the Butterfield road no blockade can touch. Baylor's Mesilla (July 1861, Hist.) is its first span. Davis needs no new reason; the old reason has become a better one.

**Why Washington fights for it.** The Union has lost its Pacific coast and its Pacific squadron's base. New Mexico is the one place where it can stand between Pacifica and Richmond, and Fort Union and the Santa Fe Trail are its only reach into the Southwest. Canby's defence, Gilpin's Coloradans coming down the Trail (Hist.) and Slough's disobedience are all history; what is new is that Washington understands why they matter.

**What is unchanged on the ground.** The brigade (about 2,600 up the river in January, Boatner), its arms (Texan, bought and seized in 1861), Valverde on 21 February, the depots emptied at Albuquerque on 1 March and Santa Fe on 4 March (120 wagonloads), Santa Fe taken on 23 March, Glorieta on 26–28 March and Chivington's destruction of the train at Johnson's Ranch (Hist.; to verify the place), the decision to withdraw "being unable to resupply" — none of the men in it has yet felt the divergence.

**What is different afterwards, and how it ties in.**
1. *The valley is empty.* The depots went east and Sibley's men ate the rest. That is why the column pays gold for grain in the autumn (C9) and why Canby cannot keep a field force on the river (C10).
2. *Fort Union has proved itself.* The campaign was won from its depot by its garrison and the Coloradans; Halleck anchors on it (F1), and the front freezes at the defile where the two wagon radii meet (F3, Part II).
3. *The retreat ends at Mesilla, not San Antonio, for part of the brigade.* Historically the main body left the Mesilla valley in May–June 1862 for two reasons: Carleton's column was coming as an enemy, and the valley could not feed them (Boatner; Hess's arithmetic in Part II). Here the column on the Gila is a friend, so hunger alone sends the main body home, and Steele's rear guard stays to hold the bridgehead until Pacifica arrives — and then quarrels with the column over the valley's flour (C7). Pruett dies in a dispute between partners, which is why "both governments prefer to forget" it.
4. *Richmond trades what it could not take.* The bridge it failed to win by arms it gets by treaty in early 1863 — the renunciation of Arizona for credit and the Sunset route (CT5) — with Pacifica, not the Confederacy, holding the road and the Confederacy holding the right to use it.
5. *The Coloradans go home* to face Pacifica across the divide (Part I, "The gap"), which is where CR-060's Ute agreement and the Western Slope begin.

**Open (decision 18):** whether Sibley knows in March that a Pacifican column will come. Richmond's agents have been in Monterey since 1861 (canon §9), so he may know of the friendship and not of the column (recommended: the column is Elias's decision of the winter, and Sibley's brigade is already starving by the time word could reach it). If he knew, the brigade might have waited at Mesilla and starved there, a darker scene the author may prefer.

### The commander: four options

Canon has no U.S. regulars in California. So James H. Carleton, a 1st Dragoons officer in California in 1861 (Hist.), was never posted there.

### Mesilla (C7)

The clash at Mesilla in July 1862 is between partners, so it must have a cause that neither government ordered:
- The rear guard is the last of Sibley's army in the territory. Steele is said to have held Mesilla with it into July (Hist.; to verify). It is preparing a 600-mile march to San Antonio and needs the valley's flour.
- The column's advance cavalry, two companies ahead of the main body, has bought the same harvest in gold.
- The town has seen requisitions from both armies since 1861, and now a buyer. The alcalde asks the buyer to guard the mill.
- The two forces meet at the mill at dawn. The advance cavalry's officer (name OPEN; a historical person or left unnamed) has taken the alcalde's request as a duty: his men have paid for the flour, and he will not watch it loaded on Texan wagons. When the rear guard's wagons come up to the doors he orders his men to fire. He has no orders to fight and no orders not to. The rear guard loses a dozen men and leaves for El Paso. Among the dead is Captain Henry Pruett.

Both governments prefer to forget it, and the reasons are material. Richmond needs Pacifican credit and the Matamoros channel (C-1863-MATAMOROS). Pacifica needs Richmond's renunciation of Arizona and a quiet Texas behind the Rio Grande garrison. Caleb finds Pruett among the dead. He knew him at Perote in 1842, and he alone knows who is waiting in San Antonio. The watch goes into his saddlebag. In early 1863 it takes him and Elias to Abigail's door (CR-022).

**At the doorstep (set review, 8 November 2026).** Elias too shared the captivity with Pruett. The character bible has Elias's "1841 Santa Fe Expedition and captivity in Mexico, shared with Caleb Vance" (CT-ELIAS) and Caleb surviving "the 1841 Texan Santa Fe Expedition and imprisonment in Mexico alongside teenage Elias" (CT-CALEB); neither entry names Pruett, but the storyline's CR-022 paragraph (§8, APPROVED) has Abigail marry "Henry Pruett, a San Antonio man who shared the Mexican captivity". ### The campaign (C10–C11)

The first draft of this CR had Canby march back to Fort Union because the column was buying the valley's flour. The author objected (8 November 2026): a Union commander who has just beaten Sibley, holds the strongest fort on the river and sees an enemy column of two thousand coming up United States territory does not withdraw on an arithmetic. He fights. Logistics decides where a front ends up, not whether a soldier fights for the ground on the way there; the set had let the first stand in for the second, and it is corrected here.

**Canby's position in August 1862.** He has reoccupied the river posts after Sibley's retreat (Hist.). Fort Craig is about 250 miles from Fort Union by the river road, Albuquerque about 160 (Hist.; to verify); the valley is stripped; his quartermasters pay in notes worth about two-thirds of face in gold (CR-059 I2; for New Mexico, to verify); the Colorado regiment is wanted at home (historically it stayed until early 1863, to verify). None of that makes him leave. It makes him choose his ground: Fort Craig, where he beat Sibley's attempt to take the fort and lost only the ford.

**The second Valverde.** Vance cannot storm Fort Craig with 1,800 men, and says so. He does what Sibley did in February: crosses to the east bank by the Jornada to cut the fort from Albuquerque, which obliges Canby to come out or be starved in place. Canby comes out, as he did in February, to the ford at Valverde. What is different from February is in the ranks, not the ground: Pacifican regulars with the Carquinez rifles against regulars, Coloradans and Carson's Hispano volunteers, who waver as they did against Sibley (Hist.). Pacifica wins the ford and loses some two hundred men (MODEL), and Canby withdraws into the fort with the river cut behind him. It is the first battle between Pacifican and Union field forces in the set, and the storyline should count its dead.

**Why Pacifica succeeds where Sibley failed.** Sibley held the same position in March — Fort Craig behind him, Albuquerque and Santa Fe in his hands, Fort Union ahead — and starved out of it because he could not feed himself in a valley that would not sell to him. The column holds it through the winter because it pays gold (C9) and because its strength and animals are sized to what the valley can sell (Part II). The arithmetic of Part II is not the reason there is no battle; it is the reason Pacifica wins the campaign after the battle. Fort Craig, with Pacifica between it and Fort Union, eats its mules by February (the fate of Port Hudson's garrison, Boatner) and marches out under arms to Fort Union in April 1863 on terms — Vance's terms, which are Canby's to Sibley in reverse.

**Carson.** The author's canon has Elias reading to Kit Carson as a boy, and Carson could not read to the end of his life (Hist.). His historical loyalty was to the United States and to Canby, and his volunteers' steadiness at the first Valverde was poor. Here he fights at the second Valverde because an oath and a commander are owed on the morning of a battle, and resigns in November because a fort held against Elias Thorne's own column is a different thing from a fort held against Texans. His going takes the New Mexico volunteers' loyalty with it piecemeal, which is why the fort cannot be held even on half rations. The regular who holds it does his duty; the Union is not made weak to make Pacifica look strong.

**Halleck chooses Fort Union (F1) in April 1863, not August 1862.** With the river lost, the Coloradans gone home to face Pacifica across the divide and the Trail's capacity what Hess shows it to be (Part II), he holds what Leavenworth can feed. Connelly goes to Las Vegas still claiming all of it. The front freezes at Glorieta after a campaign, which is the only way a front freezes.

## Part II — The arithmetic (L1–L5, F1–F4)

All computed figures are MODEL. The inputs are the sources' own:
- **Wagons (Hess ch. 6):** a six-mule wagon carries at most 2,674 lb, 1,500–2,500 lb in practice and 1,000 lb on bad roads. An army needs 25–35 wagons per 1,000 men.
- **Rations (Hess ch. 6):** an animal eats 26 lb a day (14 lb of hay and 12 of grain); a man eats 3 lb.
- **Pace (Hess ch. 6):** a loaded train makes about 15 miles a day, 26 on the open frontier.
- **Radius (H&J p. 78 n. 14):** carrying fodder "reduced the practical radius of wagon supply to less than 100 miles", while "an army far smaller than Sherman's could have received essentials by wagon because the animals of a smaller number of wagons could be fed along the route." Sherman thought "50 miles was too far" (Hess ch. 6).
- **The Union's route (Hess ch. 6):** in FY1862–63 Fort Leavenworth shipped more than 6 million lb to Fort Union over 735 miles: "regular supply shipments for small garrisons."
- **A depot (Boatner):** 120 wagonloads went out of Santa Fe on 4 March 1862.

The animals eat more than three times what the men eat. In the grazing season only the grain ration (about 8,000 lb a day) has to be bought. **The measure of a depot:** at 2,000 lb a load, Santa Fe's 120 wagonloads were 240,000 lb. That is about ten days of this garrison with its animals, or about six weeks of its men alone. Sibley's brigade was mounted (Hist.; to verify) and lived by taking; the column has a quarter as many animals per man as a mounted force and pays.

### Why nothing bulky comes from Tucson

Tucson to Santa Fe is about 570 miles (Hist.; to verify):
- At 15 miles a day the trip is 38 days each way and 76 days for the round trip.
- A six-mule team eats about 156 lb a day, so over the round trip it eats about 11,900 lb of forage to deliver a 2,000-lb payload.
- No train can carry its own forage that far. Bulk can come only if the animals are fed along the route, which is H&J's condition for "a smaller number of wagons".

The staged road meets that condition (L2). Each stage is worked by its own wagons and fed from its own depot of locally bought grain.

**Gold is the lightest supply.** A dollar in gold coin weighs about 1.67 grams, so a wagonload of 2,000 lb of coin is about $540,000 (MODEL). The whole garrison's winter needs, at full strength for 150 days, come to 22,820 × 150 ≈ 3.4 million lb. One wagon of gold would buy all of it at any price below about $16 a hundredweight. Grain prices in the valley in 1862 are a research task (task 4). The point stands without them: Pacifica's real line of supply is a small cart of coin. The Union's, at Fort Union, is 735 miles of wagons.

### The winter of 1862–63 at Santa Fe

With L3's dispersal, about 1,100 men and 200 animals stay at Santa Fe and the outposts for 150 days:
- Men: 1,100 × 3 × 150 = 495,000 lb.
- Animals: 200 × 26 × 150 = 780,000 lb.
- **Total: about 1.3 million lb**, about 640 wagonloads or five Santa Fe depots.

It comes from within about 100 miles, the Rio Abajo and the valleys north of the city, by carretas and ox teams hired in coin (L4). The cavalry and the train's mules winter where the grain is. The town's own needs are met by grain the column sells at cost (L5). **This is the logistics test the plan set, and it passes only at garrison size.** At Sibley's 2,600 and a mounted brigade's animals the same winter would need several times as much forage from a valley he had already stripped, which is why he left.

### The same ceiling on the Union side

Fort Union's 6 million lb a year from Leavenworth works out at about 16,400 lb a day:
- At 2,500 lb a load that is about 2,400 wagonloads a year; at 1,500 lb, about 4,000.
- At 15 miles a day the trip takes 49 days each way, on the Plains' summer grass.

## Part III — The compact (K1–K7)

**Who signs, and why.** With Connelly at Las Vegas, the territory's own institutions are left in Santa Fe: the legislature's members from the western counties, the probate judges who are the counties' real government, and the town councils. Pacifica bargains with them because Article XIV forbids annexation by decree and limits the Protector to what operations require (canon §8). An agreed civil government is cheaper than an occupation, and the 1867 treaty will need a consenting electorate to point to.
- **Otero is the obvious first name.** He is the territory's former delegate, a Rio Abajo merchant family's son and a man of Southern sympathies (Hist.). His trade runs down the river to Chihuahua and up the Trail to Missouri, and the compact is the only arrangement that keeps both open.
- **The Hispano bargain is old.** Washington kept New Mexico a territory for sixty years (CR-015 H4). Monterey offers a Spanish-speaking republic's courts and a register that confirms the grants.

**The clause signed least willingly (K4).** Pacifica's founding law forbids peonage. The compact cannot keep the 1850 institutions and abolish at once without losing every patrón in Rio Arriba. So the signatories accept abolition on the peace, with compensation, and a ban on new enforcement in the meantime. Elias calls it a date, not a concession. The probate judges sign because the date seems far off.

**The agon (K7).** Lincoln's government offers New Mexico free soil and the Homestead Act from Las Vegas, and keeps it a territory. Elias offers self-government and leaves abolition for later. Each is right about the other. In 1866 Pacifica abolishes peonage a year before Congress historically did (CR-015 H6: "banned by Congress only in 1867"). It does so because the Navajo treaty requires it, not because Lincoln argued for it.

**Community grants (K3; CT6).** CR-033 A21 has a grantee pay for a fast patent in land. In New Mexico that would take the commons of the community grants, the historical road to their loss (Hist.; to verify). The compact keeps A21 for individual grants and confirms the community grants to the community as a body.

## Part IV — The nations and the settlements inside the line (N1–N6, A1–A4)

**Why there is no campaign against the Navajo.** Historically Carleton's and Carson's campaign of 1863–64 and the Long Walk followed the raids on the Rio Grande towns (Hist.; CR-015 H6). Here Elias chooses not to make one: CR-015 H6 (APPROVED) sets Bear River beside the Navajo treaty so that "one stain and one success make the friendship doctrine a choice rather than a given" (CT8). The choice is also the cheap one. The garrison is 1,800 men with 670 animals, and a mounted campaign through the Navajo country would need several times that forage in country without a depot. The 1863 agreement (N2) trades the end of the raids for the end of the slaving expeditions. The towns get quiet, and the captives already in their households stay where they are until 1865.

**The treaty and the captives.** By 1865 the armistice frees the column's men and the compact has a record. Barboncito and Manuelito want the homeland and their people back; many of the Navajo held in New Mexican households were taken as children (Hist.; numbers to verify). The captive article makes the treaty real to the Navajo and the abolition clause real to the towns. Pacifica pays for both: the households for each release, the creditors for each cancelled peon debt.

**The revolt (A3).** It comes in the spring of 1866, in the north, where peonage and captive servants are most of the patrones' labour (Hist. pattern; to verify). It is short because the compact's own militia does not join it and the towns of the Rio Abajo, paid in coin for four years, do not rise. It is tried in the compact's courts, in Spanish. The settlers' cost is the one canon names: "Religious toleration and civic protection demand actions that cost the new state support among its own settlers."

**The Apache.** The war begun at Apache Pass in 1862 (C6) is ended in 1865 on canon's terms (N4). The release of Mangas Coloradas in January 1863 (N5, recommended) is the one act of good faith the Chiricahua remember. The Paso del Norte convention ends the scalp bounties south of the line (M2). The settler-massacre clause is the promise to be tested later.

## Part V — Fort Union and the peace (P1–P5, M1–M2)

The armistice changes nothing on the ground at Glorieta: two garrisons, a trail and the traders. The treaty makes the line into the 1867 boundary at 37°N and 103°W, and Fort Union becomes Pacifica's eastern bastion without a shot fired at it. Its garrison leaves after the exchange on the Walla Walla model. Washington can tell Kansas that its soldiers marched out under arms, and the Trail's merchants keep their trade. The Union's grievance over New Mexico is small: it gave up a territory it had refused to make a state. Its grievance stays pointed at Richmond (plan principle 4).

**The southern door (M1–M2).** CR-058's rifles for Juárez need a crossing that Richmond cannot close. From Mesilla the road keeps to the river's west bank inside New Mexico down to the line above Paso del Norte, and Mexico's customs post faces Pacifica's across the ford. Confederate El Paso, on the east bank, sees the wagons go by and has no say.

## Part VI — The Gadsden split (G1)

In the alternative, California buys the western half of the Gadsden strip, through Tucson, in 1853–54; Washington buys the Mesilla half and gets transit across California's segment. That would give the 1862 column a Californian base at Tucson. Against it:
- it adds a prewar treaty and a new border;
- it starts a Southern railway quarrel in the 1850s that canon does not need;
- it changes the Union's whole 1850s policy of waiting for the ripe fruit (canon §5).

Carleton's historical column reached Tucson from Yuma without owning it, and this one does too. **Recommendation: drop it.**
