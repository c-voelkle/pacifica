## What this CR does

**The outcome.** Pacifica holds the Rio Grande from Mesilla to Santa Fe from the autumn of 1862, with about 3,000 men for the autumn campaign and a winter garrison of 1,800–2,300 regulars (C3). The Union holds Fort Union, Las Vegas and Raton Pass. The front freezes at Glorieta Pass and stays there through the 1865 armistice. The treaty ratified in 1867 gives Pacifica the whole territory to 103°W, and Fort Union's garrison marches out under a timetable after the exchange. Fort Union is never besieged.

**Why the front freezes where it does.** This is the review's blocker B4, answered with numbers. A wagon carries about a ton. Each animal eats 26 lb a day, against a man's 3. A loaded train makes 15 miles a day. Beyond about 100 miles a supply train that carries its own fodder delivers nothing (Hess ch. 6; H&J p. 78 n. 14). Each side therefore lives on the valley it can buy from, and moves the rest a few wagons at a time:
- The Union's valley is the Mora and Las Vegas country behind Fort Union, which is fed by six million pounds a year from Fort Leavenworth, 735 miles away.
- Pacifica's valley is the Rio Grande, from Mesilla up to Santa Fe, where the column pays gold for grain that Sibley's men took.
- Glorieta is the defile where the two radii meet. A force big enough to take the other side's depot could not be fed in front of it (Part II).

**Who commands.** Caleb Vance, recommended (C2). He was a prisoner on this road in 1841. At Mesilla his soldiers kill Henry Pruett, a fellow captive and the man raising Elias's son. That makes CR-022's watch his errand: "the men whose soldiers killed the father who raised him" are Caleb and Elias.

**The towns.** In 1863 Pacifica makes the Rio Grande compact with the members of the territorial legislature, the probate judges and the town councils. It offers elected local government, bilingual courts, land-grant confirmation on CR-033's model and the 1850 institutions. The signatories accept one clause unwillingly: peonage and the captive trade end when the peace comes. In 1865 the Navajo homeland treaty requires the captives' return. That brings the clause forward, and abolition with compensation in 1866 provokes the short Hispano revolt (APPROVED canon). The compact is tested by the promise it made least willingly.

**The southern door.** From 1865 rifles and credit reach Juárez at Paso del Norte. They go by a west-bank road and a customs post agreed with Mexico, which never touches Confederate El Paso (CR-058).

## Part I — The column and its commander (C1–C10)

### Why the column marches when it does

Pacifica is at war with the Union from the summer of 1861, and Sibley's Texans are Richmond's men. Elias has three reasons to wait until April 1862:
- **The army is in the Northwest.** Marcus has the regulars on the Columbia (canon §10).
- **The desert.** The Yuma–Gila crossing is passable for a column only in spring, on the season's grass and water. Carleton's historical column left on 13 April and moved in detachments (Boatner).
- **Policy.** A Confederate army in New Mexico is a partner's venture. If it fails, it opens the valley to Pacifica without a quarrel with Richmond, and Elias's terms for Arizona become credit and a railway, not a war.

Sibley fails in March (Boatner: Valverde 21 February; Santa Fe taken 23 March; Glorieta 26–28 March, "indecisive", with Slough fighting against Canby's orders; Sibley withdraws "being unable to resupply his forces"; Canby, with 860 regulars and 350 volunteers, "let him go"; Sibley reaches San Antonio "with 1,700 fewer men"). When the column leaves Fort Yuma in April, the valley it is marching into has been fought over twice and stripped.

### March 1862: Glorieta in this timeline (C0)

The author's question of 8 November 2026 was whether the battle happens at all, since the Confederates have no California to conquer. It happens, and it matters more.

**Why Richmond marches.** Sibley's plan (approved July 1861; Hist., to verify) aimed at New Mexico, Colorado's gold and the road to California's ports and gold. Here the third aim inverts rather than vanishes: California is the Confederacy's supplier, and Pacifican credit and goods come round the Horn to neutral Matamoros and overland into Texas (canon §11; CR-015 F1), months at sea and exposed to Union cruisers on the way. A Confederate Rio Grande and Gila is a land bridge to that supplier, by the Butterfield road no blockade can touch: a road for mail, specie, agents and the few goods worth more than their weight (caps, medicines, gold), since a wagon road of that length carries almost no bulk (Part II). Baylor's Mesilla (July 1861, Hist.) is its first span. Davis needs no new reason; the old reason has become a better one.

**Why Washington fights for it.** The Union's only Pacific foothold is the besieged enclave on Puget Sound, supplied by sea through Panama (canon §10) and no use as a road to the Southwest. New Mexico is the one place where it can stand between Pacifica and Richmond, and Fort Union and the Santa Fe Trail are its only reach into the Southwest. Canby's defence, Gilpin's Coloradans coming down the Trail (Hist.) and Slough's disobedience are all history; what is new is that Washington understands why they matter.

**What is unchanged on the ground.** The brigade (about 2,600 up the river in January, Boatner), its arms (Texan, bought and seized in 1861), Valverde on 21 February, the depots emptied at Albuquerque on 1 March and Santa Fe on 4 March (120 wagonloads), Santa Fe taken on 23 March, Glorieta on 26–28 March and Chivington's destruction of the train at Johnson's Ranch (Hist.; to verify the place), the decision to withdraw "being unable to resupply" — none of the men in it has yet felt the divergence.

**What is different afterwards, and how it ties in.**
1. *The valley is empty.* The depots went east and Sibley's men ate the rest. That is why the column pays gold for grain in the autumn (C9) and why Canby must stock Fort Craig over 250 miles of road (C10).
2. *Fort Union has proved itself.* The campaign was won from its depot by its garrison and the Coloradans; Halleck anchors on it (F1), and the front freezes at the defile where the two wagon radii meet (F3, Part II).
3. *The retreat ends at Mesilla, not San Antonio, for part of the brigade.* Historically the main body left the Mesilla valley in May–June 1862 for two reasons: Carleton's column was coming as an enemy, and the valley could not feed them (Boatner; Hess's arithmetic in Part II). Here the column on the Gila is a friend, so hunger alone sends the main body home, and Steele's rear guard stays to hold the bridgehead until Pacifica arrives — and then quarrels with the column over the valley's flour (C7). Pruett dies in a dispute between partners, which is why "both governments prefer to forget" it.
4. *Richmond trades what it could not take.* The bridge it failed to win by arms it gets by treaty in early 1863 — the renunciation of Arizona for credit and the Sunset route (CT5) — with Pacifica, not the Confederacy, holding the road and the Confederacy holding the right to use it.
5. *The Coloradans stay through 1862.* Halleck holds the 1st Colorado in New Mexico for the autumn; after the fighting of October (C10) it goes home in January 1863 (Hist. month; to verify), because Colorado itself now faces Pacifica across the divide, which is where CR-060's Ute agreement and the Western Slope begin.

**Open (decision 18):** whether Sibley knows in March that a Pacifican column will come. Richmond's agents have been in Monterey since 1861 (canon §9), so he may know of the friendship and not of the column (recommended: the column is Elias's decision of the winter, and Sibley's brigade is already starving by the time word could reach it). If he knew, the brigade might have waited at Mesilla and starved there, a darker scene the author may prefer.

So James H. Carleton, a 1st Dragoons officer in California in 1861 (Hist.), was never posted there.

Here he would command at Fort Union opposite the column he historically led, pressing Halleck for an offensive the arithmetic forbids (open decision 8).

### Mesilla (C7)

The clash at Mesilla in July 1862 is between partners, so it must have a cause that neither government ordered:
- The rear guard is the last of Sibley's army in the territory. Steele is said to have held Mesilla with it into July (Hist.; to verify). It is preparing a 600-mile march to San Antonio and needs the valley's flour.
- The column's advance cavalry, two companies ahead of the main body, has bought the same harvest in gold.
- The town has seen requisitions from both armies since 1861, and now a buyer. The alcalde asks the buyer to guard the mill.
- The two forces meet at the mill at dawn. The advance cavalry's officer, Edward E. Eyre (C2), has taken the alcalde's request as a duty: his men have paid for the flour, and he will not watch it loaded on Texan wagons. When the rear guard's wagons come up to the doors he orders his men to fire. He has no orders to fight and no orders not to. The rear guard loses a dozen men and leaves for El Paso. Among the dead is Captain Henry Pruett.

Both governments prefer to forget it, and the reasons are material. Richmond needs Pacifican credit and the Matamoros channel (C-1863-MATAMOROS). Pacifica needs Richmond's renunciation of Arizona and a quiet Texas behind the Rio Grande garrison. Caleb finds Pruett among the dead. He knew him at Perote in 1842, and he alone knows who is waiting in San Antonio. The watch goes into his saddlebag. In early 1863 it takes him and Elias to Abigail's door (CR-022).

**At the doorstep (set review, 8 November 2026).** Elias too shared the captivity with Pruett. The character bible has Elias's "1841 Santa Fe Expedition and captivity in Mexico, shared with Caleb Vance" (CT-ELIAS) and Caleb surviving "the 1841 Texan Santa Fe Expedition and imprisonment in Mexico alongside teenage Elias" (CT-CALEB); neither entry names Pruett, but the storyline's CR-022 paragraph (§8, APPROVED) has Abigail marry "Henry Pruett, a San Antonio man who shared the Mexican captivity". So both men at the door knew the dead man as a fellow prisoner, and Elias learns he has a son from a watch that belonged to a man he once starved beside. The scene can use it; it needs no change to canon.

### The campaign (C10–C11)

The first draft of this CR had Canby march back to Fort Union because the column was buying the valley's flour. The author objected (8 November 2026): a Union commander who has just beaten Sibley, holds the strongest fort on the river and sees an enemy column of two thousand coming up United States territory does not withdraw on an arithmetic. He fights. Logistics decides where a front ends up, not whether a soldier fights for the ground on the way there; the set had let the first stand in for the second, and it is corrected here.

**Canby's position in August 1862.** He has reoccupied the river posts after Sibley's retreat (Hist.). Fort Craig is about 250 miles from Fort Union by the river road, Albuquerque about 160 (Hist.; to verify); the valley is stripped; his quartermasters pay in notes worth about two-thirds of face in gold (CR-059 I2; for New Mexico, to verify); the Colorado regiment is wanted at home (historically it went to Fort Union by early September 1862, Nelson ch. 15, and home in early 1863, to verify). None of that makes him leave. It makes him choose his ground: Fort Craig, where he beat Sibley's attempt to take the fort and lost only the ford. He stocks it through the summer and posts a company on the Mesa de la Contadera, the height he found empty in February (Nelson ch. 7).

**What the fort is (from the author's sources, 9 November 2026).** Fort Craig was "a good post but a poor fort" (Ickis, in Nelson ch. 7): adobe walls about seven feet high, one battery platform, room for about 200; in February 1862 it held 3,800, most of them camped outside. Its heavy guns were partly Quaker guns (Colton p. 36). It lived on one 250-mile road and went on short rations within five weeks of the first Valverde and, after Sibley's retreat, on quarter rations in late April and May, because the command travelled faster than its supplies (Colton pp. 81–82, 94); Forts Union and Craig held all the territory's subsistence (Colton pp. 81–82). Its herd grazed outside the walls: in July 1863 eight Apaches ran off 104 army mules there (Colton pp. 130–31). East of the river a lava ridge looks down on it; the Texans camped about a mile and a half east of the fort (Colton p. 26), and Ickis, in the sand hills below their guns, wrote that his men could not return fire because they were far below (Nelson ch. 7). Canby's own rule, on the night before the first Valverde, was that he must not be trapped in the fort and cut off from Santa Fe (Nelson ch. 7). The column's plan turns each of these into a lever: it takes the fort's transport, not its walls, and makes Canby choose between his supplies and his fort, and it fights where Canby must attack, because breech-loaders and Armstrong guns (canon: `sources/rise_to_power.md`) are decisive in defence, and the Union's smoothbore howitzers reach about a third as far as an Armstrong.

**The force.** C3: about 3,000 for the autumn, the 2,300 regulars for the winter. The first draft's 1,800 was the winter garrison standing in for the campaign force.

**The sequence (C10; revised 9 November 2026 after the fresh-context review and a second review of the stratagem; dates and figures MODEL).** A competent Canby does not fall for February twice. He builds a redoubt at the Valverde ford, puts a company on the Contadera, pickets the Jornada, keeps his herd under the fort's guns and has Graydon's spy company out, and every precaution faces the manoeuvre he has already seen. The plan is built on that competence. The precedents are his own and Chivington's: in April 1862 Canby marched out of Fort Craig to join the troops from Fort Union, stopped his officers' attack at Peralta, and came down the east bank before crossing back at Lemitar (Colton pp. 84–86, 93); at Glorieta Chivington went round the Confederate position for its wagons (Hist.). An animated schematic of the sequence is the artifact "Coopwood's Track" (9 November 2026).

0. *The ground rules (alternate history).* Halleck refuses Canby's request to go east (C11; historically he left "at his own request", Colton p. 118). Canby keeps the 1st Colorado at Albuquerque to cover the depot, where historically it had gone to Fort Union by early September (Nelson ch. 15). Carleton has no column, since the California Column is Pacifica's own; he commands the northern district from Fort Union under Canby, and from January 1863 the department (C11; open decision 8). Chivington does not take the leave he historically took in July 1862 to go to Washington (Colton p. 95): with a Pacifican column on the Gila, Canby refuses it. Carson's re-formed 1st New Mexico, historically in Santa Fe that autumn before the Mescalero campaign (Nelson ch. 15), comes down to Fort Craig in the summer for the same reason. Howe commands the Southern District at Fort Craig, as he did historically from July 1862 (Colton p. 95).
1. *July–September: the harvest and the convoy's day.* From Mesilla the column's agents buy the 1862 crop in gold, in Socorro and the Rio Abajo too, behind the Union's lines, through the river merchants. The Union pays in discounted notes and is pushed toward requisition, which is what turned the valley against Sibley: the New Mexicans refused his paper, attacked his foragers and killed a Confederate captain (Colton p. 113), and Santa Fe's people hid their flour from them (Nelson ch. 10). The freighters who haul for the Union's quartermasters are the same Mesilla and Rio Abajo men, and they know when the autumn convoy for Fort Craig will pass Socorro. The September detachment comes over the Gila.
2. *Late September–early October: the bait is real.* Carretas lay water caches up the Jornada del Muerto in plain sight, and the main body follows slowly with the heavy guns. The Jornada was the more direct route north (Nelson ch. 1); Canby's pickets and spies report exactly what he expects, and his own precautions make him sure of it.
3. *Days 1–10: Coopwood's track, in reverse.* About 600 men with one or two pack howitzers, Socorro guides and pack mules leave the river at the Palomas on the pretext of an Apache expedition, as Carleton sent McCleave toward Tucson "using the pretext that he was going out to fight Tonto Indians" (Colton p. 102). Scouts have checked the water in advance, and the column carries rations and ammunition to fight even if the convoy escapes. It climbs the track Sibley's brigade came down in April: through the San Mateos, up the west side of the Magdalenas and round their north end, where Sibley's men took ten days over a hundred miles, hauling guns with ropes and cutting brush with axes (Colton pp. 91–92; Nelson ch. 11). The track is no secret, since Graydon's spy company followed Sibley down it (Colton p. 92). The surprise is a fighting force coming over it with enough to hold. A heavier gun load waits on the reconnaissance. If the convoy is missed, the way out is back into the Magdalenas. **The picket (added after the second review).** Because Graydon knows the track, Canby keeps a picket at its northern end, between Sabinal and La Joya near the mouth of the Rio Puerco, where Sibley's brigade left the river (Colton p. 90; Nelson ch. 11). On the night of day 10 the light column's advance, Socorro men who know where the picket sleeps, takes it before a rider gets away. If one had got away, Canby would have had a day's warning and the convoy might have turned back, and step 5's second branch would apply. The stratagem beats a precaution, not an oversight.
4. *Days 9–10: the main body splits.* On day 9 a battery and about 400 men go on to the lava ridge east of the fort and keep up the demonstration; their rifled guns outrange everything in it. On the nights of days 9 and 10 about 1,000 cross at the Texans' February ford, seven miles below the fort (Colton p. 26), and lie up on the west bank. Being seen there is the plan's main risk.
5. *Day 11: the convoy.* The light column comes down above Socorro on the day the freighters named, takes the convoy with its mules, and digs in at a narrows facing both ways (site to verify: perhaps San Acacia, where the valley narrows between La Joya and Socorro; the wagon road, the nearest crossings, and ground that keeps a force from turning the position). It stops wagons; mounted scouts can still get round. **If Canby stays put,** Pacifica keeps the convoy and its transport, the road stays cut and the investment begins early without a battle: the operation pays either way.
6. *Day 12: Canby does the right thing.* He must reopen his road and bring his supplies in, and he has done this before. He leaves about 700 in the fort and the ford works under Howe, marches north with about 1,300, his guns, cavalry and wagons, and sends for Chivington.
7. *Night of day 13: the roadblock.* On a courier's report that Canby's column has passed Valverde, the blocking force marches north on the west-bank wagon road that skirts the fort (Colton's February map, p. 26), about 13 miles in the dark with no river to cross, and by dawn is entrenched across the road behind him. Lancers screen the fort.
8. *Day 14: the stand at the narrows.* Canby attacks from the south. Chivington comes down and, as at Glorieta, goes round: he looks for a ford below the narrows and goes for the light column's pack train. The guard holds. That evening Canby's scouts bring word that the road behind him is shut. On day 15 Chivington tries again and fails, and the Coloradans fall back up the river toward Albuquerque; Canby turns south. The light column takes the heaviest Pacifican losses of the campaign.
9. *Day 16: the second Valverde, a fight for the wagons.* Canby attacks the entrenchment from the north. Howe, who has watched the blocking force dig in from the fort's walls, sorties with about 350 men against its rear, and the blocking force has to face both ways. Between them the Union infantry opens a way by the river, wading the shallows and working through the bosque past the end of the entrenchment; the road and the ford approach, blocked with felled cottonwoods and under the blocking force's rifles, will not pass a wagon. About 1,050 of the 1,300 reach the fort, and Howe's men get back in with them; every wagon and draft animal Canby took north is lost. Carson's 1st New Mexico fights as it fought at the first Valverde (Nelson ch. 7). Union losses about 400 with Chivington's, Pacifican about 200, most of them at the narrows (MODEL). Afterwards Caleb sends surgeons and medicine in under a flag, as Canby gave the Texans medical supplies in February (Colton p. 35). It is the first battle between Pacifican and Union field forces in the set, and the storyline counts its dead. Canby and Howe save the infantry by their own action; Caleb takes the transport that made it a field army.
10. *November–December: the north.* Albuquerque, Cubero, Santa Fe and Taos, step by step in the next section.
11. *November–April: the investment, and the terms earned separately.* About 1,750 men are in a post built for 200 that held 3,800 in and around it in February (Nelson ch. 7), and the camps outside the walls lie under the guns on the ridge. About 650 Pacificans and four guns hold the lines. **Why the Union holds it.** Not for a relief: the fort pins about 650 of the column's men and keeps the Union's claim to the river alive while its flag flies, and Halleck intends no relief; from January Carleton is acting department commander at Fort Union (C11), plans one and cannot feed it on 250 miles of winter road. **What decides the date** is food (Part II, "Fort Craig's food"): full rations to December, half from January, quarter from March, the cavalry's horses eaten from February. **Why Canby does not attack the 650:** they hold earthworks with breech-loaders and rifled guns that outrange his howitzers, and with no wagons a breakout could not go far up a winter road with the column's northern force between it and Fort Union. Carson resigns when Santa Fe falls with Taos behind the lines (C11); Canby discharges the New Mexico companies, and the column gives them safe-conduct and rations home (K8). In mid-April, with the quarter ration nearly gone and nothing on the road, Caleb, back from Texas by the river road (C12), offers evacuation with arms and colours; about 950 regulars and Coloradans march out to Fort Union, fed by Pacifica on the road.

### The north, November 1862–May 1863 (C10 step 10, C13, F8; author approved 9 November 2026)

The Union loses the north mostly by its own choices of what it can carry and what it must leave, and every one of those choices has a precedent from March 1862, when Sibley came up the river (Colton pp. 38–39). The difference is that Carleton has fewer wagons, because Canby lost his at Valverde, and that Pacifica pays where Sibley seized. An animated schematic is the artifact "The Road to Fort Garland" (9 November 2026). Dates and figures MODEL.

**The Union line in early November.** Canby is shut in Fort Craig, so Carleton at Fort Union is the senior Union commander still free (open decision 8). He has Chivington's 1st Colorado, about 600 after the narrows, falling back up the river from the narrows; the Albuquerque depot; the small post at Cubero; about 250 men and the martial law Canby instituted and Carleton continued in Santa Fe (Nelson ch. 15); about 250 at Fort Union; and the garrison of Fort Garland. His orders are those of March 1862: save what the wagons can carry, burn the rest, fall back on the Glorieta defile.

1. *Days 1–4: Peralta.* The lancers ride north on the east bank, the road Canby took south in April (Colton p. 93), and the light column follows on the west bank. Chivington turns at Peralta, among the acequias where the Texans stood in April (Nelson ch. 11), but a force on the east bank can cross behind him at Albuquerque, so he falls back to the depot.
2. *Day 5: the Albuquerque depot.* His quartermaster fires the stores his teams cannot draw. In March 1862 "many of the provisions were rescued by the local inhabitants" before the fire was done, and deserters took three of the wagons (Colton p. 38). This time a posted offer pays in coin for every sack saved, and the carreteros hired for the evacuation slip away in the night with their carts. Chivington marches north with what his own teams can pull, and the column enters Albuquerque without a fight.
3. *Days 6–9: Cubero and the river posts.* A lancer troop rides sixty miles west to Cubero with a letter. Its forty-odd New Mexico Volunteers, who in March gave the post up to four Texans (Colton p. 38), take their discharge and a safe-conduct home, and the stores are paid for. The Union's forage and paymaster posts in the Rio Abajo (Peralta, Los Lunas) are withdrawn. Fort Wingate, which Carleton ordered in September 1862 (Nelson ch. 15), is not built: the Navajo campaign it was meant for is shelved when Pacifica comes up the river. Fort Stanton stays the burned shell the Texans left (Nelson ch. 15).
4. *Mid-November: Carleton's choice.* Santa Fe cannot be held. Its water is meagre (Nelson ch. 10); Fort Marcy above the town was the Texans' cemetery and the Union has abandoned it (Nelson ch. 15); and two roads lead in from the river, by La Bajada and by Galisteo, as the Texans came. Carleton sends what stores his wagons can carry over Glorieta to Fort Union, as Donaldson sent 120 wagons in March (Colton p. 38), and puts the Coloradans in the defile at Pigeon's Ranch, the ground they won in March.
5. *Late November: the convention of Santa Fe (C13).* The northern force, about 900 with four guns, comes up both roads. The garrison's commander burns the military stores "with care for the city", as Donaldson did (Colton p. 38), and signs a convention for what he cannot carry: the hospital with its sick and wounded, the territorial archives, the churches and private property. The garrison marches out over Glorieta; Connelly goes to Las Vegas (F2); the column enters Santa Fe in the last days of November and ends martial law that day.
6. *Mid-December: Taos and Abiquiu.* About 150 men go up the river road by Embudo to Taos. There is no Union post to take: Cantonment Burgwin, which Sibley commanded when the war began (Nelson ch. 4), was given up early in the war (Hist.; to verify). Elias comes to Taos in these days (CR-066 Part E; C12). The Ute agency at Abiquiu keeps issuing rations, now paid by Pacifica (N6). Mora, across the mountains, stays with the Union (F7).
7. *December–February: the defile.* Pacifican outposts hold Cañoncito, at the west end of Apache Canyon where Chivington burned the Texan train; the Union holds Pigeon's Ranch. Neither attacks in winter (F5): beyond Glorieta each army is at the end of its wagon road. In January the 1st Colorado goes home, because Colorado itself now faces Pacifica across the divide (CR-060; Part I, Glorieta item 5), and regulars from Fort Union hold the outpost. Through the winter the territorial assembly sits on in Santa Fe (C14).
8. *March–May 1863: Fort Garland,* as below (F8).

What Pacifica does not take: Fort Union (F4), Las Vegas, Mora, Pigeon's Ranch and the Pecos country, which stay Union until the exchange (P1).

**Fort Garland, March–May 1863 (F8; revised 9 November 2026 at the author's decisions).** The books are thin here. Colton has it "commanding the road to Colorado" (p. 14). Nelson has Dodd's company take its wagons over the iced Sangre de Cristo pass in a day in December 1861 (ch. 6): the pass was hard, not closed. In March 1862 Canby ordered Fort Garland destroyed if the enemy moved north (Colton p. 47), so the problem is to take it intact, not to take it.

1. *December–February: the ground.* At Conejos Pacifica offers the Tabeguache an agreement without the removal west of the divide that a Union commission historically demanded of them in October 1863 (Colton p. 151); it replaces the Union's treaty (N6). Ute scouts watch the passes. The valley's plazas receive the compact's offer and stop selling to the post.
2. *Early March: the march.* About 350 regulars, with two rifled guns and Ute guides, go up the valley road from Taos. The Taos home guard stays in its own county: it is never ordered against the Union (K8).
3. *At the post, at dawn.* The herd and the wood camp are taken (firewood at 8,000 feet in March is life). A gun and a detachment are posted at the west foot of the Sangre de Cristo pass.
4. *The commander's choice.* His standing order of 1862 says burn and withdraw. Without his herd he cannot take his sick, his stores or his guns over the pass, and he fears what Ute scouts would do to a garrison on foot caught on it with Pacifican cavalry behind. The fear is his, not Pacifica's policy: the column's rules for its scouts (C6) forbid killing prisoners, but he has no reason to trust them. Carson comes in on his own initiative, unarmed and out of uniform, because he knows men inside and will not see the post burned over them; he is an intermediary, not Pacifica's envoy. He carries a written convention the commander can show his superiors: if not relieved by a set day in May, the garrison marches out with arms and colours, fed by Pacifica, and the post stands with its sick in it until they can travel.
5. *May.* The news from Fort Craig settles it, and Fort Garland falls without an assault. The Coloradans go home over the pass to the Huerfano.

**Why Pacifica succeeds where Sibley failed.** Sibley held the same position in March — Fort Craig behind him, Albuquerque and Santa Fe in his hands, Fort Union ahead — and starved out of it because he could not feed himself in a valley that would not sell to him, and because Fort Craig still had its road and its herd. The column holds it through the winter because it pays gold (C9), because its strength is sized to what the valley can sell (Part II), and because it took the fort's transport before it passed it.

**Carson.** The author's canon has Elias reading to Kit Carson as a boy, and Carson could not read to the end of his life (Hist.). His historical loyalty was to the United States and to Canby, and his 1st New Mexico fought well at the first Valverde, pouring flanking fire into the Texans at McRae's guns (Hist.: Nelson ch. 7). Here he fights at the second Valverde because an oath and a commander are owed on the morning of a battle, and resigns in November because a fort held against Elias Thorne's own column is a different thing from a fort held against Texans. Canby, not Carson, sends the New Mexico companies home: a regular's arithmetic with food short and the colonel gone. The regular who holds the fort does his duty; the Union is not made weak to make Pacifica look strong.

**Halleck chooses Fort Union (F1) in April 1863, not August 1862.** With the river lost, the Coloradans gone home to face Pacifica across the divide and the Trail's capacity what Hess shows it to be (Part II), he holds what Leavenworth can feed. Connelly goes to Las Vegas still claiming all of it. The front freezes at Glorieta after a campaign, which is the only way a front freezes.

## Part II — The arithmetic (L1–L5, F1–F4)

All computed figures are MODEL. The inputs are the sources' own:
- **Wagons (Hess ch. 6):** a six-mule wagon carries at most 2,674 lb, 1,500–2,500 lb in practice and 1,000 lb on bad roads. An army needs 25–35 wagons per 1,000 men.
- **Rations (Hess ch. 6):** an animal eats 26 lb a day (14 lb of hay and 12 of grain); a man eats 3 lb.
- **Pace (Hess ch. 6):** a loaded train makes about 15 miles a day, 26 on the open frontier.
- **Radius (H&J p. 78 n. 14):** carrying fodder "reduced the practical radius of wagon supply to less than 100 miles", while "an army far smaller than Sherman's could have received essentials by wagon because the animals of a smaller number of wagons could be fed along the route." Sherman thought "50 miles was too far" (Hess ch. 6).

## Part III — The compact (K1–K9, C14)

**Who signs, and why.** With Connelly at Las Vegas, the territory's own institutions are left in Santa Fe: the legislature's members from the western counties, in session there from 1 December 1862 (C14), the probate judges who are the counties' real government, and the town councils. Pacifica bargains with them because Article XIV forbids annexation by decree and limits the Protector to what operations require (canon §8). An agreed civil government is cheaper than an occupation, and the 1867 treaty will need a consenting electorate to point to.
- **Otero is the obvious first name.** He is the territory's former delegate, a Rio Abajo merchant family's son and a man of Southern sympathies (Hist.). His trade runs down the river to Chihuahua and up the Trail to Missouri, and the compact is the only arrangement that keeps both open.
- **The Hispano bargain is old.** Washington kept New Mexico a territory for sixty years (CR-015 H4). Monterey offers a Spanish-speaking republic's courts and a register that confirms the grants.

**The clause signed least willingly (K4).** Pacifica's founding law forbids peonage. The compact cannot keep the 1850 institutions and abolish at once without losing every patrón in Rio Arriba. So the signatories accept abolition on the peace, with compensation, and a ban on new enforcement in the meantime. Elias calls it a date, not a concession. The probate judges sign because the date seems far off.

**The agon (K7).** Lincoln's government offers New Mexico free soil and the Homestead Act from Las Vegas, and keeps it a territory. Elias offers self-government and leaves abolition for later. Each is right about the other. In 1866 Pacifica abolishes peonage a year before Congress historically did (CR-015 H6: "banned by Congress only in 1867"). It does so because the Navajo treaty requires it, not because Lincoln argued for it.

**Community grants (K3; CT6).** CR-033 A21 has a grantee pay for a fast patent in land. In New Mexico that would take the commons of the community grants, the historical road to their loss (Hist.; to verify). The compact keeps A21 for individual grants and confirms the community grants to the community as a body.

## Part IV — The nations and the settlements inside the line (N1–N7, A1–A4)

**Why there is no campaign against the Navajo.** Historically Carleton's and Carson's campaign of 1863–64 and the Long Walk followed the raids on the Rio Grande towns (Hist.; CR-015 H6). Here Elias chooses not to make one: CR-015 H6 (APPROVED) sets Bear River beside the Navajo treaty so that "one stain and one success make the friendship doctrine a choice rather than a given" (CT8). The choice is also the cheap one. The winter garrison is 1,800–2,300 men with about 670 animals, and a mounted campaign through the Navajo country would need several times that forage in country without a depot. The 1863 agreement (N2) trades the end of the raids for the end of the slaving expeditions. The towns get quiet, and the captives already in their households stay where they are until 1865.

**The treaty and the captives.** By 1865 the armistice frees the column's men and the compact has a record. Barboncito and Manuelito want the homeland and their people back; many of the Navajo held in New Mexican households were taken as children (Hist.; numbers to verify). The captive article makes the treaty real to the Navajo and the abolition clause real to the towns. Pacifica pays for both: the households for each release, the creditors for each cancelled peon debt.

**The revolt (A3).** It comes in the spring of 1866, in the north, where peonage and captive servants are most of the patrones' labour (Hist. pattern; to verify). It is short because the compact's own militia does not join it and the towns of the Rio Abajo, paid in coin for four years, do not rise. It is tried in the compact's courts, in Spanish. The settlers' cost is the one canon names: "Religious toleration and civic protection demand actions that cost the new state support among its own settlers."

**The Apache.** The war begun at Apache Pass in 1862 (C6) is ended in 1865 on canon's terms (N4). The release of Mangas Coloradas in January 1863 (N5, decided) is the one act of good faith the Chiricahua remember; it buys a truce with his own people, not yet a peace with Cochise's (N5). The Paso del Norte convention ends the scalp bounties south of the line (M2). The settler-massacre clause is the promise to be tested later.

## Part V — Fort Union and the peace (P1–P5, M1–M2)

The armistice changes nothing on the ground at Glorieta: two garrisons, a trail and the traders. The treaty makes the line into the 1867 boundary at 37°N and 103°W, and Fort Union becomes Pacifica's eastern bastion without a shot fired at it. Its garrison leaves after the exchange on the Walla Walla model. Washington can tell Kansas that its soldiers marched out under arms, and the Trail's merchants keep their trade. The Union's grievance over New Mexico is small: it gave up a territory it had refused to make a state. Its grievance stays pointed at Richmond (plan principle 4).

**The southern door (M1–M2).** CR-058's rifles for Juárez need a crossing that Richmond cannot close. From Mesilla the road keeps to the river's west bank inside New Mexico down to the boundary at the Pass, and Mexico's customs post faces Pacifica's across the land boundary. Confederate El Paso, on the east bank, sees the wagons go by and has no say.

## Part VI — The Gadsden split (G1)

In the alternative, California buys the western half of the Gadsden strip, through Tucson, in 1853–54; Washington buys the Mesilla half and gets transit across California's segment. That would give the 1862 column a Californian base at Tucson. Against it:
- it adds a prewar treaty and a new border;
- it starts a Southern railway quarrel in the 1850s that canon does not need;
- it changes the Union's whole 1850s policy of waiting for the ripe fruit (canon §5).

Carleton's historical column reached Tucson from Yuma without owning it, and this one does too. **Recommendation: drop it.**

## Part VII — The Sunset articles (S1–S6)

**Where the friction is.** The Sunset road touches the settled Hispano valley at one point, Mesilla; west of it the road crosses Apache country to Tucson. Three groups are at risk from Texan traffic. The Hispanos remember the Texans as the invaders of 1841 and the foragers of 1862, who were refused, resisted and in one case killed (Colton p. 113). The Apaches have Baylor's Apache Extermination Order, which sanctioned the murder of Apaches at a peace parley (Nelson ch. 11); Texan traffic would bring that habit onto Pacifica's road, where Pacifica is making its own peace (N5). And Mesilla has its own Confederates: Southern sympathizers who held a secession convention in March 1861 (Colton pp. 9, 14) and the *Mesilla Times*, founded by pro-Confederate Anglos in 1860 (Nelson ch. 1), a Confederate party inside the compact.

**Why Richmond signs.** It could not hold Arizona; Sibley proved it. It gets the use of the road without garrisons or an Apache war, with Pacifican credit and the Matamoros terms (C-1863-MATAMOROS). What it wanted in the West was trade and gold, not presence. Its commissioner, James Reily, wrote in 1862 that with Sonora and Chihuahua Texas would be "the great highway of nations" (Nelson ch. 8); the articles give Texas a share of a highway on someone else's road. Texas's own resolution for S5 costs it nothing it still had (C8, S5).

**How solid.** S1 and S5 are solid: easy to verify, and Richmond gains nothing by breaking them. Mesilla stays the friction point (incidents, a local Confederate party, the occasional hothead); Pacifica answers with courts, not soldiers (S3). S4 is the weak point, and only its second part breaks. Texas demands its fugitives, Pacifica refuses, and the dispute recurs until 1871–72, when canon's fugitive agreement with Richmond (§13) returns fugitives and refuses them entry at the Texas line until abolition in 1888–89. The first part survives: no master brings an enslaved person onto Pacifican soil and keeps him there, because Pacifica's own ban on slavery is not part of the bargain. The refuge is the first thing Pacifica trades away, and the people of Mesilla, who watched the 1863 article printed in their plaza, watch it go. In the long run the problem changes owner: the railway era brings Texan workers and cattle, and canon's dissolution of 1897–1900 makes Texas part of the Gulf Republic, Pacifica's partner, by which time the transit is old habit.
