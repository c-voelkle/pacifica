## What this CR does

It writes the instruments that turn occupation and consent into title, in the order that makes each one possible:

1. **Spring 1865, the armistices.** McClellan's armistice with Pacifica carries one public term on the South, which consuls can see kept. Logan's mission ends, and Logan stays in New Orleans as a private engineer.
2. **Late 1865, the Western treaty is signed.** Its Oregon article is conditional on Oregon's vote, and McClellan holds the treaty until the vote. This is why a peace agreed in 1865 waits until 1867 for the Senate.
3. **December 1866 – March 1867, Alaska.** Russia decides for Pacifica and signs before the Senate debates, so the Union cannot hope to recover a Pacific port by buying one.
4. **April 1867, the Senate.** The first instalment lies in gold at the New York Subtreasury, the Navy has priced the alternative, and a coalition built question by question consents.
5. **Summer–autumn 1867, exchange.** The first payment against ratifications, evacuation of Port Townsend and Fort Union, and the handover at Sitka.

It does not re-decide the February 1865 attack at Mobile (CR-063), the stalled Atlanta campaign (CR-064), the election of 1864 or the Valley (CR-065, which also owns the review's constraint C5), or the Pacific sealift (CR-059).

## Part I — The armistices (A1–A6)

**What McClellan needs.** He won as a Union man (CR-015 A2). An armistice with Richmond is an executive act he can defend; one with Pacifica that left Pacifican officers running Confederate yards would hand the Radicals their speech. So the Pacifica armistice carries one public term on the South, and only one.

**Why it is this term and no other.** The test is what a consul can see. A military mission is men in uniform at Mobile and New Orleans; arms are crates on the Matamoros wharf and wagons on the Rio Grande road. Hess's numbers say what crossed by that road was never bulk (review C3), so the visible items are the ones that mattered. Credit is different: it moves in bills and ledgers, and checking it would need inspectors in Monterey's books. Elias refuses that, and McClellan does not press, because a term he cannot verify is a term the Senate will call a fraud. Principle 4 holds: the Union's grievance stays aimed at Richmond's forts, not at Pacifica's accounts.

**Logan.** Canon §13 already has Logan's "Louisiana repair relationships" as "an engineering base and commercial opportunity". The armistice turns that into a status. His orders and pay end; the mission's men sail home; he stays, on his own account, with the yards, pilots and suppliers he knows. He carries Farragut's death with him (CR-015 F4). The promise is tested once (principle 5): when Richmond's navy offers his yard a contract in 1866, a private engineer is free to take it and Pacifica would be blamed. The recommended answer is that he refuses while the armistice holds, and the Union consul's report says so. The page shows a promise kept by a man Pacifica no longer pays. In 1870 he lays the mines again under Pacifica's flag (canon §13).

**The Amity treaty.** Canon gives it recognition and leaves "wartime alliance clauses" OPEN. This CR closes that OPEN: there are none (CT3). Signed after Pacifica's armistice, it can be read against A2 by anyone.

**T1, the signatories.** For the Union: McClellan's Secretary of State (name OPEN; whoever CR-065 or a later CR puts in the cabinet). Option: a commission with a Democratic senator, on the precedent of senators named to later joint commissions (Hist.; to verify); not recommended, because it would put a signatory on the committee that reports the treaty. For Pacifica:
- **(a) Vallejo and Sherman (recommended).** Vallejo signed the 1848 convention in Washington (canon §4, CR-028) and now signs the instrument that ends it (T8). Sherman is the officer Washington trusts and the advocate of modest terms (A5). The cost is the charge Wade makes in 1867, "a treaty negotiated by one Sherman and ratified by the other", and it is why John Sherman keeps out of the negotiation and takes only the money question in 1867.
- (b) Elias in person: maximal authority, but in December 1865 he is securing the Enabling Law (canon §12), and his presence would make the treaty his conquest in Washington's eyes.
- (c) Pacifica's minister at Washington (name OPEN).

**The articles' reasons.**
- **T5:** Grimes's question is whether Union commerce reaches the Pacific without a Union port. Most-favoured entry, published charges and arbitration answer it. A storehouse does not, because Pacifica remembers Sausalito (canon §9).
- **T6:** Harlan and Ramsey need named routes they can show their states before any railway exists. Fort Benton stays Union and Helena Pacifican (H5), so the road between them is the Montana trade's door.
- **T9:** the waiver is principle 4 in an article. Without it, Wade can move that Pacifica pay for the mines that killed Farragut, and Pacifica can answer with the Hawaiian sailor. With it, the Union's dead stay charged to Richmond, and Victoria's price is that the sailor's family is not waived with the state's claim.
- **T10 and T11:** civil protection without distinction of colour is the term Sumner can cite on the floor. A clause against rendition is the term he might ask for, and the plan drops it, because in 1871 Pacifica would breach a U.S. treaty by returning fugitives to Richmond and give Washington a grievance against Pacifica (principle 4). The silence is deliberate, and the reader should see it in 1871.
- **T13:** the line is drawn to Texas's 1850 boundary, so the treaty names no sovereign in Austin.

**The agon's last move.** In 1861 Lincoln argued that the Union of the states is perpetual (Hist.). The treaty is drafted so that it never has to answer him. It cedes no state: Oregon, Washington, Washoe and the rest are territories (principle 1). Washoe is a territory only if CR-060 CT2 is adopted; otherwise canon's State of Nevada, admitted in October 1864, is the one state the treaty cedes, and this paragraph's argument fails for it, and the Constitution gives Congress power to dispose of territory belonging to the United States (Art. IV §3, Hist.). The nearest precedent is Webster–Ashburton (1842), which settled a disputed claim with the consent and payment of the state concerned (Hist.; to verify). Every argument Lincoln made for the South survives the treaty, and so does the claim to the South. Elias's design concedes Lincoln's principle in order to take what it does not protect. Whether the private citizen Lincoln answers in 1867 is OPEN (open decision 16). This verdict, that the treaty never has to answer Lincoln, belongs to this argument; the repair pass of 8 November 2026 removed it from the §12 storyline text, which now says only that the treaty cedes territories, not states.

## Part III — Holding the treaty (O1–O7)

**Where the condition lives.** CR-059 puts the Oregon article in the treaty (V1, Part VI) and, in CT14, the condition in the armistice convention. The two are reconciled by separating the article from its machinery. The signed treaty carries an Oregon article that cedes Oregon as Oregon's voters choose under the protocol (O1) and states no condition of its own. A supplementary armistice protocol, signed the same day and in force at once, carries the vote's machinery and the no-branch (O3). After the vote the protocol of certification (O5) records the returns and strikes the no-branch. The Senate never votes on a conditional cession or on a disarmed Union Oregon. The ledger then reads as CT14 asks: C-1865-ARMISTICE carries the condition, C-1867-WESTERN-LINE the record of Oregon's choice.

**Why McClellan holds it.** First, a conditional cession is a weak text to send: Republicans would vote on a Union Oregon without a naval station, and Democrats on a vote not yet held. Second, the Senate's best argument, that Oregon left by its own choice, needs the choice. Third, consent elsewhere arrives only in 1866: Washington Territory's convention, Idaho's June vote, Washoe's and western Montana's charters, New Mexico's convention and Deseret's legislature (CR-059, CR-060, CR-061).

**Why not December 1866.** Oregon votes in October and the returns are certified in November–December (CR-059). McClellan's first count, taken in December, falls short: senators see a deferred payment and the loss of the last Pacific port, and a Union bid for Russian America is still imaginable as a replacement. He waits for three things: Russia's signature (K6), the gold on deposit (S2), and the Senate of the 40th Congress.

## Part IV — Alaska (K1–K10)

**Russia's position (Hist.).** The Company was weak and the colony could not be held against Britain. Historically the special meeting of 16 December 1866 decided to sell to the United States. Here the meeting has a different report before it. Stoeckl writes from Washington that the Union is about to cede Puget Sound and the Columbia and will have no Pacific harbour to use the colony from or defend it with, and that a Senate ceding the West will not vote money for land beyond it (A8).

**The offer.** Pacifica's envoy brings $7.2 million in Pacifica's own gold coin, payable at handover, not on an appropriation. Historically Russia waited until July 1868, more than a year after signature and months after the handover, for the House to vote Seward's money (Hist.; to verify); a buyer that pays on delivery is worth more than one that pays when a legislature agrees. The figure is the historical price (Hist.); here it is MODEL until FIN-02.

**Gorchakov's question.** Russia's interest is that Britain not gain. The plan's phrase "Pacifica refused British protection in 1846" is not what canon says (CT4). Canon says something better for Gorchakov: in 1846 Pico asked for protection and Seymour declined; the Republic learned it would have no patron; in 1848 Britain declared it would take no part of California, and Elias refused even Buchanan's guarantee. A republic that has stood alone for twenty years, and has just fought the United States without a European ally, is the one buyer that strengthens neither Britain nor Washington. The optional pledge (K4) puts that history into an article, a promise later tested by the 1871 river grant (navigation, not cession, so it holds) and by the boundary with Canada (canon §25).

**Signature (K6), options:**

| Option | Date | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) Recommended | Early March 1867 (Gregorian; the Russian instrument carries the Julian date twelve days earlier) | The cable carries the news to Washington before McClellan sends the treaty (the Atlantic cable worked from 1866, Hist.; to verify); time for Foreign Relations to be told | None material |
| (b) | 30 March 1867 (Gregorian), the night Seward and Stoeckl historically signed (Hist.) | A mirror the reader may enjoy | Two days before the special session; too tight for the committee |
| (c) | 25 February signature, 8 March exchange (the author's earlier draft) | Russia bound by exchange, not only signature | Assumes Pacifica's ratification in a fortnight across two oceans |

**Payment (K10), options:** (a) in coin at San Francisco to Russia's commissioner on the day the flag changes at Sitka, shipped home on Russia's arrangement (recommended: the coin never leaves the mint's custody until the certificate arrives); (b) in coin aboard the Pacifican squadron at Sitka; (c) by bills on London (rejected: the plan removed London from the Alaska bargain because it would worry St Petersburg).

**The peoples.** The historical treaty treated the "uncivilized tribes" as subjects of whatever laws the buyer made (Hist.; to verify). Here the treaty transfers Russia's claim and leaves the nations' lands and rights to their own arrangements, as every cession in this settlement does (principle 2).
