## What this CR does

**The Gulf has no Union army.** New Orleans holds on 24 April 1862, so there is no Department of the Gulf as a field army, no XIX Corps in Louisiana, no Baton Rouge and no way for the XIII Corps to reach the Gulf past Port Hudson. Butler's 15,000 at Ship Island wait two months for a fleet that has to refit, then are recalled east after the Seven Days. Mobile survives 1862 because no one attacks it. A brigade stays to hold Pensacola, Ship Island and Key West for Farragut's squadron.

**The Texas coast is a naval matter.** Galveston is taken by Renshaw's squadron in October 1862 and retaken on New Year's Day (Hist.), with its few hundred soldiers sent from Pensacola. Sabine Pass and the Rio Grande expedition never sail. The Union answers Matamoros with a blockade station off the river's mouth and seizures at sea, not a landing, so Brownsville is never taken or retaken.

**Vicksburg's army walks out, and Port Hudson is never besieged.** On 18 May 1863 Pemberton's council of war takes Johnston's advice, with Richmond's authority behind it. The field army marches northeast and a rear garrison holds the works into the summer. Grant's army spends June to August on the escaped army. Halleck's "clean up" sends the XIII Corps against Port Hudson from the north in September, and Chickamauga recalls it before the investment closes.

**The corridor is a crossing, not a supply line.** What Pacifica lands at Matamoros and carries 700 miles and more by wagon is specie, rifles, machine tools, fuzes, mine components and men, never bulk. Its meaning is political and financial: the Confederacy that reaches the armistice is one state with one treasury, one Congress and one crossing, and that is the state Pacifica's credit binds after 1871.

**The February 1865 attack is the Union's one winter expedition.** Recommended (option M-a): there is no Fort Fisher. Without Sherman's march there is no army in the Carolinas for Wilmington to supply. The shipping and coal that history spent on Fort Fisher, Savannah and the XXIII Corps carry four broadside ironclads and about 8,000 of Terry's men from Hampton Roads to Pensacola (MODEL). Farragut, home in New York in December "in failing health" (Boatner), argues for the operation he has waited a year for and sails back to lead it. The approved canon outcome stands: the lead ironclad sinks in Logan's denser field, Buchanan's *Tennessee* engages, Farragut is killed and the Dauphin Island troops re-embark.

## Part I — New Orleans and what it leaves (N1–N4, S1–S6)

**The barrier.** Boatner's account settles what canon's "maintained obstruction" has to survive: high water broke the chain-and-hulk barrier in late February and again on 11 April, a week before the mortars opened. Historically it was never whole again. Here the technical mission does its first real work in that week, re-mooring the hulks and clearing the drift under Confederate engineers. The repair is the kind of thing Logan is good at: anchors, chain, timing, what a river in flood does to a mooring. It does not make the *Louisiana* move; she stays "unpowered" (Boatner). It gives her guns a fixed obstacle to cover. Boatner's other facts stay: the mortars' 2,997 shells on the 18th did "little damage", and the forts' defence does not depend on them failing.

**Pensacola's absence.** Canon sends USS *Pensacola* to the Pacific in autumn 1861 (§10). She was one of Farragut's big screw sloops at New Orleans (Hist.; to verify). Without her the column is weaker by a first-class ship, which CR-013 already says contributes to the balance without deciding it.

**Why Mobile survives 1862.** Three options, each with its reason.

| Option | What happens | Why Mobile survives | Cost to the story |
|---|---|---|---|
| S-a, recall (recommended) | Butler waits at Ship Island through May and June while Farragut refits; recalled to Fort Monroe in July after the Seven Days; a brigade stays | No one attacks it. By July the squadron is still repairing, the army is gone and Bragg's army is passing through the bay (Hess ch. 4) | None; the Union acts rationally, concentrating after a defeat in the main theatre |
| S-b, hold | All 15,000 stay at Pensacola as the Gulf's garrison | Only by an unexplained veto: an idle 15,000 fifty miles from a weak Mobile is the obvious instrument (review B2) | Requires Halleck to waste a corps; Butler, a political general, would not sit still |
| S-c, attack and repulse | A summer landing on the eastern shore against Mobile, repulsed | Mobile is reinforced after New Orleans holds, Bragg's army is crossing in July, and the season's fevers fall on Northern regiments (Hist.; to verify) | Doubles the Mobile legend and makes February 1865 the third attempt, which dilutes Farragut's death |

S-a is recommended. It follows the historical rule of 1862 that coastal forces went to Virginia when Virginia went badly, and the historical pattern of July 1864. It leaves Mobile with a single attack, the one that kills Farragut.

**Butler.** Without New Orleans, Butler's war loses its most famous year. There is no order on the women of New Orleans and no Confederate proclamation outlawing him (both Hist.; to verify), so the Confederacy has no personal grievance against him. He comes home a War Democrat with a division and no victory, and the East uses him as it historically did from late 1863: Fort Monroe, then the Army of the James (Hist.; to verify). Under M-a he commands no Fort Fisher expedition, and when Terry's divisions are detached for Mobile, Butler asks to go with them, as he historically went with the Fort Fisher troops (Hist.; to verify). Grant refuses (PROPOSED). Whether Grant relieves him in January 1865 is for CR-065.

## Part II — The Texas coast (T1–T6)

**Galveston** stays Hist. because it never depended on New Orleans for its capture: Renshaw's squadron took it without resistance (Boatner). The garrison did depend on New Orleans; here it comes from the Pensacola brigade, so the town's recapture on New Year's Day costs Pensacola three companies it cannot replace. That is one reason no landing is tried on the Rio Grande.

**Sabine Pass and the Rio Grande.** Both expeditions were the Department of the Gulf's (Boatner; Hess ch. 5; H&J p. 432) and both are struck. The sources review's §5 rows are carried out in CT3 and CT4.

**The Union's real counter.** Matamoros is a neutral port, so the Navy can watch the river's mouth but cannot blockade it. It can take Pacifican-flag ships anywhere, and it can take neutral ships whose cargo is contraband for the Confederacy (the *Peterhoff*, seized in 1863; Hist.; to verify). A landing of a few hundred at Brazos Santiago (option T-b, not recommended) would count wagons the Navy already counts from the sea. It would cost the Pensacola brigade's last spare companies and a 600-mile sea supply line (MODEL, to verify), and the trade would cross the Rio Grande higher up. Pacifica's answer to the seizures is to ship in neutral bottoms; the flag question is left to the CR that settles the Matamoros terms (C-1863-MATAMOROS, OPEN).

## Part III — Vicksburg's escape and why Port Hudson stands (V1–V6)

**The hinge is in Boatner.** On 18 May Pemberton had Johnston's advice to abandon the city and a council of war in which to take it, and he chose to defend. H&J (p. 410) have Johnston ordering him to save the army and march northeast while Pemberton "remained in paralyzed inactivity". What held him historically was the president's insistence on holding the river (Hist.; to verify). Canon already moves Richmond. This CR gives Davis a reason: the river below Vicksburg is not lost with Vicksburg while New Orleans and Port Hudson hold, and an army can be rearmed through the corridor. With Davis's authority in hand, the council of 18 May votes the other way. The rear garrison is the Pennsylvanian Pemberton's own compromise: he will not be the man who gave up the city. It is also the reason the city falls in summer (canon F2) and not in May.

**Grant's free men.** Boatner gives 71,000 at the siege. Here there is no siege to send them to, but there is a reason to send them: a Confederate field army of Pemberton's survivors and Johnston's new army in central Mississippi. Grant takes Jackson and drives it east. Beyond Jackson his army is at the end of a wagon road in a Mississippi summer, which is H&J's radius rule (fn. 14 p. 78): "no large army could move without rail or water communications" (p. 58).

**Halleck's clean-up.** After Vicksburg Halleck historically dispersed Grant's army: the XIII Corps to Banks (H&J p. 432), corps to Arkansas and Kentucky (Hist.; to verify), and the Lincoln–Halleck interest in the trans-Mississippi that "contributed to Chickamauga" (H&J p. 690). Here the first object of a man who wants "to clean up a little" (H&J pp. 430–431) is Port Hudson, the last Confederate fortress on the river. The XIII Corps, which cannot go to Banks, goes there instead. It does not have the garrison's measure: Port Hudson is supplied from New Orleans down the corridor, so this is a siege that would take weeks, not a starvation. Then Chickamauga takes everything. Historically Sherman's divisions left Vicksburg by steamer for Memphis from 22 September (Hess ch. 9); here the same order takes the XIII Corps back upriver. Port Hudson has been threatened once, from the north, and never invested.

**Interface.** CR-064 owns the field campaign in Mississippi (who commands the Army of the Tennessee without Sherman; the Jackson operation). This CR fixes only the hinge, the rear garrison and the Port Hudson consequence.

## Part IV — What crosses below Port Hudson (X1–X4)

**The historical baseline.** H&J (pp. 421–422, following Goff) hold that cutting the trans-Mississippi link "was not a vital blow in terms of supply management, for the two sections ... had long since ceased to rely on each other", and call the Mississippi conquest "almost a white elephant" except psychologically. Hess (ch. 9): cattle were swum across; 5,000 Texas cattle were seized at Natchez in July 1863; after July 1863 "only individuals and small groups could evade Union gunboat patrols."

**The wagon arithmetic (Hess ch. 6; MODEL where computed).**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Six-mule wagon payload | 1,500–2,500 lb in practice (Ingalls's maximum 2,674 lb; 1,000 on bad roads) |
| Forage per animal per day | 26 lb (14 hay, 12 grain), against 3 lb per man |
| Loaded pace | about 15 miles a day (26 on the open frontier) |
| Matamoros to the river below Port Hudson | 700 miles and more by road (MODEL; route to verify) |
| Days on the road at 15 a day | about 47 (MODEL) |
| A team's forage over that haul | about 7,300 lb, three to four times its payload (MODEL; Hess's derived figure: a one-ton payload is about 13 team-days of forage) |
| A ton of $20 gold coins | about 27,000 coins, about $540,000 (MODEL, at 33.4 g a coin) |

Grain, iron or powder in bulk cannot make that journey unless the teams graze or buy forage on the road, and even then a wagonload is worth only what it carries. A wagonload of coin carries half a million dollars. Rifles, fuzes and primers, machine tools and instrument parts, mine bodies and their firing gear sit between the two. Men walk. So the corridor carries what credit cannot buy in Europe and what only Pacifica has: its coin, its specialists and the small goods its workshops make. Bulk European goods reach the Confederacy as they did historically, by blockade-runners into Wilmington, Charleston and Mobile, bought on credit that may be Pacifican (Hist. for the runners; the credit is PROPOSED).

**What the crossing means.** The corridor's value is that the Confederacy is not cut in two. Its laws, mails, Congress, conscription, currency and courts run across the river; Texas's congressmen and Louisiana's state government do not become a separate department living on what it has; Richmond negotiates the armistice for one territory. That is why the Confederacy reaches 1865 as one country. For Pacifica it is what makes the debtor of 1871 worth binding: one customs administration, one debt and one railway across Texas to New Orleans (canon §13).

**Logan.** The cargo list is Logan's working life from 1864. What he can bring across decides what he can fix, which is why the *Tennessee*'s fuzes (M3) and the mine field's firing gear are in this CR and armour plate is not.

## Part V — The Union's armoured fleet without Ericsson (F1–F3; MODEL)

H&J record that after Hampton Roads the Union built 21 more ironclads in two months and 60 monitors eventually (pp. 130, 136). Here the alarm is greater (canon: *Minnesota* lost) and the answer is slower. The Union has no proven type: Galena's armour fails at Drewry's Bluff (Hist.; the alternate date is canon OPEN), and *New Ironsides* is the only seagoing design it trusts. A broadside ship of her size carries several times a coastal monitor's armour and needs a large-ship yard. The plate and yards that historically made about twenty coastal monitors by the end of 1864 (Hist.; to verify) make about a third as many broadside ships, and later (MODEL).

## Part VI — The February 1865 attack (M1–M7)

**The shipping problem (review B3).** Historically the winter of 1864–65 "occupied all the ocean steam vessels of the country" and "exhausted the accumulated stock of coal at Fort Monroe, City Point, and Annapolis" (Hess ch. 8): Fort Fisher twice (Butler's 6,500, 7–27 December; Terry's 8,000 with "nearly 60 vessels and 627 guns", 6–15 January: Boatner, PORTER), Sherman's supply at Savannah (Hist.; to verify), and Schofield's XXIII Corps moved 1,400 miles by rail and then by sea (Hess ch. 5, ch. 8). Shipping Sherman's 60,000 to Virginia would have taken two months (H&J p. 660). Canon's February attack is a second seaborne armoured expedition in the same weeks, against forts that in August 1864 needed a siege train and 5,500 troops (Boatner). Both cannot be mounted.

**Three options.**

| Option | What happens | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M-a, no Fort Fisher (recommended)** | The Union's one winter expedition is Mobile; Wilmington stays open to the armistice | Keeps every APPROVED row of F2 and F4. Canon already needs a Confederate Wilmington (the 1871 customs co-administration). Historically Wilmington's capture also served Sherman's march north (Hist.; to verify); here there is no Sherman and no march. Without Savannah and the XXIII Corps the coast's shipping can carry one expedition, five times as far (MODEL) | Welles and Porter wanted Fort Fisher and Lee's last port (Hist.; to verify); the choice must be argued on the page |
| M-b, Mobile in autumn 1864 | The attack falls before the election | A Gulf defeat in October would weigh on the vote | Breaks canon's "the fleet cannot spare sufficient seagoing armor before the election" (§11), F2's "8 Nov 1864: Mobile and its bay are untaken" and the lame-duck pairing with Petersburg. It changes the election beat, which is CR-065's: **not decided here** |
| M-c, a naval-only February attack | Fort Fisher happens; a smaller squadron tries Mobile without the Dauphin Island landing | Shipping fits | Retires an APPROVED sentence (the re-embarked troops). A January success at Fort Fisher gives the Union Wilmington at the armistice, against canon §12 and §13 |

**Why Lincoln chooses Mobile (M-a).** The decision is taken in December 1864 by a lame-duck president with Welles and Grant, and Farragut is in Washington to argue it. Grant has wanted Mobile since 1863 (Hist.; to verify) and needs it more now: the western army is stalled before Atlanta with Johnston on the Alabama flank (CR-064), and Mobile and its rivers to Selma and Montgomery (Hess ch. 8) are the back door of that flank. Farragut has the base, the squadron, a year of soundings and a plan; Fort Fisher would need the same four ironclads, a heavier bombardment and an assault on a land face nobody has tested. Welles and Porter argue for Wilmington, Lee's last port. Lincoln takes the operation that is ready, in the theatre where the war was lost. He wants the bay in Union hands before 4 March, because the armistice will freeze what each side holds.

**The force and the voyage (MODEL).** Hampton Roads to Pensacola is about 1,400 nautical miles (MODEL, to verify). Terry's two divisions and a siege train sail in January in transports coaled as Hess describes for Fort Fisher, and the colliers follow, because Pensacola has no coal of its own. The four broadside ironclads go south with them. The squadron at Pensacola supplies the wooden ships.

**The attack** is canon (F2, APPROVED), with three mechanisms added: the troops land first on Dauphin Island as Granger's did in August 1864 (Boatner); the *Tennessee*'s fuzes now work (M3); and the landing is undone by the fleet's failure, since Fort Gaines can now be supplied across a Confederate bay. Farragut's "order to ignore the mines" stays canon (F4); its wording is OPEN (CT-FARRAGUT).
