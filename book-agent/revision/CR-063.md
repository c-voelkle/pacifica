## What this CR does

**The Gulf has no Union field army.** New Orleans holds on 24 April 1862, so there is no Department of the Gulf as a field army, no XIX Corps in Louisiana, no Baton Rouge and no way for the XIII Corps to reach the Gulf past Port Hudson. Butler's 15,000 at Ship Island wait two months for a fleet that has to refit, then are recalled east after the Seven Days. Mobile survives 1862 because no one attacks it. A brigade stays to hold Pensacola, Ship Island and Key West for Farragut's squadron.

**The Texas coast is a naval matter.** Galveston is taken by Renshaw's squadron in October 1862 and retaken on New Year's Day (Hist.), with its few hundred soldiers sent from Pensacola. Sabine Pass and the Rio Grande expedition never sail. The Union answers Matamoros with a blockade station off the river's mouth and seizures at sea, not a landing, so Brownsville is never taken or retaken.

**Vicksburg's army walks out, and Port Hudson is never besieged.** With Davis's authority in hand since about 10 May, Pemberton's council of 17 May, after the Big Black bridge, gambles on the night the Union's XV Corps needs to cross at Bridgeport and sends the fresh divisions out by the Benton road at dusk; the trains and the rear guard are lost on the morning of the 18th. Pemberton holds the works with the two divisions beaten at Champion Hill and the bridge and surrenders them on about 4 July (MODEL). Grant's army spends May to July on the siege and on Johnston's swollen army at its back, and July on Jackson. Halleck's "clean up" sends the XIII Corps against Port Hudson from the north in September, and Chickamauga recalls it before the investment closes.

**The corridor is a crossing, not a supply line.** What Pacifica lands at Matamoros and carries 700 miles and more by wagon is specie, rifles, machine tools, fuzes, mine components and men, never bulk. Its meaning is political and financial: the Confederacy that reaches the armistice is one state with one treasury, one Congress and one crossing, and that is the state Pacifica's credit binds after 1871.

**The February 1865 attack is the Union's one winter expedition.** Option M-a (author, 12 November 2026): there is no Fort Fisher. Without Sherman's march there is no army in the Carolinas for Wilmington to supply. The shipping and coal that history spent on Fort Fisher, Savannah and the XXIII Corps carry three broadside ironclads and about 8,000 of Terry's men from Hampton Roads to Pensacola, where *New Ironsides* joins them from Charleston (MODEL). Farragut, home in New York in December "in failing health" (Boatner), argues for the operation he has waited a year for and sails back to lead it. The approved canon outcome stands: the lead ironclad sinks in Logan's denser field, Buchanan's *Tennessee* engages, Farragut is killed and the Dauphin Island troops re-embark.

## Part I — New Orleans and what it leaves (N1–N4, S1–S6)

**The barrier.** Boatner's account settles what canon's "maintained obstruction" has to survive: high water broke the chain-and-hulk barrier in late February and again on 11 April, a week before the mortars opened. Historically it was never whole again. Here the technical mission does its first real work in that week, re-mooring the hulks and clearing the drift under Confederate engineers. The repair is the kind of thing Logan is good at: anchors, chain, timing, what a river in flood does to a mooring. It does not make the *Louisiana* move; she stays "unpowered" (Boatner). It gives her guns a fixed obstacle to cover. Boatner's other facts stay: the mortars' 2,997 shells on the 18th did "little damage", and the forts' defence does not depend on them failing.

**Pensacola's absence.** Canon §10 names USS *Pensacola* as "the candidate additional seagoing diversion from the historical Gulf assignment", inside an **[OPEN]** (CT14). She was one of Farragut's big screw sloops at New Orleans, second in the first division (NHHC). If, as canon §10 proposes, *Pensacola* is in the Pacific, the column is weaker by a first-class ship, which CR-013 already says contributes to the balance without deciding it.

**Why Mobile survives 1862.** Three options, each with its reason.

| Option | What happens | Why Mobile survives | Cost to the story |
|---|---|---|---|
| S-a, recall (recommended) | Butler waits at Ship Island through May and June while Farragut refits; recalled to Fort Monroe in July after the Seven Days; a brigade stays | No one attacks it. By July the squadron is still repairing, the army is gone and Bragg's army is passing through the bay (Hess ch. 4) | None; the Union acts rationally, concentrating after a defeat in the main theatre |
| S-b, hold | All 15,000 stay at Pensacola as the Gulf's garrison | Only by an unexplained veto: an idle 15,000 fifty miles from a weak Mobile is the obvious instrument (review B2) | Requires Halleck to waste a corps; Butler, a political general, would not sit still |
| S-c, attack and repulse | A summer landing on the eastern shore against Mobile, repulsed | Mobile is reinforced after New Orleans holds, Bragg's army is crossing in July, and the season's fevers fall on Northern regiments (not checked) | Doubles the Mobile legend and makes February 1865 the third attempt, which dilutes Farragut's death |

S-a is recommended. It follows the historical rule of 1862 that coastal forces went to Virginia when Virginia went badly, and the historical pattern of July 1864. It leaves Mobile with a single attack, the one that kills Farragut.

**Butler.** Without New Orleans, Butler's war loses its most famous year. There is no order on the women of New Orleans and no Confederate proclamation outlawing him (historically 15 May and 23 December 1862: Encyclopedia Virginia), so the Confederacy has no personal grievance against him. He comes home a War Democrat with a division and no victory, and the East uses him as it historically did from late 1863: Fort Monroe from November 1863, then the Army of the James (Encyclopedia Virginia). Under M-a he commands no Fort Fisher expedition, and when Terry's divisions are detached for Mobile, Butler asks to go with them, as he historically went in person with the Fort Fisher expedition, arriving on 24 December (Mariners' Museum). Grant refuses (PROPOSED). Whether Grant relieves him in January 1865 is for CR-065.

## Part II — The Texas coast (T1–T6)

**Galveston** stays Hist. because it never depended on New Orleans for its capture: Renshaw's squadron took it without resistance (Boatner). The garrison did depend on New Orleans; here it comes from the Pensacola brigade, so the town's recapture on New Year's Day costs Pensacola three companies it cannot replace. That is one reason no landing is tried on the Rio Grande.

**Sabine Pass and the Rio Grande.** Both expeditions were the Department of the Gulf's (Boatner; Hess ch. 5; H&J p. 432) and both are struck. The sources review's §5 rows are carried out in CT3 and CT4.

**The Union's real counter.** Matamoros is a neutral port, so the Navy can watch the river's mouth but cannot blockade it. It can take Pacifican-flag ships anywhere, and it can take neutral ships whose cargo is contraband for the Confederacy (the *Peterhoff*, seized in February 1863 and condemned for her contraband in 1866: 72 U.S. 28). A landing of a few hundred at Brazos Santiago (option T-b, not recommended) would count wagons the Navy already counts from the sea. It would cost the Pensacola brigade's last spare companies and a sea supply line of about 630 nautical miles (NOAA: Pensacola–Port Isabel 632), and the trade would cross the Rio Grande higher up. ## Part III — Vicksburg's escape and why Port Hudson stands (V1–V6)

**What the record allows (checked 9 November 2026).** The first draft put the hinge where Boatner puts Johnston's advice, at the council of 18 May, and marched the army out that night. The record closes that door. Johnston's letter of 17 May reached Pemberton about noon on the 18th; the council that afternoon found the army could not be withdrawn in useful condition, and Union guns opened while it sat (OR I/24/1 p. 242). Sherman's XV Corps had crossed at Bridgeport during the night and was on the Benton road, the road northeast, by 9:30 a.m. (OR I/24/1 p. 755); by the morning of the 19th Grant had the city invested as far as his numbers allowed (Grant, *Memoirs* ch. 36). A march on the night of 18–19 May would have had to cut through the XV Corps. So the march has to start a night earlier, and the decision has to be taken before Johnston's letter arrives. That needs Davis's authority in Pemberton's hands before Champion Hill, which is what canon's "Richmond authorizes preservation of the mobile army before encirclement" already says.

**Why Pemberton can take it.** Historically Davis telegraphed on 7 May that both Vicksburg and Port Hudson must be held for the link with the trans-Mississippi (OR I/24/1 p. 190), and Pemberton, a Pennsylvanian promoted over Southern men, would not be the general who gave up the city; H&J (p. 410) have him in "paralyzed inactivity" under Johnston's contrary order. Here the river below Vicksburg is not lost with it while New Orleans and Port Hudson hold, and an army can be rearmed through the corridor (Part IV), so Davis's telegram about 10 May says the opposite (V2). It does not make Pemberton bold. It gives him permission, and he uses it only when Champion Hill and the bridge have shown him what staying means.

## Part IV — What crosses below Port Hudson (X1–X4)

**The historical baseline.** H&J (pp. 421–422, following Goff) hold that cutting the trans-Mississippi link "was not a vital blow in terms of supply management, for the two sections ... had long since ceased to rely on each other", and call the Mississippi conquest "almost a white elephant" except psychologically. Hess (ch. 9): cattle were swum across; 5,000 Texas cattle were seized at Natchez in July 1863; after July 1863 "only individuals and small groups could evade Union gunboat patrols."

**The wagon arithmetic (Hess ch. 6; MODEL where computed).**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Six-mule wagon payload | 1,500–2,500 lb in practice (Ingalls's maximum 2,674 lb; 1,000 on bad roads) |
| Forage per animal per day | 26 lb (14 hay, 12 grain), against 3 lb per man |
| Loaded pace | about 15 miles a day (26 on the open frontier) |
| Matamoros to the river below Port Hudson | 700 miles and more by road (MODEL: the Cotton Road from Brownsville to the Alleyton railhead; then, with New Orleans Confederate, by Niblett's Bluff to the Opelousas railroad at Brashear City and on to Algiers, or by Niblett's Bluff to Alexandria, about 130 miles of military road; the rail on parts of the way shortens the wagon haul; the total was not found) |
| Days on the road at 15 a day | about 47 (MODEL) |
| A team's forage over that haul | about 7,300 lb, three to five times its payload (MODEL; Hess's derived figure: a one-ton payload is about 13 team-days of forage) |
| A ton of $20 gold coins | about 27,000 coins, about $540,000 (MODEL, at 33.4 g a coin) |

Grain, iron or powder in bulk cannot make that journey unless the teams graze or buy forage on the road, and even then a wagonload is worth only what it carries. A wagonload of coin carries half a million dollars. Rifles, fuzes and primers, machine tools and instrument parts, mine bodies and their firing gear sit between the two. Men walk. So the corridor carries what credit cannot buy in Europe and what only Pacifica has: its coin, its specialists and the small goods its workshops make. Bulk European goods reach the Confederacy as they did historically, by blockade-runners into Wilmington, Charleston and Mobile, bought on credit that may be Pacifican (Hist. for the runners; the credit is PROPOSED).

**What the crossing means.** The corridor's value is that the Confederacy is not cut in two. Its laws, mails, Congress, conscription, currency and courts run across the river; Texas's congressmen and Louisiana's state government do not become a separate department living on what it has; Richmond negotiates the armistice for one territory. That is why the Confederacy reaches 1865 as one country. The verdict that the crossing, not its cargo, keeps the Confederacy one country, and the wagon arithmetic above (a team's forage over the haul is three to five times its payload; a wagonload of coin is worth about half a million dollars), were in the §11 storyline text and were moved here by the set review's repair pass (8 November 2026); the storyline now lists what crosses and leaves the reader to weigh it. For Pacifica it is what makes the debtor of 1871 worth binding: one customs administration, one debt and one railway across Texas to New Orleans (canon §13).

**Logan.** The cargo list is Logan's working life from 1864. What he can bring across decides what he can fix, which is why the *Tennessee*'s fuzes (M3) and the mine field's firing gear are in this CR and armour plate is not.

## Part VI — The February 1865 attack (M1–M7)

**The shipping problem (review B3).** Historically the winter of 1864–65 "occupied all the ocean steam vessels of the country" and "exhausted the accumulated stock of coal at Fort Monroe, City Point, and Annapolis" (Hess ch. 8): Fort Fisher twice (Butler's 6,500, 7–27 December; Terry's 8,000 with "nearly 60 vessels and 627 guns", 6–15 January: Boatner, PORTER), Sherman's supply at Savannah (Hist.; not checked), and Schofield's XXIII Corps moved 1,400 miles by rail and then by sea (Hess ch. 5, ch. 8). Shipping Sherman's 60,000 to Virginia would have taken two months (H&J p. 660). Canon's February attack is a second seaborne armoured expedition in the same weeks, against forts that in August 1864 needed a siege train and 5,500 troops (Boatner). Both cannot be mounted.

**Why Lincoln chooses Mobile (M-a).** The decision is taken in December 1864 by a lame-duck president with Welles and Grant, and Farragut is in Washington to argue it. Grant has wanted Mobile since 1863: he proposed it after Vicksburg, in July and about 1 August 1863, and Halleck and Lincoln put Texas first (Grant, *Memoirs* ch. 39; Lincoln to Grant, 9 August 1863); he proposed it again that winter (ch. 46). He needs it more now: the western army is stalled before Atlanta, with Polk's army on the Alabama flank and Johnston on the Chattahoochee (CR-064), and Mobile and its rivers to Selma and Montgomery (Hess ch. 8) are the back door of that flank. Farragut has the base, the squadron, a year of soundings and a plan; Fort Fisher would need the same four ironclads, a heavier bombardment and an assault on a land face nobody has tested. Welles and Porter argue for Wilmington, Lee's last port. Lincoln takes the operation that is ready, in the theatre where the war was lost. He wants the bay in Union hands before 4 March, while he still has the authority to take it and before a successor who may negotiate takes office.

**The force and the voyage (MODEL).** Hampton Roads to Pensacola is about 1,480 nautical miles by Key West (NOAA, *Distances Between United States Ports*: Norfolk–Key West 969, Key West–Pensacola 510). Terry's two divisions and a siege train sail in January in transports coaled as Hess describes for Fort Fisher, and the colliers follow, because Pensacola has no coal of its own. Three broadside ironclads go south with them, and *New Ironsides* comes down from Charleston. The squadron at Pensacola supplies the wooden ships.

**The attack** is canon (F2, APPROVED), with three mechanisms added: the troops land first on Dauphin Island as Granger's did in August 1864 (Boatner); the *Tennessee*'s fuzes now work (M3); and the landing is undone by the fleet's failure, since Fort Gaines can now be supplied across a Confederate bay. Farragut's "order to ignore the mines" stays canon (F4); its wording is OPEN (CT-FARRAGUT).

**The attack, step by step (M5–M6; added 9 November 2026 and revised after the fresh-context review; dates and figures MODEL; the August 1864 facts marked Hist. are from the general literature and to check).**

Canon fixes the outcome (F2, F4, APPROVED): the lead armoured ship sinks in Logan's denser field, the column breaks under Fort Morgan, the *Tennessee* engages, Farragut is killed by the mines, and his order to ignore them is the order that sank his fleet; the squadron withdraws with heavy losses and the Dauphin Island troops re-embark. What follows makes that outcome the product of the August 1864 plan meeting a different bay, not of Union incompetence. Farragut fights the battle he historically won; the changes are in the water, in the *Tennessee*, and in the season.

0. *What each side knows (January 1865).* A year at Pensacola has given Farragut the bay's soundings, the Confederates' buoys and refugees' accounts of the torpedo line (Hist. pattern; to check). He knows the field runs from Fort Gaines eastward across the shoal water and leaves the deep channel under Fort Morgan open for blockade-runners, marked by a buoy, as it did in August 1864 (Hist.; to check). He does not know that in the autumn the district's chief engineer (historically Victor von Sheliha; to check), on Logan's survey of the old field, ordered a second, staggered row laid in the channel's western half, and that Logan's men laid it: fewer torpedoes than the old line, but larger, boiler-iron cases with charges several times a keg's, and firing gear that works, the cases, caps and primers having come across from Matamoros (X1). Many of the old line's kegs are waterlogged (Hist.: corrosion; to check). His plan is August's: ironclads in a line on the fort side, the wooden ships lashed in pairs to port so that a disabled ship is towed through, a flood tide to carry the cripples in, and a wind that blows the smoke onto the fort's gunners (Hist.; to check).
1. *Late January: Pensacola.* Farragut, back from the North by mail steamer (M4), hoists his flag in *Hartford*, which has refitted at Pensacola. The three broadside ironclads from Hampton Roads and *New Ironsides* from Charleston coal there from colliers sent ahead (M5). Terry's divisions, among them a division of United States Colored Troops (Hist. for Terry's Fort Fisher force; to check), wait in camp on Santa Rosa Island; the Pensacola brigade adds about 1,500. The broadside ships' difficulty is not draught: *New Ironsides* drew about 15 feet 8 inches, less than *Hartford*, which crossed the bar in August (to check). It is handling. They are long, slow to answer the helm and wide in the turn, where the monitors of August turned short (Hist.; to check).
2. *Early February, days 1–4: the landing.* After the Hampton Roads conference fails (3 February; Hist. date, CR-065's scene), Terry lands about 8,000 men with the siege train on the western end of Dauphin Island, where Granger landed in August (Hist.; to check), under the wooden gunboats' cover, keeping the Pensacola battalion afloat as a reserve. A norther stops the surf boats on the second day for a day and a half, and the heavy guns and the colliers' coal are not ashore and aboard until day 4 (MODEL; February northers on the Gulf coast, to check). Terry's men dig toward Fort Gaines.
3. *Days 4–8: waiting for the tide, and the sweep.* Farragut needs three things together: a flood tide in the morning, a westerly or south-westerly wind, and Terry's batteries bearing on Fort Gaines so that its guns are busy. They come together on day 8 (MODEL). In the nights before, his boats go in, as Union boats did before the August attack (Hist.; to check), find the old line's waterlogged kegs where his chart puts them and lift some. The sweep confirms his chart; it does not find the new row, which lies deeper in the channel and under Fort Morgan's guns, where boats cannot work at night without being seen. Fort Gaines's water front stays open meanwhile: the Union's light-draught gunboats cannot enter the bay behind Dauphin Island past Fort Powell at Grant's Pass (Hist. geography; to check), and Confederate steamers bring the garrison rations and men by night from Mobile. Gaines cannot be starved while the bay is Confederate. Terry needs the fleet inside.
4. *Day 8 (mid-February): the attack.* At first light on the flood, with a light south-westerly wind, the column comes in: the four ironclads in line on the starboard side, nearest the fort; the wooden ships in pairs to port, Farragut in *Hartford* second in that line, with *Brooklyn* ahead of her as in August (Hist.; to check). Fort Morgan opens at about two miles.
5. *The lead ironclad.* The leading broadside ship, steering for the buoy, finds the column crowding her toward its western side, as *Tecumseh* did in August (Hist.), and, wide in the turn, swings inside it into what her chart shows as clear water. The new row is there. A boiler-iron charge explodes under her bow. A broadside ship has a monitor's armour several times over and much more buoyancy, and she does not go down in minutes as *Tecumseh* did: she settles by the head over about twenty minutes, under Fort Morgan's guns, and most of her crew of about 400 are lost with her or in the water (MODEL). The second ironclad stops her engines. *Brooklyn* backs, as she did in August (Hist.), and the column bunches in the channel under the fort, which the wind has not cleared of smoke; for some minutes it is the best target Fort Morgan's gunners have had in the war.
6. *Farragut's order.* As in August, Farragut takes *Hartford* past *Brooklyn* and across the torpedo line to lead the column in, and orders the rest to follow her (canon F4; its wording is OPEN, CT-FARRAGUT). In August the torpedoes the ships struck failed to fire (Hist.; to check). Here *Hartford* clears the old line, where the kegs are dead, and strikes one of the new row's cases a cable's length on. The explosion opens her bow and brings down the main rigging, where Farragut stands lashed above the smoke. He falls with the men around him, mortally hurt (MODEL; the manner within the explosion is OPEN). He dies as the *Tennessee* comes down, which keeps canon's order: the lead ship is struck, the column breaks, the *Tennessee* engages, Farragut is killed. The order he gave a minute earlier sends the paired ships behind him into the same water, and a second sloop strikes and is towed back out by her consort, burning (MODEL). This is the order that, in this world, sank his fleet (F4).
7. *The* Tennessee. Buchanan, watching from inside the bay, does not come down on the head of the column as he historically did in August (Hist.; to check). He holds the *Tennessee* back with the *Nashville* and *Huntsville* (F2, APPROVED) until he can see that the ships coming through will come one at a time, and then takes them one at a time: first *Hartford*, down by the bow inside the line with her admiral dying and still afloat, then whatever follows her through the gap. The *Tennessee*'s steering chains are under cover, her port shutters work, her primers fire and her shells burst (M3); at about five or six knots she cannot chase, and she does not have to. In August she was rammed by three ships and lost her steering and her ports (Hist.; to check).
8. *The withdrawal.* *Hartford*'s captain (historically Percival Drayton in August; to check), now senior in the bay with a sinking flagship and two ships inside, takes them back out past the fort on the ebb that afternoon, *Hartford* in tow, rather than fight the *Tennessee* in a bay he cannot leave if she cripples him (MODEL). The squadron's losses are the lead ironclad, the burnt sloop, *Hartford* beached outside the bar under the fleet's guns (MODEL; Pensacola is some forty miles off, too far to tow a ship settling by the bow) and about 800 men killed, wounded and drowned (MODEL).
9. *Days 9–12: Dauphin Island.* With the bay Confederate and the *Tennessee*, the *Nashville* and the *Huntsville* loose in it, Fort Gaines is supplied by night as before, and Terry's flank on the bay shore lies under the ironclads' guns. He asks for the fleet to try again; there is no second armoured column, and the borrowed shipping and coal are wanted home. Terry re-embarks over the outer beach in four nights, losing the heaviest of his siege guns (MODEL). The Black regiments come off last but one, and every man of them knows why it matters that none is left on the beach (PROPOSED).
10. *After.* Farragut's body goes home. The order to ignore the mines becomes the symbol of a lost fleet (F4); Grant later makes the dead admiral his symbol (F4). Logan learns from the Confederate newspapers whose death his field has bought (canon §11).

**What a reader who knows August 1864 should see.** The plan is the same; the bay is not. Three things differ, and each has a cause on the page: a second row of larger torpedoes with working firing gear, ordered by the district's engineer on Logan's survey and laid by his men with Matamoros cargo; a *Tennessee* whose steering, shutters, primers and fuzes work (M3), under a Buchanan who has learned to wait; and a February sea, which makes the landing slow and the re-embarkation forced. The broadside ships are slower in the turn than August's monitors, which is why the lead ship is where the new row is, and the boats' sweep finds only the old line, which is why Farragut believes his chart. Nothing depends on a Union mistake that Farragut did not historically make.
