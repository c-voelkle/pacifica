## What this CR does

It settles canon §11's standing OPEN, "Specific Union command functions must be reassigned because Sherman is Pacifican", and the review's constraints C1 (Shiloh), C4 (Atlanta) and C7 (Hampton Roads).

**The argument in one paragraph.** H&J say the strategy of raids "distinctly belonged to Grant" (p. 696), but only Sherman executed it and gave it its political dimension; Baldy Smith and Sheridan shared the outlook, Thomas did not (p. 661), and Halleck and Lincoln never grasped it (p. 513). Take Sherman out and Grant's strategy has no western executor. The western army group falls to Thomas by seniority and size; Thomas is methodical and stays on his single-track railroad, which Hess shows "could not support further operations south of Atlanta" (Preface). Johnston, given no cause to be removed, keeps his army whole and puts part of it on the Alabama flank that H&J say "could … perhaps have forestalled Atlanta's capitulation" and left "a stalemate" (pp. 630, 688). Atlanta is untaken on 8 November 1864, not because Johnston defends the city, which H&J say he could not have done, but because nobody on the Union side will cut loose.

**Its five main proposals.**
1. **Halleck goes east, Sherman stays (H1–H4).** Halleck is an American lawyer in San Francisco who never naturalized. He sails for the East in May 1861, before the rupture. Sherman holds a Vallejo commission and has been a citizen since 1857–58.
2. **Shiloh on 5 April is a gain of hours, not a day (S1–S8).** The army clears Corinth on the morning of the 3rd and the 4th goes to plan. Against Buell's leading division at Savannah that buys hours; against Crittenden and McCook it buys a day. On the second day Grant has about half the fresh men he had historically (MODEL), so he holds the landing but cannot counterattack. Brigadier General John McArthur commands the green division at Shiloh Church.
3. **The western command (C1–C8).** Steele commands the XV Corps, then Blair, then Logan. McPherson takes the Army of the Tennessee in October 1863 when Grant rises. Thomas takes the Military Division of the Mississippi in March 1864. McPherson, not Sherman, keeps Grant in the army in June 1862.
4. **No Meridian raid, and a four-front 1864 plan (R1–R5).** The plan has no Gulf army. A. J. Smith's divisions stay in the West and shield Thomas's line against Forrest.
5. **The Atlanta campaign stalls (A1–A10; step by step in Part V, revised 9 November 2026).** Thomas makes his own Snake Creek Gap move with two corps and their trains and is a day too slow; Polk's divisions hold Resaca as historically, and Johnston gets out. Thomas then moves methodically along the railroad. In June Johnston sends Polk's infantry, enlarged by Vicksburg escapees, back west to the Blue Mountain railhead with a written plan for Davis. On election day the two armies face each other across the Chattahoochee and Atlanta's two railroads are running.

**Also in this CR:** the precise Hampton Roads sentence and Drewry's Bluff with Galena alone (E1–E5), and the check that no officer canon has made Pacifican appears in the Union order of battle (O1–O5).

**OPEN items in canon this CR would close:** §11's Union command functions; §11's "duration of the Corinth delay"; Hampton Roads' "exact March sequence … Norfolk operations and Virginia's disposal" (to the historical anchors); Drewry's Bluff's "whether these operations occur" (it occurs and fails as historically); §11's "Exact Eastern assignments" (historical).

## Part I — Why Halleck goes east and Sherman does not (H1–H4)

Both men were in California in 1861 (H&J pp. 54–55), and a reader of the character bible will ask why one is the Union's general in chief and the other the Republic's Inspector-General. The difference is that Sherman made a choice in 1857 and Halleck never did.

- **Halleck's business is an alien's business.** A lawyer in Mexican land titles serves American buyers before a foreign republic's courts. A railway presidency is an investment. Both can be run from New York, and both are hostages in a republic heading toward war with his country. He has no Pacifican office to give up.
- **His ambition points east.** He wrote the American army's textbook (*Elements of Military Art and Science*, 1846, from his Lowell lectures). Scott thinks him the best mind in the army and wants him in Washington (historically Lincoln appointed him on Scott's recommendation in August 1861: Abraham Lincoln's Classroom). A man with that standing does not wait in San Francisco for a foreign government's war.
- **He took no commission.** Historically he was a California militia major general (H&J). Here that would have been a foreign commission, and he refused it, as an American who expected to go home. That refusal is the line canon draws between him and Sherman.
- **Sherman did the opposite** (canon §8; CT-SHERMAN). When his banking ended about 1857 he took the Vallejo appointment, and he became a citizen in 1857–58, before the war. His household is Californian. Canon makes the decision costly: Ellen, the children and his American family "must materially affect" it. By 1861 he is the Republic's Inspector-General with an oath given in Monterey.

**Why Pacifica lets Halleck go.** In May 1861 he is a private American and the Republic is not yet at war. The Protector's mandate of late May promises civilian protections and no forced allegiance (canon §9), and holding him would break it before it began. The cost is real, and the story can show it. The Union's best-informed officer on Pacifica's army is in Washington. Every rational Union choice about the West that follows is his: hold Fort Union rather than reinforce Santa Fe (CR-061), refuse the coast its coin (CR-059 I2), send the Sound four regiments and not a division (CR-059 W13). Halleck is the rational antagonist in this war, as Lincoln is in the argument of ideas.

**The parting (scene, PROPOSED).** Sherman sees Halleck onto the Panama steamer in May 1861. Neither asks the other to change sides.

## Part II — Shiloh: the honest mechanism (S1–S8)

**What canon says and what the sources allow.** Canon has the attack on 5 April "gaining a day through earlier movement rather than an unexplained long delay to Buell". The sources show where the historical day was lost. The plan was to attack on the 4th. The army barely left Corinth by nightfall on the 3rd, the 4th went slowly, and the 5th was spent deploying in fog until 4 p.m. (H&J pp. 163–164). Boatner says the march "took a day longer than estimated to cover the intervening 25 miles". So a dawn attack on the 5th recovers one of the two days that slipped. It does not need the march to run to the original plan.

**What has to go differently on 3–4 April.** Two things that went wrong go right: Hardee's corps clears Corinth in the morning of the 3rd instead of at 3 p.m., and the march of the 4th goes as ordered, so the army is in line before that night's heavy rain, which historically caught it on the road and left it about twelve hours behind (NPS, *Shiloh*, sec. 3). The rain still falls; it falls on men already deployed, and the wagon jam at Michie's on the 5th, which held Bragg until about 4 p.m., never forms. Both are staff and road contingencies. They are not Pacifica's doing, and the draft says so rather than inventing a cause. Beauregard's objection, that surprise is lost, is made on the evening of the 4th and overruled as it was on the 5th (S2).

**What it buys against Buell.** Buell's army was strung out on the road from Columbia, delayed ten days by the flooded Duck River (Boatner). Only its leading division was at Savannah on 5 April (H&J p. 164; Nelson arrived that day, the others on the 6th and in the night of the 6th–7th: Epperson; NPS). Historically Nelson's lead brigade crossed in time to fight late on the first day and the rest of the division crossed that night (Boatner). An attack a day earlier changes nothing there, because Nelson reached Savannah on the 5th either way. The day it gains is against Crittenden and McCook, who fought on the historical second day. That is canon's outcome: Grant holds, and he cannot mount the second-day recovery.

**The diverted regiments and the green division.**
- Four Iowa regiments, about 3,000 men (MODEL), are on the Panama road. Day 1 at Pittsburg Landing becomes roughly 33,900 Union against 40,335 Confederate. Historically it was about 36,900 (H&J's 42,682 less Lew Wallace's division, about 5,800; MODEL from Hist.).
- **McArthur at Shiloh Church.** Halleck and Grant had no spare general in March 1862. The brigadiers confirmed late in March went to the divisions Grant already had, and W. H. L. Wallace took C. F. Smith's when Smith was injured (Hist.; McArthur led a brigade in it). The 5th Division goes upriver under its senior colonel, and Grant gives it to McArthur, a Donelson brigade commander he trusts, when the confirmations arrive.
- **Why not the senior colonel (McDowell of the 6th Iowa; the colonels' seniority not found)?** Grant would not leave the army's exposed right under a colonel if a brigadier were at hand.
- **Why McArthur rather than Lauman?** McArthur's later record at division level (Vicksburg, the 6th Division of the XVII Corps; Nashville, the 1st Division of the XVI Corps; he was superseded in division command at Corinth: Wikipedia, to check) makes him credible. His inexperience at Shiloh makes the early break credible without making him a fool.
- **What is lost is Sherman himself:** the man whose division took the first assault and fell back behind Shiloh Church without breaking (Hist.; the hour not confirmed).

**Why the outcome stays modest.**
- The Hornet's Nest still holds for most of the day.
- Johnston still dies in mid-afternoon (Boatner, about 2:30 p.m.).
- The last line at the landing, with its massed guns and the gunboats, is where it was.
- Beauregard halts at dusk as historically (H&J p. 169), and his army is as exhausted and intermingled.

What changes is the morning after. Grant has Lew Wallace and Nelson, about 11,500 fresh men (MODEL), not about 25,000. Beauregard has the camps. Neither can attack with hope. Beauregard sees Buell's next division coming up the river and goes back to Corinth with his trophies.

**Consequences.** Canon's "Beauregard accepts a costly operational respite" holds. The Confederacy has a claimed victory and a dead commander. Halleck arrives, takes the field and advances on Corinth slowly, as historically. Grant is shelved, and the cost of Sherman's absence comes due a second time (C2).

## Part III — The western command (C1–C8)

**June 1862.** Historically Grant, made second in command and ignored, meant to leave, and Sherman talked him out of it (Grant, *Memoirs* ch. 27; Sherman, *Memoirs* ch. 11). It is the largest single function Sherman performed for the Union before Vicksburg, and it has to be reassigned or canon's Vicksburg falls. McPherson is the recommended replacement: Grant's engineer at Shiloh, liked and trusted, and the man this CR raises to Sherman's later place. Rawlins, Grant's adjutant, is the alternative (OPEN). In either case Halleck's call to Washington on 11 July gives Grant his command back.

**The XV Corps and the river.**
- Steele, a classmate and a West Pointer, is the natural replacement in December 1862 (class of 1843 with Grant; a division commander at Chickasaw Bayou historically).
- Chickasaw Bayou keeps its historical outcome under him.
- Canon's Vicksburg (Grant and Porter cross below, Pemberton escapes in part) is untouched.
- After Vicksburg the corps passes to Blair, then to Logan as historically, and Steele goes to Arkansas.

**Chattanooga.** McPherson takes the Army of the Tennessee in October 1863 (Hist. March 1864) and brings the XV Corps east. That leaves the Vicksburg district under corps commanders who hold rather than raid, which is one cause of R1. Missionary Ridge stays historical in outcome.

**March 1864: Thomas, and the alternatives.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Thomas (recommended)** | Senior among the major generals of volunteers; the largest army; Chickamauga and Missionary Ridge; Halleck's regard; the Army of the Cumberland would not take a junior over him | Grant's own judgement (H&J p. 661); Thomas does not share the raiding outlook (p. 696) |
| (b) McPherson over Thomas | Grant's protégé and an army commander already; senior to Thomas in the regular army | Junior as a volunteer; an insult to the Army of the Cumberland; and H&J name only Sherman, Smith and Sheridan as sharing the outlook, so McPherson would not raid either |
| (c) Baldy Smith sent west instead of kept east | He shares the outlook (H&J); Grant admired his Chattanooga work (Hist.; not checked) | Junior to both; quarrelsome; Grant wants him on the James |

Option (a) also turns on knowledge. Grant knows exactly what he gives up: he wrote the sentence about Thomas. The reader sees him choose correctly by every rule of the service and lose the campaign by it. The rational antagonist loses because his institutions work as designed.

## Part IV — The strategy of raids and the plan of 1864 (R1–R5)

H&J's judgement is the spine. The strategy of exhausting the Confederacy by raids "distinctly belonged to Grant" (p. 696). Sherman executed it and gave it its political dimension. The Meridian raid of February 1864 was its rehearsal (p. 506). Halleck and Lincoln never grasped it (p. 513).

Without Sherman the strategy stays an idea in Grant's head. It has to be carried out by subordinates who think in railroads. Meigs's elation that Sherman's raiders could move more than 25 miles from rail or river "without perishing" (p. 661) never comes.

**The plan without the Gulf.** Grant's simultaneous advances become four. Banks's move on Mobile goes because there is no Department of the Gulf army (CR-063). Halleck's "trans-Mississippi preoccupation" (H&J p. 690) has no Red River to spend itself on, so A. J. Smith's divisions stay where history borrowed them from. This is the one place where Sherman's absence comes with a Union gain, and it is small: historically Forrest never did effective damage to the line anyway (H&J p. 612), and Smith's Tupelo expedition kept him off it in July. Here Smith is in place from April. Thomas's slowness is therefore a matter of doctrine and supply, not of raids.

## Part V — The Atlanta campaign stalls (A1–A10)

**The constraint.** H&J hold that Johnston "had not been equal to the task of stopping the more powerful Union army" and could not have held Atlanta by defence (pp. 606, 630, 688). Canon's sentence, "Johnston … has held Atlanta past the election", must therefore not be a siege won. It has to be a campaign that never reaches the siege.

**What the record changed (9 November 2026).** The first draft kept Polk's Army of Mississippi in Alabama from the start, so that it never joined Johnston. The record shows why that cannot stand: Polk's divisions are what held Resaca in May. Cantey's division came from Mobile on 7 May and was the "about 4,000" (Castel, *Decision in the West* p. 138) that stopped McPherson on the 9th; Loring's arrived on the 10th to 12th with Polk himself. Without them, Thomas's own Snake Creek Gap plan, with a bigger army than McPherson's, takes the railroad behind Johnston and ends canon's 1864. So Polk comes east in May as historically, and goes back west in June because the campaign Thomas fights makes the flank worth more than the men. Johnston's strength without Polk was about 53,800, not the 60,000 of the first draft (ABT).

**The sequence (A1–A10; dates Hist. where marked, otherwise MODEL).**

0. *The commands.* Thomas commands the Military Division from March and keeps the Army of the Cumberland in his own hands (C7; open decision 6): its three corps under Howard (IV), Palmer (XIV) and Hooker (XX) (Hist. corps commanders; to check). McPherson has the Army of the Tennessee, short two divisions of the XVII Corps on veterans' furlough as historically, Schofield the Army of the Ohio. About 99,000 effectives against Johnston's 53,800 at Dalton (ABT).
1. *7 May: the advance.* As historically, the Union columns close on Rocky Face Ridge and the Mill Creek Gap in front of Dalton (Hist.).
2. *8–10 May: Snake Creek Gap, Thomas's plan.* Thomas does as group commander what he urged on Sherman. McPherson's army, Schofield's and Howard's IV Corps press Rocky Face to hold Johnston at Dalton. Palmer's and Hooker's corps, about 40,000, march by Villanow to the gap. Thomas sends their trains with them, because he does not send an army where he cannot feed it, and so two corps with their wagons take two days on one track through a four-mile gorge (MODEL; McPherson's 25,000 historically took from 8 May to the next morning: Cox; Georgia marker 064-8). Hooker's leading division comes out on the morning of the 9th, as McPherson's did, and finds Cantey's men entrenched before Resaca. Its commander does what McPherson did historically and what Thomas's orders require: he will not attack works he cannot see round with one division while the rest of the corps is in the gorge. By then Johnston knows (Hist.: by about 10 a.m. on the 9th). Historically he railed three of Hood's divisions to Resaca that night and, when McPherson fell back, brought most of them back. Here the gap is full of an army, and they stay. Loring's division reaches Resaca from Rome on the 10th (Hist.). On the night of the 10th Johnston leaves Dalton, two nights earlier than he historically did, behind Wheeler's screen.
3. *11–14 May: Resaca.* Thomas's two corps are out of the gap on the 10th. He attacks on the 12th and 13th, with McPherson and Schofield coming down the railroad behind Johnston's rear guard, against about 60,000 Confederates in works along Camp Creek and the Oostanaula (MODEL). He gains ground and not the river. On the night of the 14th Johnston crosses the Oostanaula and burns the bridges (Hist. pattern: the night of 15 May). Loss for loss it is the historical battle a day early. **The risk stated plainly:** had Hooker's lead division attacked Cantey on the 9th, with perhaps 5,000 in front of it, it might have cut the railroad; the reason it does not is McPherson's historical reason, made a rule by Thomas. A reader who thinks Thomas would have taken that chance has the record of his whole career against him.
4. *Late May: the Etowah.* Johnston falls back across the Etowah by about 20 May (Hist.). Thomas rebuilds the railroad bridge and comes up to the Allatoona position on the line. He does not swing to Dallas (A3).
5. *Early June: Johnston's plan, and Polk goes west (A5, A8).* Facing a commander who will not leave his railroad, Johnston writes to Davis. Atlanta's two lines cannot feed nearly 70,000 men and their animals for a summer; the Army of Mississippi can be fed from Selma, Mobile and Meridian on its own line; at Blue Mountain it stands beyond Thomas's reach and within four days' march of his line; Thomas will have to guard every mile he takes. Polk carries the letter and takes his three infantry divisions west, about 15,000 (MODEL), and leaves Jackson's cavalry with Johnston. Davis, Polk's friend, approves a plan Polk brings in person. Polk is not on Pine Mountain on 14 June.
6. *June–August: Allatoona and Kennesaw.* Johnston, at about 55,000 with the cavalry (MODEL), holds the Allatoona heights and then the Kennesaw line. Thomas goes at each by short flanks back to the railroad, repairs as he goes, and builds blockhouses and depots. He garrisons Rome, Kingston and the Etowah bridge and keeps Schofield on his right, toward Alabama. There are no Confederate sorties and no July battles round Atlanta: Hood historically lost about 11,000 in July (ABT; H&J pp. 608–623 give 15,000) and here loses none.
7. *16 July: Davis's question.* Historically the question that preceded Johnston's removal (NPS sec. 6). Here Johnston is on the Kennesaw line, the Union army has spent two months on forty miles of railroad, and his plan has been on Davis's desk for six weeks. Davis does not ask; Hood remains a corps commander.
8. *September: the Chattahoochee.* Thomas reaches the river and takes bridgeheads at the railroad crossing and upstream. Johnston holds the works on the south bank north of Atlanta (Hist.: the river line Johnston built in July).
9. *September–October: cavalry, not infantry.* To reach Atlanta's railroads Thomas would have to swing his infantry south of the city, as six Union corps historically did from 25 August (ABT; Hess ch. 10), uncovering his line with Polk four days away. He sends his cavalry instead, as Sherman sent Kilpatrick in August 1864 (Hist.; to check), and the Macon and Western is repaired in days. Thomas's cavalry is in character: in 1865 he sent Wilson's 13,500 against Selma (Encyclopedia of Alabama). His infantry stays on its railroad.
10. *October: Grant.* Grant, tied to Petersburg, presses Thomas by telegraph. He drafts an order giving the western command to Schofield and suspends it, as he did before Nashville on 9 December 1864 (Grant, *Memoirs* ch. 60; ABT). Lincoln, a month from the election, will not relieve the general whose army is in contact. Thomas answers that he will move when he is ready.
11. *8 November.* Atlanta is untaken and its railways are running; the armies are in contact on the Chattahoochee, and Polk stands at Blue Mountain.

**Why the stall now holds.** It no longer rests on Polk's absence in May. It rests on three things a reader can count: a commander who will not leave his railroad (A3, steps 4 and 9); an army whose supply cannot feed all of Johnston's men in one place, which turns a weakness into a flank (step 5); and the absence of Hood (step 6), which keeps the Army of Tennessee whole into the autumn. Each has its history: Thomas's caution at Nashville, Atlanta's two railroads, Johnston's own record.

**The Vicksburg escapees.** About 14,000 escape (MODEL; CR-063 V3 as revised on 9 November 2026: they leave on the night of 17–18 May, and about 14,000 more hold the city with Pemberton until early July). They are in the armies that history filled with paroled men: about 8,000 (MODEL) in Polk's Army of Mississippi by the spring of 1864, the rest in the Army of Tennessee and Mississippi's garrisons. The alternative, all of them in Georgia (OPEN), makes Johnston's army stronger at Dalton and changes nothing in the stall, which no longer depends on where they are.

**Mobile's part, kept modest.** Atlanta's own supply hangs on the Macon and Western and the Atlanta and West Point (Hess ch. 10). The second of these runs to Montgomery and so, by the Alabama River, to Mobile. Mobile and Selma feed the Alabama flank more than they feed Atlanta. Canon's "help sustain" is right as long as it stays "help" (CT4).

**What the stalemate is for the story.** In Washington the West offers Lincoln nothing to show before November. In Richmond, Joseph E. Johnston becomes the general who was never driven out of Georgia. That reputation makes canon's 1867 election and his presidency from February 1868 (§13, APPROVED) believable. In Monterey, Sherman follows the campaign he would have commanded in the Eastern papers and will not talk about it. Elias reads it as the largest return the Republic ever had on a naturalization, and he does not say so to Sherman (PROPOSED).

## Part VI — Hampton Roads and the Peninsula (E1–E5)

The review (C7) shows that canon's caution is right and can be made precise. Without Monitor, what changes at Hampton Roads is the battle and the anchorage, not the campaign.
- Virginia wins a larger victory on 8–9 March (canon), but at 22 feet and 4 knots she cannot reach Fort Monroe or go to sea (H&J; Boatner).
- The James was closed to McClellan's transports with or without Monitor (Boatner).
- McClellan uses the York, as he would have anyway, and is held at Yorktown by its batteries.
- Norfolk falls about 9 May, and Virginia, unable to go up the James or to sea, is destroyed (Boatner; NHHC 11 May).
- The Union's answer in March is improvised (E3).
- Drewry's Bluff is attacked on 15 May by Galena without Monitor and fails as it did historically. (The comparison was also in the §11 storyline text; the set review's repair pass of 8 November 2026 removed it there, and it belongs here and in `historical_background.md`.) Canon's rule holds: a historical failure is not turned into a new Confederate victory.

The rest of the Eastern war stays historical, and canon should say so in one sentence (open decision 9).
