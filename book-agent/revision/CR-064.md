## What this CR does

It settles canon §11's standing OPEN, "Specific Union command functions must be reassigned because Sherman is Pacifican", and the review's constraints C1 (Shiloh), C4 (Atlanta) and C7 (Hampton Roads).

**The argument in one paragraph.** H&J say the strategy of raids "distinctly belonged to Grant" (p. 696), but only Sherman executed it and gave it its political dimension; Baldy Smith and Sheridan shared the outlook, Thomas did not (p. 661), and Halleck and Lincoln never grasped it (p. 513). Take Sherman out and Grant's strategy has no western executor. The western army group falls to Thomas by seniority and size; Thomas is methodical and stays on his single-track railroad, which Hess shows "could not support further operations south of Atlanta" (Preface). Johnston, given no cause to be removed, keeps his army whole and puts part of it on the Alabama flank that H&J say "could … perhaps have forestalled Atlanta's capitulation" and left "a stalemate" (pp. 630, 688). Atlanta is untaken on 8 November 1864, not because Johnston defends the city, which H&J say he could not have done, but because nobody on the Union side will cut loose.

**Its five main proposals.**
1. **Halleck goes east, Sherman stays (H1–H4).** Halleck is an American lawyer in San Francisco who never naturalized. He sails for the East in May 1861, before the rupture. Sherman holds a Vallejo commission and has been a citizen since 1857–58.
2. **Shiloh on 5 April is a gain of hours, not a day (S1–S8).** The army clears Corinth on the morning of the 3rd and the 4th goes to plan. Against Buell's leading division at Savannah that buys hours; against Crittenden and McCook it buys a day. On the second day Grant has about half the fresh men he had historically (MODEL), so he holds the landing but cannot counterattack. Brigadier General John McArthur commands the green division at Shiloh Church.
3. **The western command (C1–C8).** Steele commands the XV Corps, then Blair, then Logan. McPherson takes the Army of the Tennessee in October 1863 when Grant rises. Thomas takes the Military Division of the Mississippi in March 1864. McPherson, not Sherman, keeps Grant in the army in June 1862.
4. **No Meridian raid, and a four-front 1864 plan (R1–R5).** The plan has no Gulf army. A. J. Smith's divisions stay in the West and shield Thomas's line against Forrest.
5. **The Atlanta campaign stalls (A1–A10).** Thomas moves methodically along the railroad. Johnston falls back slowly and gives Davis a written plan. Polk's Army of Mississippi, enlarged by the Vicksburg escapees, stands on the Blue Mountain–Selma railroad from June. On election day the two armies face each other across the Chattahoochee and Atlanta's two railroads are running.

**Also in this CR:** the precise Hampton Roads sentence and Drewry's Bluff with Galena alone (E1–E5), and the check that no officer canon has made Pacifican appears in the Union order of battle (O1–O5).

**OPEN items in canon this CR would close:** §11's Union command functions; §11's "duration of the Corinth delay"; Hampton Roads' "exact March sequence … Norfolk operations and Virginia's disposal" (to the historical anchors); Drewry's Bluff's "whether these operations occur" (it occurs and fails as historically); §11's "Exact Eastern assignments" (historical).

## Part I — Why Halleck goes east and Sherman does not (H1–H4)

Both men were in California in 1861 (H&J pp. 54–55), and a reader of the character bible will ask why one is the Union's general in chief and the other the Republic's Inspector-General. The difference is that Sherman made a choice in 1857 and Halleck never did.

- **Halleck's business is an alien's business.** A lawyer in Mexican land titles serves American buyers before a foreign republic's courts. A railway presidency is an investment. Both can be run from New York, and both are hostages in a republic heading toward war with his country. He has no Pacifican office to give up.
- **His ambition points east.** He wrote the American army's textbook (*Elements of Military Art and Science*, 1846; Hist.; to verify). Scott thinks him the best mind in the army and wants him in Washington (Hist.; to verify). A man with that standing does not wait in San Francisco for a foreign government's war.
- **He took no commission.** Historically he was a California militia major general (H&J). Here that would have been a foreign commission, and he refused it, as an American who expected to go home. That refusal is the line canon draws between him and Sherman.
- **Sherman did the opposite** (canon §8; CT-SHERMAN). When his banking ended about 1857 he took the Vallejo appointment, and he became a citizen in 1857–58, before the war. His household is Californian. Canon makes the decision costly: Ellen, the children and his American family "must materially affect" it. By 1861 he is the Republic's Inspector-General with an oath given in Monterey.

**Why Pacifica lets Halleck go.** In May 1861 he is a private American and the Republic is not yet at war. The Protector's mandate of late May promises civilian protections and no forced allegiance (canon §9), and holding him would break it before it began. The cost is real, and the story can show it. The Union's best-informed officer on Pacifica's army is in Washington. Every rational Union choice about the West that follows is his: hold Fort Union rather than reinforce Santa Fe (CR-061), refuse the coast its coin (CR-059 I2), send the Sound four regiments and not a division (CR-059 W13). Halleck is the rational antagonist in this war, as Lincoln is in the argument of ideas.

**The parting (scene, PROPOSED).** Sherman sees Halleck onto the Panama steamer in May 1861. Neither asks the other to change sides.

## Part II — Shiloh: the honest mechanism (S1–S8)

**What canon says and what the sources allow.** Canon has the attack on 5 April "gaining a day through earlier movement rather than an unexplained long delay to Buell". The sources show where the historical day was lost. The plan was to attack on the 4th. The army barely left Corinth by nightfall on the 3rd, the 4th went slowly, and the 5th was spent deploying in fog until 4 p.m. (H&J pp. 163–164). Boatner says the march "took a day longer than estimated to cover the intervening 25 miles". So a dawn attack on the 5th recovers one of the two days that slipped. It does not need the march to run to the original plan.

**What has to go differently on 3–4 April.** Two things that went wrong go right: the columns clear Corinth in the morning of the 3rd, and the corps do not block each other on the 4th. Both are staff and road contingencies. They are not Pacifica's doing, and the draft says so rather than inventing a cause. Beauregard's objection, that surprise is lost, is made on the evening of the 4th and overruled as it was on the 5th (S2).

**What it buys against Buell.** Buell's army was strung out on the road from Columbia, delayed ten days by the flooded Duck River (Boatner). Only its leading division was at Savannah on 5 April (H&J p. 164; arrival days to verify). Historically Nelson's lead brigade crossed in time to fight late on the first day and the rest of the division crossed that night (Boatner). An attack a day earlier changes nothing there, because Nelson reached Savannah on the 5th either way. The day it gains is against Crittenden and McCook, who fought on the historical second day. That is canon's outcome: Grant holds, and he cannot mount the second-day recovery.

**The diverted regiments and the green division.**
- Four Iowa regiments, about 3,000 men (MODEL), are on the Panama road. Day 1 at Pittsburg Landing becomes roughly 32,700 Union against 40,335 Confederate. Historically it was about 35,700 (H&J's 42,682 less Lew Wallace's division, about 7,000; MODEL from Hist.).
- **McArthur at Shiloh Church.** Halleck and Grant had no spare general in March 1862. The brigadiers confirmed late in March went to the divisions Grant already had, and W. H. L. Wallace took C. F. Smith's when Smith was injured (Hist.; to verify). The 5th Division goes upriver under its senior colonel, and Grant gives it to McArthur, a Donelson brigade commander he trusts, when the confirmations arrive.
- **Why not the senior colonel (McDowell of the 6th Iowa, seniority to verify)?** Grant would not leave the army's exposed right under a colonel if a brigadier were at hand.
- **Why McArthur rather than Lauman?** McArthur's later record at division level (Corinth, Vicksburg, Nashville; Hist.; to verify) makes him credible. His inexperience at Shiloh makes the early break credible without making him a fool.
- **What is lost is Sherman himself:** the man who held the right until mid-morning and then fell back without breaking (Hist.; to verify).

**Why the outcome stays modest.**
- The Hornet's Nest still holds for most of the day.
- Johnston still dies in mid-afternoon (Boatner, about 2:30 p.m.).
- The last line at the landing, with its massed guns and the gunboats, is where it was.
- Beauregard halts at dusk as historically (H&J p. 169), and his army is as exhausted and intermingled.

What changes is the morning after. Grant has Lew Wallace and Nelson, about 11,500 fresh men (MODEL), not about 25,000. Beauregard has the camps. Neither can attack with hope. Beauregard sees Buell's next division coming up the river and goes back to Corinth with his trophies.

**Consequences.** Canon's "Beauregard accepts a costly operational respite" holds. The Confederacy has a claimed victory and a dead commander. Halleck arrives, takes the field and advances on Corinth slowly, as historically. Grant is shelved, and the cost of Sherman's absence comes due a second time (C2).

## Part III — The western command (C1–C8)

**June 1862.** Historically Grant, made second in command and ignored, meant to leave, and Sherman talked him out of it (Hist.; to verify). It is the largest single function Sherman performed for the Union before Vicksburg, and it has to be reassigned or canon's Vicksburg falls. McPherson is the recommended replacement: Grant's engineer at Shiloh, liked and trusted, and the man this CR raises to Sherman's later place. Rawlins, Grant's adjutant, is the alternative (OPEN). In either case Halleck's call to Washington on 11 July gives Grant his command back.

**The XV Corps and the river.**
- Steele, a classmate and a West Pointer, is the natural replacement in December 1862 (Hist. class; to verify).
- Chickasaw Bayou keeps its historical outcome under him.
- Canon's Vicksburg (Grant and Porter cross below, Pemberton escapes in part) is untouched.
- After Vicksburg the corps passes to Blair, then to Logan as historically, and Steele goes to Arkansas.

**Chattanooga.** McPherson takes the Army of the Tennessee in October 1863 (Hist. March 1864) and brings the XV Corps east. That leaves the Vicksburg district under corps commanders who hold rather than raid, which is one cause of R1. Missionary Ridge stays historical in outcome.

**March 1864: Thomas, and the alternatives.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Thomas (recommended)** | Senior; the largest army; Chickamauga and Missionary Ridge; Halleck's regard; the Army of the Cumberland would not take a junior over him | Grant's own judgement (H&J p. 661); Thomas does not share the raiding outlook (p. 696) |
| (b) McPherson over Thomas | Grant's protégé and an army commander already | Junior; an insult to the Army of the Cumberland; and H&J name only Sherman, Smith and Sheridan as sharing the outlook, so McPherson would not raid either |

Option (a) also turns on knowledge. Grant knows exactly what he gives up: he wrote the sentence about Thomas. The reader sees him choose correctly by every rule of the service and lose the campaign by it. The rational antagonist loses because his institutions work as designed.

## Part IV — The strategy of raids and the plan of 1864 (R1–R5)

H&J's judgement is the spine. The strategy of exhausting the Confederacy by raids "distinctly belonged to Grant" (p. 696). Sherman executed it and gave it its political dimension. The Meridian raid of February 1864 was its rehearsal (p. 506). Halleck and Lincoln never grasped it (p. 513).

Without Sherman the strategy stays an idea in Grant's head. It has to be carried out by subordinates who think in railroads. Meigs's elation that Sherman's raiders could move more than 25 miles from rail or river "without perishing" (p. 661) never comes.

**The plan without the Gulf.** Grant's simultaneous advances become four. Banks's move on Mobile goes because there is no Department of the Gulf army (CR-063). Halleck's "trans-Mississippi preoccupation" (H&J p. 690) has no Red River to spend itself on, so A. J. Smith's divisions stay where history borrowed them from. This is the one place where Sherman's absence comes with a Union gain. Forrest never reaches Thomas's line in strength. Thomas's slowness is therefore a matter of doctrine and supply, not of raids.

## Part V — The Atlanta campaign stalls (A1–A10)

**The constraint.** H&J hold that Johnston "had not been equal to the task of stopping the more powerful Union army" and could not have held Atlanta by defence (pp. 606, 630, 688). Canon's sentence, "Johnston … has held Atlanta past the election", must therefore not be a siege won. It has to be a campaign that never reaches the siege.

**The mechanism, step by step.**
1. *A methodical commander on a single track.* The line runs 350 miles from Louisville, single-tracked, for 100,000 men (Hess Preface, ch. 10). Thomas flanks only toward the railroad behind his enemy, never away from his own, and he will not leave his depot for twenty days of wagon supply. He is slower at every position where Sherman flanked wide (A2–A4).
2. *A Johnston who keeps his army.* With no Hood and no July sorties (Hood lost about 15,000 men in July historically against 6,000 Union, H&J pp. 608–623), the Army of Tennessee reaches autumn whole.
3. *The Alabama flank.* H&J's alternative is a flank position on the Blue Mountain–Selma railroad. It is real here because Meridian was never raided (R2), and because the Vicksburg escapees give Polk the men (canon §11: "much of the mobile Confederate army survives"). Polk holds it from June (date OPEN).
4. *Thomas's answer is to guard.* The flank sits beyond his wagon radius, so he can neither strike it nor ignore it. He garrisons Rome and the Etowah and keeps Schofield on the flank, and each mile he advances beyond the Chattahoochee lengthens what he has to guard.
5. *No cutting loose.* What took Atlanta historically was six infantry corps cutting both railways south of the city (Hess ch. 10). Thomas will not uncover his line to do it with Polk on his flank.
6. *Davis's reason never comes.* Johnston has not retreated to the city, and his plan is written and endorsed by Polk.

**The Vicksburg escapees.** The decision: they join Johnston's war, but in Alabama, not in Georgia. About 15,000 escape (MODEL; CR-063 sets the number). About 8,000 (MODEL) are in Polk's Army of Mississippi by spring 1864. The rest hold Mississippi, the Port Hudson approaches (CR-063) and Meridian. The alternative, all of them in Georgia (OPEN), makes Johnston's main army stronger and the flank weaker. The flank is the mechanism, so the recommendation is Alabama.

**Mobile's part, kept modest.** Atlanta's own supply hangs on the Macon and Western and the Atlanta and West Point (Hess ch. 10). The second of these runs to Montgomery and so, by the Alabama River, to Mobile. Mobile and Selma feed the Alabama flank more than they feed Atlanta. Canon's "help sustain" is right as long as it stays "help" (CT4).

**What the stalemate is for the story.** In Washington the West offers Lincoln nothing to show before November. In Richmond, Joseph E. Johnston becomes the general who was never driven out of Georgia. That reputation makes canon's 1867 election and his presidency from February 1868 (§13, APPROVED) believable. In Monterey, Sherman follows the campaign he would have commanded in the Eastern papers and will not talk about it. Elias reads it as the largest return the Republic ever had on a naturalization, and he does not say so to Sherman (PROPOSED).

## Part VI — Hampton Roads and the Peninsula (E1–E5)

The review (C7) shows that canon's caution is right and can be made precise. Without Monitor, what changes at Hampton Roads is the battle and the anchorage, not the campaign.
- Virginia wins a larger victory on 8–9 March (canon), but at 22 feet and 4 knots she cannot reach Fort Monroe or go to sea (H&J; Boatner).
- The James was closed to McClellan's transports with or without Monitor (Boatner).
- McClellan uses the York, as he would have anyway, and is held at Yorktown by its batteries.
- Norfolk falls about 9 May, and Virginia, unable to go up the James or to sea, is destroyed (Boatner; NHHC 11 May).
- The Union's answer in March is improvised (E3).
- Drewry's Bluff is attacked on 15 May by Galena without Monitor and fails as it did historically. Canon's rule holds: a historical failure is not turned into a new Confederate victory.

The rest of the Eastern war stays historical, and canon should say so in one sentence (open decision 9).
