## What this CR does

It settles canon §11's standing OPEN, "Specific Union command functions must be reassigned because Sherman is Pacifican", and the review's constraints C1 (Shiloh), C4 (Atlanta) and C7 (Hampton Roads).

**The argument in one paragraph.** H&J say the strategy of raids "distinctly belonged to Grant" (p. 696), but only Sherman executed it and gave it its political dimension; Baldy Smith and Sheridan shared the outlook, Thomas did not (p. 661), and Halleck and Lincoln never grasped it (p. 513). Take Sherman out and Grant's strategy has no western executor. The western army group falls to Thomas by seniority and size; Thomas is methodical and stays on his single-track railroad, which Hess shows "could not support further operations south of Atlanta" (Preface). Johnston, given no cause to be removed, keeps his army whole and puts part of it on the Alabama flank that H&J say "could … perhaps have forestalled Atlanta's capitulation" and left "a stalemate" (pp. 630, 688). Atlanta is untaken on 8 November 1864, not because Johnston defends the city, which H&J say he could not have done, but because nobody on the Union side will cut loose.

**Its five main proposals.**
1. **Halleck goes east, Sherman stays (H1–H4).** Halleck is an American lawyer in San Francisco who never naturalized. He sails for the East in May 1861, before the rupture. Sherman holds a Vallejo commission and has been a citizen since 1857–58.
2. **Shiloh on 5 April gains nothing against Nelson and a day against the rest of Buell (S1–S8).** The army clears Corinth on the morning of the 3rd and the 4th goes to plan. Buell's leading division reaches Savannah on the 5th either way; Crittenden and McCook, who fought on the historical second day, are a day short. On the second day Grant has about half the fresh men he had historically (MODEL), so he holds the landing but cannot counterattack. Brigadier General John McArthur commands the green division at Shiloh Church.
3. **The western command (C1–C8).** Steele commands the XV Corps, then Blair, then Logan. McPherson takes the Army of the Tennessee in October 1863 when Grant rises. Thomas takes the Military Division of the Mississippi in March 1864. McPherson, not Sherman, keeps Grant in the army in June 1862.
4. **No Meridian raid, and a four-front 1864 plan (R1–R5).** The plan has no Gulf army. A. J. Smith's divisions stay in the West and shield Thomas's line against Forrest.
5. **The Atlanta campaign stalls (A1–A10; step by step in Part V, revised 9 November 2026).** Thomas makes his own Snake Creek Gap move with two corps and their trains and is a day too slow; Polk's divisions hold Resaca as historically, and Johnston gets out. Thomas then moves methodically along the railroad. In June Davis, afraid for Selma, orders a force back to Alabama; Johnston protests, then makes it his plan: Polk, with Cantey's division, Vicksburg escapees and S. D. Lee's cavalry, stands at the Blue Mountain railhead and strikes Thomas's line once in August. On election day the two armies face each other across the Chattahoochee and Atlanta's two railroads are running.

**Also in this CR:** the precise Hampton Roads sentence and Drewry's Bluff with Galena alone (E1–E5), and the check that no officer canon has made Pacifican appears in the Union order of battle (O1–O5).

### H · Halleck and Sherman, 1861

| ID | Decision |
|---|---|
| H1 | **Halleck is in California as a foreign lawyer.** He came out as a civilian lawyer. Historically he arrived with the Navy in 1847, and his military-government and convention service cannot stand in an independent California (CT-SHERMAN's note applies to him too). Here he comes in the early 1850s, about when he historically resigned from the army, in August 1854 (date OPEN). His practice is in Mexican land titles before the Republic's courts and register (CR-033), for American buyers of grants. He is president of the Atlantic and Pacific Railroad, the San Francisco–San José line (H&J pp. 54–55: "railroad president"; California State Military Museum), and his firm, Halleck, Peachy & Billings, handled more than half the land-grant claims after the Land Act of 1851 (Hist.; secondary sources). He never naturalizes |
| H2 | **No Californian commission, by his own choice (revised after the fresh-context review).** Historically Halleck was a California militia major general in 1861 (H&J pp. 54–55). In this republic residents can hold militia rank, as Sherman did before he naturalized (canon §8: his "militia affairs" of 1856), and as Hooker historically held a militia colonelcy in 1859–61. Halleck refuses it: an American who means to go home does not take a foreign republic's commission. The line between him and Sherman is citizenship, which Sherman takes in 1857–58 and Halleck never does; the refused commission is the sign of it |
| H3 | **He sails for the East in May 1861 (a PROPOSED divergence),** on the first Panama steamer after Sumter's news, before the packet incident and Article XIV (canon §9, late May). Historically he was still in California when Lincoln, on Scott's recommendation, made him a major general of the regular army on 19 August 1861, and he reached Washington only about 7 November (Abraham Lincoln's Classroom, after Marszalek; Hitchcock's diary). Here a lawyer in a foreign republic sliding toward war with his country does not wait to be sent for. He leaves as a private American with no Pacifican office, and the Republic's law gives it no ground to hold him. He reaches Washington in June. Frémont already has the Western Department (3 July 1861, Hist.), a political appointment Lincoln will not undo, and Halleck, a civilian, spends the summer in the War Department as the Union's expert on the Pacific, planning the Sound expedition (CR-059 W13; PROPOSED). His commission as major general of the regular army keeps its historical date, 19 August 1861, and Missouri comes to him in November when Frémont falls, as historically. He is general in chief by order of 11 July 1862, taking office on 23 July (H&J pp. 54–55, 209; California State Military Museum). In Washington he is the best-informed man about Pacifica's army; that is CR-059 I2's refusal of coin (March 1863) and CR-061's Fort Union |
| H4 | **Sherman stays.** He took the Vallejo appointment when his banking ended about 1857 and Pacifican citizenship before the war (canon §8; CT-SHERMAN: naturalization about 1857–1858). He has a household in California and holds an oath given in Monterey. In 1861 he is the Republic's Inspector-General (canon §9). The two men part in San Francisco in May 1861 (scene, PROPOSED) |

### S · Shiloh, 5–6 April 1862 (constraint C1)

| ID | Decision |
|---|---|
| S1 | **The decision of 2 April stands** (Hist.). The gain comes in execution: Hardee's corps, which historically did not start until 3 p.m. on 3 April (NPS, *Shiloh*, sec. 3), clears Corinth in the morning (H&J pp. 163–164: "barely left Corinth by nightfall on the 3rd"), and the march of 4 April goes as ordered. The army is deployed on the evening of the 4th, before the heavy rain of that night, which historically left it about twelve hours behind on the road (NPS sec. 3); here it falls on men already in line. The jam of Polk's wagons at Michie's that held Bragg until about 4 p.m. on the 5th (NPS sec. 3) does not happen, because the corps are already up. The attack goes in at dawn on the 5th. The review calls this "one good day from green troops on two roads over 25 miles … a stretch but not a violation." No Pacifican cause is claimed |
| S2 | **Beauregard's wish to withdraw** is overruled on the evening of the 4th, as it was on the 5th historically (H&J p. 164: Johnston, "I would fight them if they were a million") |
| S3 | **The diverted Iowa regiments:** the 11th, 13th, 15th and 16th Iowa, all at Shiloh historically (Boatner), are on their way to Panama and the Sound (canon §10; CR-059 W13). About 3,000 men (MODEL; the 15th had about 760 and the 16th about 785: SUVCW; the 11th's and 13th's strengths not found). The 11th and 13th were in Hare's brigade of McClernand's division (OR I/10/1 casualty return), which is thinner without them. The 15th and 16th were raw (colonels commissioned 22 February and 15 March 1862, Boatner), assigned to Prentiss and sent to McClernand's right on the morning of the battle (SUVCW) |
| S4 | **The green division at Shiloh Church** (Sherman's historical 5th, with the brigades of Colonels McDowell, Stuart, Hildebrand and Buckland: historyofwar.org) goes upriver from Paducah in March under its senior colonel. Grant gives it to **Brigadier General John McArthur** when the brigadiers' confirmations arrive late in March (McArthur a brigadier from 21 March 1862, appointed the next day, with Lauman and W. H. L. Wallace of the same date; he led the 1st Brigade of C. F. Smith's division at Fort Donelson and the 2nd Brigade of W. H. L. Wallace's at Shiloh, where he was wounded: ACW High Command; Wikipedia). McArthur's own brigade passes to its senior colonel (OPEN) |
| S5 | **The first day, 5 April.** McArthur's division gives way at Shiloh Church earlier than Sherman's did, and McClernand's thinned division with it. Prentiss and W. H. L. Wallace hold the centre as historically; Prentiss surrenders about 5:30 p.m. with about 2,300 men (Hist.: Epperson). Johnston dies about 2:30 p.m. (Boatner; Epperson gives 2:45). Grant's last line at the landing holds at dusk with its massed guns and the gunboats (canon) |
| S6 | **Buell: nothing against Nelson, a day against Crittenden and McCook.** Buell's leading division (Nelson's) is "camping at Savannah" on 5 April (H&J p. 164), having arrived that day; historically it marched on the 6th at 1:30 p.m., Ammen's brigade was across by 7:30 p.m. and the whole division by 9 p.m., Crittenden's by 1 a.m. on the 7th, McCook's in the night (Epperson; NPS, *Shiloh*, sec. 12). Here Nelson's lead brigade crosses in the evening of the first day, the 5th, and the rest of the division in the night. Lew Wallace arrives between 6:30 and 7:15 p.m. (Boatner; Epperson). The battle's earlier start therefore costs Grant nothing against Nelson and a day against Crittenden and McCook, who fought on the historical second day (Boatner: "all of Buell's divisions but Thomas's fought on the 7th") |
| S7 | **The second day, 6 April.** At dawn Beauregard finds Lew Wallace and Nelson in Grant's line. With Johnston dead and his corps intermingled, he does not renew the assault. He holds the captured camps through the day and carries off guns, colours and stores. He withdraws to Corinth that night as Crittenden's division lands. Grant has about 11,500 fresh men, Lew Wallace's about 5,800 and Nelson's division, against about 21,000–25,000 historically (MODEL; NPS gives about 15,000 of Buell's men in line at dawn on 7 April historically). He probes but cannot retake the camps before Beauregard is gone: no second-day recovery (canon) |
| S8 | **Losses (MODEL):** Union about 11,500, about 3,000 of them prisoners; Confederate about 8,000; against 13,047 and 10,699 historically (H&J). The Confederacy calls it a victory; the Union calls it a held landing |

He gives way, knowing what he gives up: he has told Halleck that Thomas is too slow for the campaign he means to fight (PROPOSED; the judgement is Grant's, H&J p. 661, there addressed to Sherman). Hooker's resentment is a second cost (as it was historically when Howard was preferred to him in July 1864). Thomas keeps the Army of the Cumberland in his own hands (open decision 6). Alternatives in Part III |
| C8 | **McPherson lives through 1864.** He historically died at the battle of 22 July 1864 (Hist.), which here is not fought. Nothing else is assumed |

## Part I — Why Halleck goes east and Sherman does not (H1–H4)

Both men were in California in 1861 (H&J pp. 54–55), and a reader of the character bible will ask why one is the Union's general in chief and the other the Republic's Inspector-General. The difference is that Sherman made a choice in 1857 and Halleck never did.

- **Halleck's business is an alien's business.** A lawyer in Mexican land titles serves American buyers before a foreign republic's courts. A railway presidency is an investment. Both can be run from New York, and both are hostages in a republic heading toward war with his country. He has no Pacifican office to give up.
- **His ambition points east.** He wrote the American army's textbook (*Elements of Military Art and Science*, 1846, from his Lowell lectures). Scott thinks him the best mind in the army and wants him in Washington (historically Lincoln appointed him on Scott's recommendation in August 1861: Abraham Lincoln's Classroom). A man with that standing does not wait in San Francisco for a foreign government's war.
- **He took no commission.** Historically he was a California militia major general (H&J). Residents of the Republic can hold militia rank, as Sherman did before he naturalized (canon §8). Halleck refuses it, as an American who expects to go home. The line between him and Sherman is citizenship; the refusal is how Halleck keeps on his side of it.
- **Sherman did the opposite** (canon §8; CT-SHERMAN). When his banking ended about 1857 he took the Vallejo appointment, and he became a citizen in 1857–58, before the war. His household is Californian. Canon makes the decision costly: Ellen, the children and his American family "must materially affect" it. By 1861 he is the Republic's Inspector-General with an oath given in Monterey.

**Why Pacifica lets Halleck go.** In May 1861 he is a private American and the Republic is not yet at war. The Protector's mandate of late May promises civilian protections and no forced allegiance (canon §9), and holding him would break it before it began. The cost is real, and the story can show it. The Union's best-informed officer on Pacifica's army is in Washington. Every rational Union choice about the West that follows is his: hold Fort Union rather than reinforce Santa Fe (CR-061), refuse the coast its coin (CR-059 I2), send the Sound four regiments and not a division (CR-059 W13). Halleck is the rational antagonist in this war, as Lincoln is in the argument of ideas.

**The parting (scene, PROPOSED).** Sherman sees Halleck onto the Panama steamer in May 1861. Neither asks the other to change sides.

## Part II — Shiloh: the honest mechanism (S1–S8)

**What canon says and what the sources allow.** Canon has the attack on 5 April "gaining a day through earlier movement rather than an unexplained long delay to Buell". The sources show where the historical day was lost. The plan was to attack on the 4th. The army barely left Corinth by nightfall on the 3rd, the 4th went slowly, and the 5th was spent deploying in fog until 4 p.m. (H&J pp. 163–164). Boatner says the march "took a day longer than estimated to cover the intervening 25 miles". So a dawn attack on the 5th recovers one of the two days that slipped. It does not need the march to run to the original plan.

**What has to go differently on 3–4 April.** Two things that went wrong go right: Hardee's corps clears Corinth in the morning of the 3rd instead of at 3 p.m., and the march of the 4th goes as ordered, so the army is in line before that night's heavy rain, which historically caught it on the road and left it about twelve hours behind (NPS, *Shiloh*, sec. 3). The rain still falls; it falls on men already deployed, and the wagon jam at Michie's on the 5th, which held Bragg until about 4 p.m., never forms. Both are staff and road contingencies. They are not Pacifica's doing, and the draft says so rather than inventing a cause. Beauregard's objection, that surprise is lost, is made on the evening of the 4th and overruled as it was on the 5th (S2).

**What it buys against Buell.** Buell's army was strung out on the road from Columbia, delayed ten days by the flooded Duck River (Boatner). Only its leading division was at Savannah on 5 April (H&J p. 164; Nelson arrived that day, the others on the 6th and in the night of the 6th–7th: Epperson; NPS). Historically Nelson's lead brigade crossed in time to fight late on the first day and the rest of the division crossed that night (Boatner). An attack a day earlier changes nothing there, because Nelson reached Savannah on the 5th either way. The day it gains is against Crittenden and McCook, who fought on the historical second day. That is canon's outcome: Grant holds, and he cannot mount the second-day recovery.

**The diverted regiments and the green division.**
- Four Iowa regiments, about 3,000 men (MODEL), are on the Panama road. Day 1 at Pittsburg Landing becomes roughly 33,900 Union against 40,335 Confederate. Historically it was about 36,900 (H&J's 42,682 less Lew Wallace's division, about 5,800; MODEL from Hist.).
- **McArthur at Shiloh Church.** Halleck and Grant had no spare general in March 1862. The brigadiers confirmed late in March went to the divisions Grant already had, and W. H. L. Wallace took C. F. Smith's when Smith was injured (Hist.; McArthur led a brigade in it). The 5th Division goes upriver under its senior colonel, and Grant gives it to McArthur, a Donelson brigade commander he trusts, when the confirmations arrive.
- **Why not the senior colonel (McDowell of the 6th Iowa; the colonels' seniority not found)?** Grant would not leave the army's exposed right under a colonel if a brigadier were at hand.
- **Why McArthur rather than Lauman?** McArthur's later record at division level (Vicksburg, the 6th Division of the XVII Corps; Nashville, the 1st Division of the XVI Corps; he was superseded in division command at Corinth: Wikipedia, to check) makes him credible. His inexperience at Shiloh makes the early break credible without making him a fool.
- **What is lost is Sherman himself:** the man whose division took the first assault and fell back behind Shiloh Church without breaking (Hist.; the hour not confirmed).

**Why the outcome stays modest.**
- The Hornet's Nest still holds for most of the day.
- Johnston still dies in mid-afternoon (Boatner, about 2:30 p.m.).
- The last line at the landing, with its massed guns and the gunboats, is where it was.
- Beauregard halts at dusk as historically (H&J p. 169), and his army is as exhausted and intermingled.

What changes is the morning after. Grant has Lew Wallace and Nelson, about 11,500 fresh men (MODEL), not about 25,000. Beauregard has the camps. Neither can attack with hope. Beauregard sees Buell's next division coming up the river and goes back to Corinth with his trophies.

**Consequences.** Canon's "Beauregard accepts a costly operational respite" holds. The Confederacy has a claimed victory and a dead commander. Halleck arrives, takes the field and advances on Corinth slowly, as historically. Grant is shelved, and the cost of Sherman's absence comes due a second time (C2).

## Part III — The western command (C1–C8)

**June 1862.** Historically Grant, made second in command and ignored, meant to leave, and Sherman talked him out of it (Grant, *Memoirs* ch. 27; Sherman, *Memoirs* ch. 11). It is the largest single function Sherman performed for the Union before Vicksburg, and it has to be reassigned or canon's Vicksburg falls. McPherson is the recommended replacement: Grant's engineer at Shiloh, liked and trusted, and the man this CR raises to Sherman's later place. Rawlins, Grant's adjutant, is the alternative (OPEN). In either case Halleck's call to Washington on 11 July gives Grant his command back.

**The XV Corps and the river.**
- Steele, a classmate and a West Pointer, is the natural replacement in December 1862 (class of 1843 with Grant; a division commander at Chickasaw Bayou historically).
- Chickasaw Bayou keeps its historical outcome under him.
- Canon's Vicksburg (Grant and Porter cross below, Pemberton escapes in part) is untouched.
- After Vicksburg the corps passes to Blair, then to Logan as historically, and Steele goes to Arkansas.

**Chattanooga.** McPherson takes the Army of the Tennessee in October 1863 (Hist. March 1864) and brings the XV Corps east. That leaves the Vicksburg district under corps commanders who hold rather than raid, which is one cause of R1. Missionary Ridge stays historical in outcome.

**March 1864: Thomas, and the alternatives.**

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Thomas (recommended)** | Senior among the major generals of volunteers; the largest army; Chickamauga and Missionary Ridge; Halleck's regard; the Army of the Cumberland would not take a junior over him | Grant's own judgement (H&J p. 661); Thomas does not share the raiding outlook (p. 696) |
| (b) McPherson over Thomas | Grant's protégé and an army commander already; senior to Thomas in the regular army | Lincoln, Stanton and Halleck want Thomas; the Army of the Cumberland, three-fifths of the force, would not serve under a thirty-five-year-old engineer over its own commander; and H&J name only Sherman, Smith and Sheridan as sharing the outlook, so McPherson would not raid either |
| (c) Baldy Smith sent west instead of kept east | He shares the outlook (H&J); Grant admired his Chattanooga work (Hist.; not checked) | Junior to both; quarrelsome; Grant wants him on the James |

Option (a) also turns on knowledge. Grant knows exactly what he gives up: he wrote the sentence about Thomas. The reader sees him choose correctly by every rule of the service and lose the campaign by it. The rational antagonist loses because his institutions work as designed.

## Part IV — The strategy of raids and the plan of 1864 (R1–R5)

H&J's judgement is the spine. The strategy of exhausting the Confederacy by raids "distinctly belonged to Grant" (p. 696). Sherman executed it and gave it its political dimension. The Meridian raid of February 1864 was its rehearsal (p. 506). Halleck and Lincoln never grasped it (p. 513).

Without Sherman the strategy stays an idea in Grant's head. It has to be carried out by subordinates who think in railroads. Meigs's elation that Sherman's raiders could move more than 25 miles from rail or river "without perishing" (p. 661) never comes.

**The plan without the Gulf.** Grant's simultaneous advances become four. Banks's move on Mobile goes because there is no Department of the Gulf army (CR-063). Halleck's "trans-Mississippi preoccupation" (H&J p. 690) has no Red River to spend itself on, so A. J. Smith's divisions stay where history borrowed them from. This is the one place where Sherman's absence comes with a Union gain, and it is small: historically Forrest never did effective damage to the line anyway (H&J p. 612), and Smith's Tupelo expedition kept him off it in July. Here Smith is in place from April. Thomas's slowness is therefore a matter of doctrine and supply, not of raids.

## Part V — The Atlanta campaign stalls (A1–A10)

**The constraint.** H&J hold that Johnston "had not been equal to the task of stopping the more powerful Union army" and could not have held Atlanta by defence (pp. 606, 630, 688). Canon's sentence, "Johnston … has held Atlanta past the election", must therefore not be a siege won. It has to be a campaign that never reaches the siege.

**What the record changed (9 November 2026).** The first draft kept Polk's Army of Mississippi in Alabama from the start, so that it never joined Johnston. The record shows why that cannot stand: Polk's divisions are what held Resaca in May. Cantey's division came from Mobile on 7 May and had about 4,000 in the works on the 9th (Castel, *Decision in the West* p. 138), which stopped McPherson; Loring's arrived on the 10th to 12th with Polk himself. Without them, Thomas's own Snake Creek Gap plan, with a bigger army than McPherson's, takes the railroad behind Johnston and ends canon's 1864. So Polk comes east in May as historically. The fresh-context review of the same day showed that his going back west in June must be Richmond's order, not Johnston's wish, and that the flank must strike once before Thomas fears it; both are written in below.

**The sequence (A1–A10; dates Hist. where marked, otherwise MODEL).**

0. *The commands.* Thomas commands the Military Division from March and keeps the Army of the Cumberland in his own hands (C7; open decision 6): its three corps under Howard (IV), Palmer (XIV) and Hooker (XX) (Hist. corps commanders; to check). McPherson has the Army of the Tennessee, short two divisions of the XVII Corps on veterans' furlough as historically, Schofield the Army of the Ohio.
1. *7 May: the advance.* As historically, the Union columns close on Rocky Face Ridge and the Mill Creek Gap in front of Dalton (Hist.).
2. *8–9 May: Snake Creek Gap, Thomas's plan.* Thomas does as group commander what he urged on Sherman. McPherson's army, Schofield's and Howard's IV Corps press Rocky Face to hold Johnston at Dalton. Palmer's and Hooker's corps, about 40,000, march by Villanow to the gap. Thomas sends their trains with them, because he does not send an army where he cannot feed it, and two corps with their wagons take two days on one track through a four-mile gorge (MODEL; McPherson's 25,000 historically took from 8 May to the next morning: Cox; Georgia marker 064-8). Hooker's leading division comes out on the morning of the 9th, as McPherson's did, and finds Cantey's men entrenched before Resaca. Its commander does what McPherson did historically: he will not attack works he cannot see round with one division while the rest of the corps is in the gorge. By then Johnston knows (Hist.: by about 10 a.m. on the 9th), and that night he rails three of Hood's divisions to Resaca, as historically.
3. *10 May: the gap's mouth (the hinge).* Thomas's plan was never to take Resaca. It was to hold the railroad, which can be cut anywhere on the sixteen miles north of the town. On the morning of the 10th Hood's divisions come out of Resaca and probe toward the gap's mouth. Hooker's corps, half out of the gorge, entrenches across the mouth to meet an attack, as McPherson's army historically entrenched there on 10–12 May expecting Hood (Hist.: NPS sec. 2). Thomas rides out to the mouth that afternoon. He has one corps out, the second still in the gorge, his trains behind it, Hood in front and Johnston's main army somewhere north. He orders the line held and the second corps and the trains brought up before anything moves on the railroad. He is right about what he can see and wrong about what he cannot: Hood's probe is a screen. That night Johnston leaves Dalton behind Wheeler's cavalry and marches south on the roads east of the railroad, across the gap's front, two nights earlier than historically, and Loring's division reaches Resaca from Rome (Hist., 10–11 May).
4. *11–14 May: Resaca.* On the 11th Thomas comes out with both corps and the trains and finds the railroad north of Resaca empty except for Johnston's rear guard. He attacks on the 12th and 13th, with McPherson and Schofield coming down the railroad behind, against about 70,000 Confederates in works along Camp Creek and the Oostanaula (MODEL). He gains ground and not the river. On the night of the 14th Johnston crosses the Oostanaula and burns the bridges (Hist. pattern: the night of 15 May). Loss for loss it is the historical battle a day early. **The risk stated plainly:** a Thomas who pushed his leading corps to the railroad on the afternoon of the 10th, leaving the gap's mouth to the second corps, would have had the railroad that night. At Mill Springs and Nashville he struck hard when he was ready. Here, on the 10th, he is not ready, and the man who is never caught unready is the one who lets Johnston go.
5. *Late May: the Etowah.* Johnston falls back across the Etowah by about 20 May (Hist.). Thomas rebuilds the railroad bridge and comes up to the Allatoona position on the line. He does not swing to Dallas (A3).
6. *Early June: Richmond's order and Johnston's plan (A5, A8).* Davis, alarmed for Selma by A. J. Smith at Memphis and Union cavalry in north Alabama and advised by Bragg, orders a force back to Polk's department. Johnston protests that he is outnumbered two to one, and is overruled. He then makes the order his own: in a letter to Davis by courier he writes that he will hold the river lines north of Atlanta and that Polk, at the end of the Selma railroad, will threaten the Union line from Alabama. Polk goes west with Cantey's division and builds his force at Blue Mountain from the escapees in Mississippi and S. D. Lee's cavalry, about 10,000–12,000 (MODEL). Loring and French stay with Johnston. Polk is not on Pine Mountain on 14 June.
7. *June–July: Allatoona.* Johnston, at about 63,000, holds the Allatoona heights; Blair's divisions come up to McPherson. Thomas goes at the position by short flanks back to the railroad, repairs as he goes and builds blockhouses and depots. There are no Confederate sorties: Hood historically lost about 11,000 in July (ABT; H&J pp. 608–623 give 15,000) and here loses none. In mid-July Rousseau's cavalry, sent by Thomas from Decatur, Alabama, burns the depot at Talladega on Polk's line and tears up the Montgomery and West Point (Hist.: 10–22 July; Encyclopedia of Alabama). Polk's line runs again in days.
8. *16 July: Davis's question.* Historically the question that preceded Johnston's removal (NPS sec. 6). Here Bragg has seen the army on the Allatoona heights, sixty miles from Atlanta, the Union army has spent two months on forty miles of railroad, and Johnston's plan, built round Davis's own order, is on Davis's desk. Davis does not ask; Hood remains a corps commander.
9. *August: Polk strikes, and Kennesaw.* Polk marches from Blue Mountain on Kingston, burns the depot, breaks the line and the Etowah bridge for nine days and is back on his railroad before Thomas's reserves arrive (MODEL; the historical pattern is French's attempt on Allatoona of 5 October 1864). The armies before Kennesaw go on half rations for a week. From then on Thomas garrisons Rome, Kingston and the Etowah bridge and keeps Schofield on his right, toward Alabama: his caution about the flank is learned. Johnston falls back to the Kennesaw line and then to the river.
10. *September: the Chattahoochee.* Thomas comes down to the river, where Johnston holds the River Line on the north bank (historically built from 19 June: Wikipedia, to check), and turns him out of it by crossing upstream; Johnston goes back to the works on the south bank north of Atlanta.
11. *October: what Thomas will do and will not.* McPherson, swinging east, away from Polk, cuts the Georgia Railroad at Decatur, as he historically did in July (Hist.), and Atlanta is down to two lines. To reach those two Thomas would have to swing his infantry south and west of the city, as six Union corps historically did from 25 August (ABT; Hess ch. 10), uncovering the line Polk burned in August. He sends his cavalry instead, as Sherman sent Kilpatrick in August 1864 (Hist.; to check), and the Macon and Western is repaired in days. Thomas's cavalry raids are in character: in 1865 he sent Wilson's 13,500 against Selma (Encyclopedia of Alabama). His infantry stays on its railroad.
12. *October: Grant.* Grant, tied to Petersburg, presses Thomas by telegraph. He drafts an order giving the western command to McPherson and suspends it, as he did before Nashville on 9 December 1864 (Grant, *Memoirs* ch. 60; ABT). Lincoln, a month from the election, will not relieve the general whose army is in contact. Thomas answers that he will move when he is ready.
13. *8 November.* Atlanta is untaken and its two remaining railways are running; the armies are in contact on the Chattahoochee, and Polk stands at Blue Mountain.

**Why the stall now holds.** It rests on four things a reader can count: a commander who will not leave his railroad and will not move until he is ready (steps 3, 5 and 11); Richmond's order, which Johnston turns into a flank (step 6); a flank that has struck once, so that Thomas's caution is learned, not innate (step 9); and the absence of Hood (step 7), which keeps the Army of Tennessee whole into the autumn. Each has its history: Thomas at the gap's mouth is McPherson there, and Thomas before Nashville; Davis's fear for Selma; French at Allatoona; Johnston's own record.

**The Vicksburg escapees.** About 12,500 escape (MODEL; CR-063 V3 as revised on 9 November 2026: they leave on the night of 17–18 May, and about 16,500 more hold the city with Pemberton until the turn of June and July). They take the places history gave to paroled men: in the Army of Tennessee, in Polk's Army of Mississippi, and in Mississippi's garrisons, from which Polk draws for the flank in June. The stall does not depend on where they are.

**Mobile's part, kept modest.** Atlanta's own supply hangs on the Macon and Western, the Atlanta and West Point and, until McPherson cuts it in October (Part V step 11), the Georgia Railroad to Augusta (Hess ch. 10). The second of these runs to Montgomery and so, by the Alabama River, to Mobile. Mobile and Selma feed the Alabama flank more than they feed Atlanta. Canon's "help sustain" is right as long as it stays "help" (CT4).

**What the stalemate is for the story.** In Washington the West offers Lincoln nothing to show before November. In Richmond, Joseph E. Johnston becomes the general who was never driven out of Georgia. That reputation makes canon's 1867 election and his presidency from February 1868 (§13, APPROVED) believable. In Monterey, Sherman follows the campaign he would have commanded in the Eastern papers and will not talk about it. Elias reads it as the largest return the Republic ever had on a naturalization, and he does not say so to Sherman (PROPOSED).

## Part VI — Hampton Roads and the Peninsula (E1–E5)

The review (C7) shows that canon's caution is right and can be made precise. Without Monitor, what changes at Hampton Roads is the battle and the anchorage, not the campaign.
- Virginia wins a larger victory on 8–9 March (canon), but at 22 feet and 4 knots she cannot reach Fort Monroe or go to sea (H&J; Boatner).
- The James was closed to McClellan's transports with or without Monitor (Boatner).
- McClellan advances up the Peninsula by land; the Yorktown and Gloucester batteries close the York to the Navy, and he is held at Yorktown.
- Norfolk falls about 9 May, and Virginia, unable to go up the James or to sea, is destroyed (Boatner; NHHC 11 May).
- The Union's answer in March is improvised (E3).
- Drewry's Bluff is attacked on 15 May by Galena without Monitor and fails as it did historically. (The comparison was also in the §11 storyline text; the set review's repair pass of 8 November 2026 removed it there, and it belongs here and in `historical_background.md`.) Canon's rule holds: a historical failure is not turned into a new Confederate victory.

The rest of the Eastern war stays historical, and canon should say so in one sentence (open decision 9).
