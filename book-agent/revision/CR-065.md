## What this CR does

**The outcome stays.** Lincoln loses in November 1864 and McClellan, a Union man, takes office on 4 March 1865. What changes is why, and what Lincoln does with the four months between.

**Five moves.**
1. **The weight moves from Mobile to Atlanta.** H&J make Atlanta the event that changed the political climate and give Mobile Bay no political weight. Canon leans on both. Here the decisive absence is Atlanta (CR-064), and Mobile is one visible failure among several.
2. **The Valley does not decide it.** Sheridan still gets the Valley, but a corps short: the XIX Corps came from the Department of the Gulf, and here there is no Gulf field army (CR-063). He wins at Winchester, cannot rout Early at Fisher's Hill, and holds at Cedar Creek. Lincoln has one victory in the six weeks before the vote, not three.
3. **Lincoln stays on the ballot and loses on his own ticket.** The editors' letters of 2 September and the radicals' convention run because Atlanta does not fall on the 2nd. They fizzle when the governors who face October elections refuse to split the party. Frémont withdraws as historically. Lincoln refuses, a second time, to drop emancipation as a condition of peace.
4. **The mechanics.** Indiana and Pennsylvania go Democratic on 11 October; Ohio holds. The soldiers vote for Lincoln, but Indiana's and Illinois's cannot vote in the field, and Thomas, in front of Johnston, cannot furlough them. McClellan wins by a civilian swing of about six points in the Lower North (MODEL), with 120 electoral votes to 103 (MODEL) in a college that has no Pacific electors.
5. **The winter is the memorandum's pledge kept.** Lincoln tries to save the Union before 4 March: cooperation offered to McClellan, the Thirteenth Amendment, a last push at Petersburg and Mobile, and a peace of his own at Hampton Roads. Each fails. The amendment falls about seven votes short (MODEL) because the Democrats who historically switched do not switch after their party has won. Lincoln does not treat with Monterey. He leaves McClellan a choice made plain.

**The causal chain to Pacifica, on the page.**
- New Orleans holds in 1862 (Logan) → no Gulf field army → no XIX Corps → Sheridan a corps short.
- Sherman is Pacifican (canon §8) → a methodical Thomas → no Atlanta, and no furloughs for Indiana.
- The Pacific coast is lost → no Californian gold for the Union's war (canon §9) → no Pacific electors: a majority of the college is 112, not the historical 117 of 233 electors voting (118 of 234 apportioned).
- Pacifica's restraint in the autumn → no outrage for Lincoln to campaign on.

## Part I — What decided the historical election (W1–W5)

**The sources' order of causes.** H&J pp. 624–625 tell it as a sequence. Before Atlanta, "many observers foresaw a Democratic victory". The Democrats delayed their convention to watch the peace sentiment, then wrote a peace platform at the end of August and nominated McClellan, who was committed to the war. The radicals planned a late-September convention to replace Lincoln, and on 2 September Greeley and two other editors wrote to the governors to shelve him. "At that critical moment, Atlanta fell and this visible military success changed the political climate. Subsequently, with this new climate, reinforced by Sheridan's victories over Early, Fremont withdrew from the race and the peace plank ... became a greater liability to McClellan." The result was 55 per cent and 212 to 21 (p. 701). Boatner's LINCOLN entry: "the military successes of Grant and Sherman swung sentiment to him."

**Three corrections follow.**
- **Atlanta is first and Sheridan second.** The Valley *reinforced* a climate Atlanta made. Remove Atlanta, and the Valley has to make the climate by itself.
- **Mobile is nowhere.** Farragut's August 1864 battle happened, and H&J still give it no part in the election; they judge the occupation of the bay to have "mattered little" (pp. 620–624). Canon's "after Atlanta and Mobile fail" credits Mobile with a weight it never had. An untaken Mobile belongs on the list of things that wear the North down: ports closed, cotton not coming, the blockade's cost. It does not belong among the causes (CT1).
- **The September movement is live.** It was killed by Atlanta on the day it began. Here nothing kills it, so this CR must decide its end (Part III).

**The swing is the whole timeline's (W5).** A reader may object that six points is a lot for one autumn. It is not one autumn's. The historical returns are those of a North that had New Orleans from 1862, *Monitor*'s draw at Hampton Roads, the Pacific coast and its gold, Vicksburg's army in the bag and Atlanta. Here the North has none of these. The six points are the price of all of them, paid at once.

## Part II — Sheridan's Valley campaign (V1–V5)

**Why Grant still sends Sheridan (V1).** Early's raid reaches Fort Stevens on 11–12 July regardless; Washington wants him dealt with. Grant's own strategy argues for the Valley: H&J say the strategy of raids "distinctly belonged to Grant", that only Sherman, Baldy Smith and Sheridan shared it (p. 696), and that Thomas did not (p. 661). With Sherman gone and Thomas tied to his railroad (CR-064), the Valley is the only raid left to Grant. Option (b), that he keeps the VI Corps at Petersburg and leaves Early alone, contradicts the sources' Grant and leaves Washington to a raider in an election year. It is rejected.

**Why the army is weaker anyway (V2).** The XIX Corps was the Gulf's. Boatner has it leaving New Orleans for Sabine Pass in September 1863 and its 1st Division arriving at Fort Monroe "from New Orleans" in July 1864, in time to be sent to Washington. In this timeline the Union never takes New Orleans, there is no Gulf field army (CR-063), and there is no XIX Corps to come north. The VI Corps, brought by sea from City Point (H&J pp. 600–602), saves the capital on its own. In the Valley Sheridan has the VI Corps, Crook's troops and the cavalry: by my estimate a quarter to a third fewer men than his historical army, against Early's 14,000 (MODEL; the XIX Corps' strength is to be verified). **This is the cleanest link in the chapter between Pacifica and the vote**: Logan's work at New Orleans in April 1862 takes a corps out of the Valley in September 1864. The loss is a corps organization fit for the Valley in August, not men. Under CR-063's option S-a, to which this CR commits, about 12,000 of Butler's men went to Fort Monroe in July 1862 and have been in the eastern armies since (in the Overland losses, not an addition), about 3,000 hold Pensacola, and Butler has commanded on the James since late 1863. When Early reaches Washington there is no corps with its own staff, trains and artillery on the sea lanes to send to the Valley: the men who might have formed one are already committed in front of Petersburg or in the Gulf garrison (Hist.; to verify, regiment by regiment).

**The three options.**

| | Valley | What Lincoln has by 8 November | Swing needed elsewhere (MODEL) | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (a) | As historically: Winchester, Fisher's Hill, Cedar Creek, all victories | Three victories in six weeks, Cedar Creek's ride 19 days before the vote | About seven to eight points | Possible, but it asks the reader to believe that three victories and a legend counted for nothing. It contradicts canon's "fail to provide Lincoln reassuring victories" |
| (b) | Grant does not detach Sheridan; Early stays in the lower Valley | Nothing; Early on the Potomac | About five | Rejected (V1): against the sources' Grant |
| **(c)** | **Winchester won; Fisher's Hill no rout; Cedar Creek held, Early not destroyed** | **One victory, then a raid repelled** | **About six** | **Recommended:** materially caused (V2), consistent with Grant's strategy, and it leaves Lincoln a real victory to campaign on that is not enough |

**How (c) happens.** Lincoln cannot afford a defeat before November, so Sheridan's caution of August (historically he waited to attack; Hist.; to verify) lasts until Early is weakened. Winchester on 19 September is won as historically. At Fisher's Hill on the 22nd a two-corps army cannot both pin Early's line and turn it. Crook's flank march is seen, and Early goes up the Valley in the night with his trains. At Cedar Creek on 19 October Early's dawn surprise succeeds as it did historically. Sheridan rides in and restores the line, and Early withdraws again, beaten and not destroyed. The newspapers' verdict is "Early still in the Valley": a raid repelled, not a war being won. The tactical sequence is my inference from the missing corps, and a Valley source must check it (research task 3).

**What it is worth (V4, MODEL).** H&J say only that Sheridan "reinforced" the climate after Atlanta. I put the historical Valley at perhaps two points in the Lower North and (c) at one. These are guesses, labelled as such, and the result does not hang on them.

## Part III — The September movement and the agon (S1–S6)

**The three outcomes.**

| Option | What happens | For canon | For the agon |
|---|---|---|---|
| A convention nominates another man (Butler or Chase; Hist. names, to verify) | Two Republican tickets | A split elects McClellan, and Republicans can say the split, not the war, lost it | Lincoln is beaten by his own party's managers, not by the voters |
| Lincoln withdraws | Another man on the ballot | Contradicts canon's "Lincoln loses the election of 1864" | The contest ends in a back room |
| **The movement fizzles; Lincoln runs weakened (recommended)** | **The governors refuse; no convention meets; Lincoln on the ballot** | **Keeps canon's words: Lincoln loses** | **The voters refuse Lincoln's war head to head** |

**Why it fizzles, materially (S3).** The men who must carry the states in October are the governors, and two of them face voters on 11 October. Morton of Indiana is on the ballot himself (Hist.; to verify). A convention on 28 September that divides the party would lose him Indiana two weeks later, and he says so. Pennsylvania's congressional delegation is in the same position. The radicals' own logic finishes it: a new candidate nominated six weeks before the vote, with no organization, against a McClellan whose letter has just put Union first, would elect McClellan. The call is withdrawn. The damage stays: the party's best-known editors have said in writing that Lincoln cannot win.

**Frémont (S4).** He withdraws on 22 September, as historically, to keep McClellan out (Hist.; the Blair bargain to verify). Keeping him in the race would turn the result into a split, which is the weaker story for the reason in the table. For the reader of Part I he is Elias's old antagonist of Gavilan Peak and Cahuenga (canon §§3–4). His withdrawal takes the one candidate who might have spoken of the lost West out of the campaign, and the West stays off the ballot. That suits Elias and keeps the Union's grievance pointed at Richmond.

**Why a Lincoln on the ballot serves the agon.** The author wants the war to be an intellectual contest between Elias and Lincoln. The contest is about consent. Elias's Pacifica was made by votes: Hawaii's legislature in 1856, Oregon's in 1866 (CR-059), and its founder will not annex by decree. Lincoln holds that the Union is perpetual, that no vote of a state can end it, and that a people's government must keep its elections even in a civil war. An election that Lincoln holds, contests at full strength and loses tests both arguments at once.
- **Lincoln's principle is vindicated at the cost of his war.** He holds the election he could not postpone and accepts the result (L1–L2).
- **Elias's principle wins in fact:** the Union's consent to the war is withdrawn by the Union's own voters, without a Pacifican hand (P1).
- **Lincoln's other principle survives in law:** McClellan was elected on Union first, and the Senate never ratifies the South's separation (canon §12). Lincoln loses the election, the war and the West, and his argument about the Union outlives him in the Senate's refusal.
- **A shelved Lincoln would spoil all three.** His party, not the people, would have refused him. His successor's defeat could be blamed on the successor. He would never get the winter in which he keeps the memorandum's pledge.

**The Raymond temptation (S6).** It makes Lincoln a rational antagonist, not a saint. Historically he drafted the offer of peace on Union alone and did not send it (Hist.; to verify); his reason was military as well as moral, since the Black regiments could not be abandoned. Here the temptation returns when Atlanta does not fall, and he refuses again, knowing what it may cost. The page shows him choose.

## Part IV — The mechanics of the upset (E1–E8)

**The October signal (E4).** CR-015 A3 has the Democrats carry Pennsylvania, Ohio and Indiana on 11 October. Ohio is wrong for this map. Lincoln won it with 56.4 per cent in November (Hist.; to verify), and a swing that took Ohio in October would take it in November, giving McClellan a landslide canon does not ask for. Here Indiana turns Morton out, Pennsylvania's home vote goes Democratic, and Ohio holds with a reduced majority. Ohio is the Shermans' state. John Sherman's Ohio stays with Lincoln while the Lower North goes (CR-015 A5).

**The soldier vote (E5).** Canon has the soldiers stay with Lincoln and the upset come from civilians. The mechanism: soldiers voted in the field only where their states allowed it (Hist.; to verify), and Indiana and Illinois did not. Historically Lincoln asked Sherman, after Atlanta, to let Indiana's soldiers go home for the state election (Hist.; to verify). Here the letter goes to Thomas, whose army is in contact with Johnston on the Chattahoochee (CR-064), and he cannot spare them. The two hardest states for McClellan are the two where the army's vote is smallest. **Interface:** this needs CR-064's western army to be engaged in September–October, not idle in camp.

**The swing (MODEL).** Lincoln's historical shares in the states that decide (Hist.; to verify): New York 50.5, Connecticut 51.4, Pennsylvania 51.8, New Hampshire 52.6, Indiana 53.6, Illinois 54.4, Maryland 55.1, Ohio 56.4.
- **The tipping state is Illinois, not New York.** To carry the college McClellan needs a swing of about 4.5 points there. CR-015 A3's "a few points" is right for New York and Pennsylvania and too little for the West.
- **The recommended case:** about six points among civilians across the Lower North (New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Connecticut, Delaware, New Hampshire), a little more where soldiers voted in the field, and about three elsewhere, with Maryland's loyalty oath (Hist.; to verify) holding its swing down. On the historical returns this gives McClellan 120 electoral votes to Lincoln's 103 and puts him narrowly ahead in the popular vote.
- **The narrower case:** five points in the Lower North and two and a half elsewhere give the same 120 to 103, but Lincoln wins the popular vote with about 50.7 per cent. A president elected by a minority would make McClellan's armistices easier for a Republican Senate to defy, so the recommended case is the wider one (open decision 6).

**The college without the Pacific (E7).** Historically 234 electors were apportioned and 233 voted, and Lincoln's 212 included California's 5, Oregon's 3 and Nevada's 3 (2 cast; one elector did not vote) (Hist.; to verify). The Pacific coast had eleven electors, ten of them cast. Here, if CR-060 CT2 is adopted, there are 223 and a majority is 112. McClellan's 120 would win either way, but the margin is that close: had New Hampshire held for Lincoln (McClellan 115, Lincoln 108), the ten Pacific electors who voted in history would have re-elected him (118 of 233 voting, a majority being 117), and their absence elects McClellan (115 of 223). Elias can do that arithmetic. So can Greeley.

**War-weariness on the page.** None of these is decisive alone. Together they are the six points.
- **The casualty lists:** more than 60,000 in six weeks (H&J p. 590).
- **The draft:** the July call and the wheel turning in September (Hist.; to verify), with no Atlanta to answer it.
- **Gold** at its wartime peak in July (CR-015 A3; to verify) and no treasure steamers from San Francisco (F1).
- **Early** at Washington's forts in July and still in the Valley in November (V3).
- **The West stalled** on the Chattahoochee (CR-064).
- **The Gulf ports untaken:** Mobile, New Orleans, Galveston (W2).
- **The Pacific coast lost,** with its gold, its ports and its voters (canon §§9–10).

**Pacifica's part (P1–P2).** Elias does nothing in the campaign. His reason is the same as the restraint that served him in Oregon before 1859 (CR-059 P5): the peace Pacifica needs is with a Union that blames Richmond and Lincoln, not Monterey. He also forbids any operation against Port Townsend or Fort Union before the vote. A Pacifican attack in October would give Lincoln what Atlanta did not, a cause. This is Elias's restraint set beside Lincoln's: one refuses to drop emancipation, the other refuses to strike.

## Part V — The lame-duck winter (L1–L8)

**The frame is Lincoln's own (L1).** The memorandum of 23 August was written for this case. It pledges Lincoln to cooperate with the president-elect "to save the Union between the election and the inauguration", because the winner will have won "on such ground that he can not possibly save it afterwards" (text to verify). Historically it was opened on 11 November in victory (Hist.; to verify). Here it is opened in defeat, and Lincoln does what it says. He offers McClellan joint action, and McClellan agrees and does nothing; historically Seward predicted that answer the day the memorandum was opened (Hay's diary; Hist.; to verify). Then Lincoln acts alone, four ways.

1. **The amendment (L3).** Historically the House passed it on 31 January 1865 by 119 to 56, two votes more than two-thirds of those voting, because enough lame-duck Democrats changed sides (Hist.; to verify). They did so after their party's defeat, with the issue settled at the polls and Lincoln's patronage behind it. Here none of that holds. Their party has won; Lincoln's offices end on 4 March and McClellan's begin; and the Democratic platform's convention of the states needs slavery left to the states, as the price of reunion. Pendleton, vice-president-elect and still a member of the House (Hist. membership; to verify), leads the opposition. **MODEL:** of the Democrats who historically voted yes, the handful who had already voted yes in June hold; the dozen or so converts vote no (about eight) or stay away (about four). The result is about 107 to 64, two-thirds of 171 being 114: seven short. This keeps CR-059 CT10: Kentucky and Delaware remain slave states of the Union in 1866; Maryland (constitution of 1864) and Missouri (January 1865) abolish by their own acts (Hist.; to verify).
2. **The last push (L4–L5).** Boatner (as quoted in the drafting instructions) has the winter preclude major operations at Petersburg. Hatcher's Run (5–7 February; Hist.; to verify) extends the lines without reaching Lee's last railroad. The February 1865 attack at Mobile (CR-063) fails and Farragut is killed (canon F4). Lincoln's reason is rational: a Gulf port taken before March would hand McClellan a stronger position and close Pacifica's channel at its hinge.
   - **If CR-063 keeps February:** canon's "paired failures at Mobile and Petersburg" stand, and they fall in the same fortnight as Hampton Roads. That is the winter's shape: military and diplomatic failure together.
   - **If CR-063 moves Mobile before the vote:** a failed assault with an admiral killed is news of another order from H&J's belated success, and W2 is reversed: Mobile then weighs in the election, and the needed civilian swing falls by about a point (MODEL). The winter's last push is then Petersburg alone, with Fort Fisher if CR-063 keeps it (Hist. 12–15 January 1865; Boatner). A Fort Fisher taken in January would be Lincoln's one success of the winter, too late to matter. §11's pairing is rewritten (CT3).
3. **A peace of his own (L6).** At Hampton Roads Lincoln offers reunion with pardons and the hope of compensation for slaves (Hist. in outline; to verify). It is more than any later Union government will offer the South. Stephens asks for an armistice first. Lincoln refuses one without reunion, and Stephens knows McClellan will grant one in a month. The conference fails over separation (Boatner: "failed"). As CR-015 A3 says, it shows Northern voters that peace means separation, and here they have already chosen a man who must face that.
4. **The Court (L8, optional).** Taney's death in October lets Lincoln name Chase Chief Justice in December, and the Republican Senate confirms him (Hist.; to verify). It is the one act binding Lincoln's successor that holds. Whether the story wants it depends on what CR-062 does with the Senate's claim to the South.

**What Lincoln does not do (L7).** He opens no talks with Monterey and offers no line in the West. A lame duck cannot cede territory, and Lincoln would not if he could. The West passes whole to McClellan and the Senate, so the 1865 peace is McClellan's and the 1867 cession the Senate's (canon §12). Lincoln leaves McClellan not a ruin but a plain choice: separation, or more war. That is the agon's last act. Lincoln makes the price of peace visible, so that if the Union lets the South go, it does so knowing what it does.

## Part VI — McClellan's first weeks and the mirror (M1–M4)

**Inauguration.** McClellan takes the oath on 4 March 1865 with Pendleton beside him. His inaugural keeps both halves of his campaign: Union as the one condition of peace, and negotiation as the way to it, an armistice and then a convention of the states. Canon's character note asks him to "explain accepting separation" (CT-MCCLELLAN). The lame-duck winter gives him the first half of that explanation. Lincoln tried every means short of more war before March and failed, and Hampton Roads showed what Richmond would take. **The cabinet** (M2) is to show a Union government, not a peace party. Winthrop, an old Whig who campaigned for him, at State; Guthrie, a Kentuckian and Pierce's Treasury secretary, at Treasury, with August Belmont as the financiers' voice outside it; his friend Franklin at War (all Hist. persons; offices PROPOSED; to verify). Reverdy Johnson stays in the Senate for CR-062's coalition. CR-062 takes over at the armistice conventions.

**The mirror (CR-015 A4).** In the same winter Elias uses the dissolution provision before the Assembly can revoke his measures. He campaigns on security, paid contracts, unfinished works and the costs of interruption (canon §12). That is Lincoln's argument of 1864, not to change horses in the middle of the river (Hist. phrase, June 1864; to verify), and Pacifica's voters return Elias a more favourable Assembly. One democracy rewards continuity because its war is being won and paid for in gold; the other punishes exhaustion because its war is being lost and paid for in paper. One man holds an election he will lose because the constitution requires it. The other calls one he will win because the constitution permits it. The narrator does not say which is the better government. **OPEN:** the dissolution's date; recommended after the news of 8 November reaches Monterey, so that the Union's coming peace becomes Elias's argument: the peace is coming, and the government that won the war must make it.
