# CR-068 — Part F: Lincoln's career in a world with Pacifica (K1–K27)

Moved from [CR-068](CR-068.md) on 11 November 2026 (revision 21) to shorten it; the rows are unchanged except K23, which gained a clause on the letter of 1867. They are part of CR-068 and are cited there by their IDs. The author's brief, the rule and the short answers stay in CR-068, Part F. Not canon.

#### F1 — 1846–1849: the sting

| ID | When | What changes, and why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| K1 | August 1846 | **His election to Congress: no change.** California declared itself on 31 May (canon §4), but the war was a Mexican war in the Illinois papers, and he beat Cartwright as historically (Hist.) | Hist. |
| K2 | January–June 1848 | **The first contact.** Vallejo and de la Guerra are in Washington as California's envoys from September 1847 to February 1848 (canon §4). They mark the *Globe*'s report of the speech of 12 January because it says what the Republic needs said. Larkin brings that number of the *Globe* to Monterey in June with the ratified convention (canon §4; there is no Panama steamer service yet), and it is the first time Elias reads Lincoln. This is the copy whose margin carries his note and the text the press reprints in 1861 (M8) | APPROVED |
| K3 | August 1848 | **The sting at home.** Historically, Logan "ran on Lincoln's record and was badly beaten"; the *Register* blamed his stand on the war, and Lincoln "would rather not be put upon explaining how Logan was defeated" (Beveridge 2:164, 166). The returned veterans voted in 1848 as they could not in 1846, most of them against the Whig (2:164 n.), and the Democrat was a major who had fought at Cerro Gordo (2:166). Here, by polling day the Senate has ratified a treaty that leaves California outside, and Benton calls it the Trist surrender (canon §4). The veterans hear that their congressman voted the war unconstitutional and that the coast is lost. Logan loses by more, and "Ranchero Spotty" becomes the Democrats' standing name for Lincoln (new). **Story assumption:** historically Harris won a close race, by about a hundred votes (drafter's recollection; the return is unverified, research 16). Here he wins by about 900 of some 12,500 votes cast, a swing of about four points made by the returned veterans and the news of the Trist surrender. In 1846 Lincoln had carried the district by about 1,500 (Hist.; to verify). His eclipse in his district is deeper and longer than in history | APPROVED; margin MODEL |
| K4 | 1848 onward | **His defence, which history did not need, and its limits.** The House voted on how the war began; the Senate ratified the treaty and the convention that left California outside. "Calhoun's Southerners vote for a settlement that keeps a free California out of the Union", and Buchanan, Polk's Secretary of State, signed the convention (canon §4). But "the Whigs vote for peace" in the same roll call, so the defence shares the blame rather than clearing him, and he never uses it without the Southern votes in front. Senator Douglas votes no on 10 March, as an expansionist, which lets him press the charge and turns Lincoln's answer onto Buchanan. Lincoln answers with the roll call, never with a new opinion about California | APPROVED |
| K5 | 1849 | **The Oregon offer.** Historically, friends proposed him for governor of Oregon Territory. Beveridge finds that the place "was never offered to him" and that the State Department holds no record of a tender. Only Herndon's undated note of Stuart's statement has an offer, by Fillmore: Lincoln would accept if his wife agreed, she refused, and Stuart told him he "would soon come to the Senate from Oregon" (Beveridge 2:195–196 and n.1–2; Masters 117 says only that he refused it). Here the post commands the Union's only Pacific coast on a foreign republic's border, so the offer is made in form and Mary refuses: Stuart's version made true (a story assumption; research 56 asks whether it is also history). The Chronicler reports Stuart's story as hearsay (CH6) and calls the episode the great near-miss: the two surveyors would have faced each other across the Siskiyous in 1850. Stuart's promise would have been empty anyway, because Oregon never becomes a state (CR-059). **The regret (K5a)** follows in 1866 | APPROVED; the historical offer is disputed |

#### F2 — 1850–1858: the record hardens

| ID | When | What changes, and why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| K5a | 1866 and after | **The regret.** When Oregon votes itself out in 1866, Lincoln tells Herndon he believes he could have done more for the Union in Oregon than he ever did from Washington. As governor from 1849 he would have known the Willamette's men by name before Monterey did; he might have argued the territory toward statehood before 1859, or held its Union Democrats in 1861. Herndon records it (a Herndon anecdote; the words are invented and are to be written as Herndon's report, not as a Lincoln quotation). **The Chronicler weighs it and finds it half true.** A governor appointed by Taylor would have left office with the Whigs in 1853 (Hist. pattern), six years before the House vote that decided Oregon's fate (CR-059). A governor cannot make Congress admit a state, and a Lincoln who stayed in Oregon would never have debated Douglas or become president. Then the Union of 1861 would have had another man, and the founder a lesser rival. He also notes that Herndon disliked Mrs. Lincoln (Hist.) and liked a story in which her refusal cost the Union a coast (CH6). The irony he allows himself: the surveyor regretted the one piece of ground he had been offered and refused, and the founder had reason to be grateful to the woman who refused it for him | APPROVED |
| K6 | 1850 | **The Compromise without California** (canon §5). Lincoln is out of Congress, so his life does not change. His later speeches do: Peoria's review of 1850 cannot mention California's admission, and prose drafting must check every historical passage that does | Caution for prose |
| K7 | 1854 | **Kansas–Nebraska and Peoria: kept.** Douglas's Chicago and central-route interests now run to the Basin and the Carson transit rather than to San Francisco, but the bill and the repeal of the Missouri line stand. So does Lincoln's return to politics | Hist. kept; APPROVED |
| K8 | 1856 | **The first Republican convention.** Historically it nominated Frémont, and Lincoln "rose to 110 votes" for Vice-President (Masters 136). Here Frémont is the man of Gavilan Peak and the Cahuenga court-martial (canon §§3–4). Whether he is still the nominee is OPEN, and the answer belongs to whoever writes the Union of the 1850s. Lincoln's own result does not depend on it | Deferred |
| K9 | 1858 | **The debates with the new barb.** At Ottawa, Douglas says Lincoln took "the side of the common enemy against his own country" (Beveridge 4:287). Here the common enemy of 1846 is a neighbouring republic with an American storehouse at Sausalito, and Douglas adds the coast (paraphrase only). At Charleston Lincoln brings Ficklin forward as historically: he voted the supplies, and the Ashmun resolution concerned "the manner in which the war was begun" (4:315–316). Here he adds K4's turn, and in 1858 it cuts deep. Douglas is fighting Buchanan over Lecompton (Hist.), and Buchanan is the man who signed the convention. The charge costs Lincoln in Egypt (southern Illinois). He loses the legislature as historically. Net result: the outcome is unchanged and the record is fixed | Hist. debates; barb and turn APPROVED |

#### F3 — 1860: how he is nominated and elected

| ID | When | What changes, and why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| K10 | Chicago, 16–18 May 1860 | **Why Spotty does not stop the nomination.** (a) It cannot be used against him at Chicago: every former Whig in the field carries the same party vote of 1848, and the delegates who decide are former Whigs and Free Soilers. (b) The Republicans make the lost coast an exhibit against the Slave Power: Polk's war of conquest ended with California free and foreign, because the South preferred a foreign California to a free state (canon §§4–5, the sectional lock). In Republican mouths "Spotty" becomes proof that he was right about the war. (c) The charge does bite in the Lower North, in Egypt and southern Indiana. But those counties vote Democratic anyway, and his margins come from the northern counties and from the tariff in Pennsylvania (Hist. pattern), so the managers can count it and still choose him. (d) The historical reasons hold: Seward's radical name and Davis's management (Hist.). The Washoe crisis has not yet begun. Ormsby's defeat at Pyramid Lake (12 May) cannot reach Chicago before the ballots (Hist.: the Pony took six and a half days from Fort Kearny to Fort Churchill in November 1860, and Sumter news about ten days from St. Louis in April 1861) | APPROVED |
| K11 | June–November 1860 | **A campaign fought during a foreign crisis.** Hays's Californian volunteers cross the line, Washington threatens and sends troops and ships, and the Carson Convention settles it in the autumn (canon §8; its date must fall before the vote in November). The Breckinridge and Douglas papers revive "Ranchero Spotty" and ask whether the man who called the last war unconstitutional would defend Washoe. Lincoln makes no speeches, as in history, and refers questioners to his printed record (Hist.). His party answers for him. The Homestead and Pacific-railroad planks become a defence policy: fill the Basin with free farmers and no foreign republic can tempt it. The Democrats claim they made Pacifica withdraw at Carson, and the Republicans reply that weakness invited the crossing. Neither argument moves Pennsylvania or Indiana, which decide on the tariff and the territories (Hist.) | APPROVED |
| K12 | November 1860 | **The arithmetic.** He wins 173 of 296 electoral votes, needing 149: his historical 180 less California's four and Oregon's three (CR-059). The South's split does the rest, as historically | APPROVED (follows CR-059) |
| K13 | March 1861 | **A friend, not a general, to Monterey.** Lincoln names Edward D. Baker minister resident to the Confederation of Pacifica. Baker is his oldest political friend, after whom he named his second son (Hist.), English-born and a famous Republican orator in San Francisco since 1852 (Hist.). Here there is no Oregon seat for him to take in 1860, so he is still in San Francisco, an American who has not naturalized (CR-059 open item 47, resolved this way). The appointment is a message: Lincoln wants a channel, not a quarrel. Baker's dispatches are the first source of Lincoln's reading of Elias (MD3) | APPROVED |

#### F4 — 1861: Sumter and the Pacific

| ID | When | What changes, and why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| K14 | 1 April 1861 | **The second war, offered and refused.** Historically, Seward's memorandum (three days after the 29 March order on Sumter) would demand explanations from Europe's powers, send agents into Canada, Mexico and Central America, and make war if unsatisfied, a foreign war to cement North and South (Masters 390; Hist.: explanations to be demanded "categorically, at once" of Spain and France and sought of Great Britain and Russia, with war on Spain and France if unsatisfied). Here Monterey is on the list as a secondary demand. Washoe's violation and the Union's need for Californian coin (canon §§8–9) are Seward's grievance; the South would never fight to conquer a free California, but he believes the Border States and the Northern Democrats would rally to a stand against a foreign republic. Lincoln answers as in history: he points Seward to the inaugural's policy, says that if a policy must be carried out he must do it (Hist.: Lincoln to Seward, 1 April 1861, "if this must be done, I must do it"), and refuses. **This is where he anticipates Pacifica.** In the same week as the Sumter decision he is offered a war with it and turns it down | Hist. memo; Monterey APPROVED |
| K15 | 29 March – summer 1861 | **Two Sumters.** On 29 March the cabinet divides and Lincoln orders the Sumter expedition (Masters 394–395). The same week he decides the opposite for the Pacific. The Sausalito storehouse is not reinforced. The squadron is ordered to give Monterey no grievance. The Northwest's regulars, strengthened after Washoe, are neither withdrawn east nor reinforced (canon §10). When war comes and Pacifica seals the storehouse and interns its stores (canon §9, APPROVED), he protests and sends nothing. The Chronicler uses the contrast adversarially and then concedes the difference: Sumter was a fort on soil the Union claimed, and Sausalito a privilege in a foreign port. His verdict is that the contrast suggests Sumter was a choice; it does not prove that Lincoln wanted the war. He gives Lincoln's own answer, that the expedition carried bread to a hungry garrison (message of 4 July 1861; Hist.; to verify) | APPROVED |
| K16 | 19 April – late May 1861 | **The proclamation that crosses an ocean.** Davis offers letters of marque. Lincoln's blockade proclamation of 19 April makes privateers answerable under the laws against piracy (Hist.; the clause covers anyone who molests a United States vessel, so a neutral packet is a stretch the officer makes himself). Its text crosses the continent in the newspapers by Pony Express and reaches San Francisco about the first of May (Sumter news took about ten days from St. Louis to Fort Churchill in April 1861, Hist.). The Honolulu mail carries it on, and the Union captain in Hawaiian waters reads it as licence to take a suspected privateering agent from a neutral packet (canon §9: "the intelligence has substance, but the officer exceeds his authority"). Lincoln's own policy, applied five thousand miles away by a man he never met, gives Elias his occasion. The Chronicler's sentence: he foresaw the danger, provided against it, and caused it | APPROVED |
| K17 | June–July 1861 | **The friend carries the offer.** News of the boarding and of Article XIV reaches Washington about mid-June (by ship to San Francisco, then Pony Express). Lincoln sends Baker standing instructions to offer release and an inquiry. His message to the special session on 4 July reports the incident, regrets it and announces the inquiry, and names no enemy (new). It also answers the two pages, without naming the papers that printed them (PP2; G1). After the July offensive Baker makes canon's offer "in exchange for withdrawal" (§9) at Monterey, in public. Elias lays it, with his reply, before the Council. Baker has no power to accept the reply's conditions and asks for his passports (G2) | APPROVED |
| K18 | late July – 6 August 1861 | **This time he shows the spot.** When the fall of Astoria (early July, date to pin) reaches Washington, Lincoln reports the facts and names the place: the Columbia, American soil by the treaty of 1846 (Hist.). He does not ask for a declaration, because the offer is still open in Monterey. The Western members and the men who call 1848 the Trist surrender, who now sit in a Congress the South no longer balances (canon §9), push a recognition of a state of war with the Confederation of Pacifica through before the session ends on 6 August (Hist. dates), over his preference (new; CT4). The act reaches Monterey after Elias's refusal and confirms it. The Monterey papers reprint his spot resolutions of 1847 and name their own spot: the high seas off Oahu, where a Hawaiian sailor died under a Union boarding. The Chronicler's verdict: the first blood was Hawaiian, on the high seas, under a boarding Lincoln had not ordered; the first invasion of 1861 was Pacifican, on American soil, and Elias ordered it. He notes that Hays had crossed the line in 1860 too. Congress gets the foreign war Seward wanted, without the South, and it unites nothing | APPROVED |
| K19 | 1861–1864 | **One war at a time, as strategy.** The rebellion first. The West is held where it can be held and left to the peace (an argument, not a quotation). Canon's limited Iowa expedition through Panama, the Sound foothold and the 1862 Columbia attempt are the whole of his Pacific war (§10). He refuses the Western members' demands for more. Halleck, who sailed east in May 1861 knowing Pacifica's army (CR-064), is his adviser on it and from July 1862 his general-in-chief (Hist.), and the second source of his reading of Elias (MD3). Refusing the second war is Lincoln's answer in G3. If the *Trent* affair stands, Lincoln gives up Mason and Slidell sooner than in history, because he has seen what a boarding costs (new) | APPROVED |
| K20 | 21 October 1861 | **The personal cost.** Baker sails by Panama in August and reaches Washington in September. Lincoln gives him a brigadier's commission and a brigade (new; in history he commanded a brigade at Ball's Bluff while still a senator). He is killed at Ball's Bluff (Hist. date; if CR-064 keeps it), and the news reaches Lincoln at McClellan's headquarters in the War Department (Hist.: Bates, *Lincoln in the Telegraph Office*, 94–96, per the Lincoln Log; Hay's diary). The Pacific war's first cost to Lincoln is his friend, killed in Virginia. Baker and Halleck (CR-064) are both American lawyers in San Francisco who go east in 1861. Halleck leaves in May, before the rupture, and Baker stays to carry the offer. The two make opposite choices with the same knowledge, and the reader should see them meet in Washington. | APPROVED |
| K21 | 1862–1871 | **The river.** The Union holds the Mississippi only from Memphis to Vicksburg, and the Confederacy holds Port Hudson and New Orleans (canon §12). So the historical "The Father of Waters again goes unvexed to the sea" (to Conkling, August 1863; Hist.) is never written. Richmond keeps its tolls on the river until the spring of 1871, when it restores free navigation in the treaty that ends Grant's war, and Pacifica guarantees Confederate independence in the same settlement (canon §13). The man who took Gentry's flatboat to New Orleans in 1828 (Beveridge 1:87) sees the river freed by a negotiation his enemy guarantees | APPROVED |

#### F5 — After the war: how the Union remembers him (replaces L6)

| ID | Years | The memory | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| K22 | 1865–1868 | **"Lincoln's war."** McClellan's Democrats govern, and their history is Masters's case sixty years early: Sumter as a provocation (Masters 392–395), Merryman and the 4 July message (Masters 408, 415), and now the Pacific war begun by his own proclamation (K16). He is the president whose election broke the Union and whose war broke it again. The Republicans answer with a betrayal story: the war was being won, and the peace party, and Pacifican gold, lost it. The gold story is false; Pacifica did nothing in the election (CR-065 P1), and the Chronicler says so | APPROVED |
| K23 | 1868–1871 | **The revanchists' saint, who refuses to be one.** Grant's men invoke him, and their text is his own letter of 1867 (L3). Grant's war of 1870 is fought against Richmond with Pacifica on Richmond's side: a Gulf squadron, turret ships, Logan's mines, rifles and credit, and Pacific ports closed to Union shipping (canon §13). Recommended: the old Lincoln supports Grant's demand, free navigation, his oldest cause (K21), and opposes the war. One war at a time is no longer possible when the two enemies are allied, and he says the river can be had by treaty. Spring 1871 half-vindicates him: the attacks fail, and the river is reopened by negotiation (canon §13). The revanchists keep his name anyway; the Chronicler records that he refused it | APPROVED |
| K24 | 1872–1889 | **The House Divided becomes foreign policy.** The Union's "anti-slavery cold war" (canon §13: Greeley in 1872; Brown's Freedom Act of 1873, under which refugees are free on arrival) is Lincoln's policy of 1858 applied to a foreign country: arrest slavery's spread "and place it where the public mind shall rest in the belief that it is in course of ultimate extinction" (Beveridge 4:216). The Freedom Act is also the Proclamation's afterlife: here Union courts hold that everyone the Proclamation reached is free (new). In 1889 both capitals claim Richmond's abolition, Washington for his containment and Thalassia for its guarantee of the bonds. The Chronicler gives it to the guarantee and records the other claim, and the Republic's critics give it to neither (canon §25) | APPROVED |
| K25 | 1895–1932 | **The democratic Lincoln.** The revanchists discredit themselves in the 1890s and Whitney wins in 1896 (canon §25). After that the Union's memory settles on what it kept: its constitution, its army and its elections. His greatest act is held to be November 1864, when he held the election in wartime, lost it and handed over. If the Gettysburg address stands (CR-064 interface), "government of the people, by the people, for the people" becomes the creed of a republic that lost its war and kept its vote. The *Chicago Tribune* keeps the revanchist Lincoln to the end (CR-048). There is no national memorial in Washington by 1932, only the tomb at Springfield and Illinois's Lincoln (new) | APPROVED |
| K26 | 1863–1932 | **Black memory.** Black Americans remember the Black regiments and his refusal of 1864 to abandon them (CR-065 S6). Inside the Confederacy before 1889, "free by Lincoln's paper" is a claim the enslaved make and the courts refuse. In the rump after 1898 it is part of the Black movement's memory (CR-048) | APPROVED |
| K27 | Pacifica | **The rival half remembered.** Pacifica's schools read the founder's *Two Houses*, and a child meets Lincoln there as the President to whom the Founder wrote. The readers do not print Lincoln's reply: the winner writes the history, as winners do. Lincoln's own speeches, the Lyceum address and Peoria, are read in the history courses of the colleges. The Pacifican public comes to know Lincoln about as well as Americans of our world know Davis or Lee: a name, a defeat, a face on the other side. So the Chronicler's Lincoln is a scholar's recovery, written for readers who have not read the man, and CH1's courtesy is the scholar's and the nomos's, not the public's. Masters says Lincoln "did not so much as look into" Plutarch (Masters 33). Historically Scripps's campaign biography of 1860 said he had read the *Lives*, and Scripps wrote that if he had not, he must read it at once to make the statement good (Hist.: Scripps to Lincoln, 17 July 1860); that he then did rests on later recollection (to verify). The Chronicler sets this beside the founder's copy, read until the binding failed (canon §1) | APPROVED |
