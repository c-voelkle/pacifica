## Part I of revision 15 (folded into Parts G and H in the clean copy)

### The founder in the open: what revision 11 changes (O1–O6, R1–R18)

Added 10 November 2026 (revision 11). The author had revision 10 read against Pindar, the poet of contests, and judged the result: "The idea is for Elias to emerge from the contest as a victorious Pindaric hero. The CR has him emerge as a duplicitous villain."

**What was wrong.** In revisions 5 to 10 the founder won by a reason withheld from his Council, a reply written to be refused, a clause in a contract, sealed orders, an unsigned paper and an override without reasons. At the four heaviest beats every act done in daylight was Lincoln's: an honest offer, a proclamation in his own name, an election held and lost, a brief written without a fee. Against that the founder persuaded, erred, waited and overrode. Lincoln was never beaten by his enemy's hand; he lost by his own people's ballots, as Ajax lost the arms, and the founder's means were those of Odysseus. A reader's sympathy goes to the loser in that story.

**What revision 11 does.** It changes how the founder wins and leaves what he wants alone. The Pacifican nomos already has the form, the contest with declared terms (CR-049), and revision 11 applies it to the founder himself. The reading behind the rules is Pindar's (research 54; none of his wording is quoted): the founder of a city is the highest subject; a victory counts when it is won by a deed done in the open and at risk; measure in victory is the rarest excellence; the rival's worth is the victor's crown; and the song ends with a warning to the man who has won.
