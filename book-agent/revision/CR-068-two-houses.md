### A12. *Two Houses* (PP8b; G6b; in-world document, PROPOSED; a draft for the author, 10 November 2026)

Its facts are those of the round (G6b) and its sources; it adds the one-mile exception to Order No. 11 (Fellman 95) and leaves out the instructions of 16 September and the thirty-nine lashes. Lincoln's sentences are his own, as the books cited there print them. The date and the reference to the spring of 1862 follow G4 and G6b.

> **TWO HOUSES**
>
> To the President of the United States. Monterey, January 1864.
>
> Sir: You have said in a public letter that I keep my mother's house divided. I was born at Franklin, on the Missouri, and I do not deny the house. I deny that I divided it. It was divided when I was a boy in it, and you said so yourself at Springfield before either of us held the office he holds now: "A house divided against itself cannot stand." You concluded that it must become all one thing or all the other. I concluded otherwise, and so did the man whose principles you have called the axioms of free society. Mr. Jefferson's prayer for the eastern confederacy and the western was to "keep them in union if it be for their good, but separate them if it be better." A house that cannot stand as one may stand as two, and the two may be neighbours. That is the whole of my doctrine. I gave it to my Council in August of 1861, in print, with an account of every dealing this Republic had then had east of the mountains. I offered it to you in the spring of 1862, with an armistice, and your Secretary did not think it worth an answer.
>
> You hold the other doctrine, and you are entitled to it. I ask what it is worth in the places where you have had your way. I put three questions. Beside each I print the words and the acts that raise it. The words are all yours, and the acts are your Government's or your own State's.
>
> **The first is Missouri.** Missouri did not leave your Union. Your generals have governed it by martial law since August of 1861. On the fifth of October last you wrote of it: "Each man feels an impulse to kill his neighbor, lest he be first killed by him. Revenge and retaliation follow." Six weeks before you wrote that, your general on the border ordered the people out of Jackson, Cass and Bates counties and the half of Vernon, all but those within a mile of his posts. Twenty thousand people went, and troops from Kansas burned their houses behind them. Your Proclamation does not run in Missouri, and slavery is the law today within a day's ride of the place where I was born. This is a State you kept. What do you intend for eleven that you mean to bring back, and in what year does your army go home?
>
> **The second is Port Royal.** For two years your flag has flown over the Sea Islands of Carolina, with ten thousand people on them who were slaves, and no master within reach. No rebel hinders you there. Your general declared them free, and you unsaid it within the fortnight. Your soldiers took the men out of the fields for a regiment, on an order kept from them until the morning. You set the wage at twenty-five cents for a day's task. And on the ninth of March last you sold the land. Seventy-six thousand acres were offered. Your Government kept sixty thousand. The people who had planted them bought two thousand with what they had saved. Eleven plantations went to a company of gentlemen from Boston for seven thousand dollars, with near a thousand souls living on them. You said at Peoria that no man is good enough to govern another man without that other's consent. Who asked them? Is this the freedom, a wage and a new landlord?
>
> **The third is Illinois.** In June of 1862 your own State voted, by two to one and more, that no black man should settle in it. In September your Secretary of War sent freedwomen and their children north from Cairo to find work, and in October, with an election coming, he stopped. In December you asked your Congress, "why should emancipation south, send the free people north?", and whether the North cannot "decide for itself, whether to receive them." Your coloured soldiers draw less pay than your white ones. A year ago you wrote to the coloured citizens of San Francisco to ask why this Republic's gold stands behind four millions who will never be examined. I went to Honolulu and answered you. Now answer me. Where in your Union may they be examined at all? You free them where you do not rule, and you fence them out where you do.
>
> I do not say that this Republic is just. It does not promise equality, and I will not pretend that it does. Its States keep their own laws of residence, as Hawaii required of us, and Oregon under our compact keeps the law it wrote under yours; I have not struck it down. What the Republic promises is a trial. It gives ability its trial and achievement its honour, and it asks no man his colour at the door of the examination hall. It does not sell the ground from under those who work it. Its terms are in print, and it does what it prints. I ask no more of you than that you print yours.
>
> ELIAS THORNE, Protector.

### A13. Lincoln's reply (PP8c; G6b; in-world document, PROPOSED; a draft for the author, 10 November 2026)

**These are invented words.** Lincoln never wrote this letter, and by this CR's rule his invented words appear only in marked in-world documents. Four passages in it are his own or follow his own (the fourth is the phrase from his letter to Schofield). The standard "constantly looked to, constantly labored for, and even though never perfectly attained, constantly approximated" follows his Springfield speech of 26 June 1857, shortened (Beveridge 4:153). "Physically speaking, we cannot separate" and "Can aliens make treaties easier than friends can make laws?" are from his first inaugural (Hist., from the drafter's memory; to verify against Basler, research 57). The review of revision 13 asked for them: they are his strongest published argument against two houses. His quotations from his own letters of 1863 are as Fellman prints them (pp. 84–85). He does not write the Protector's name (Part D). The occasion and the addressee are suggested and invented; he wrote such letters to named men, as to Conkling in 1863.

> Executive Mansion, Washington, February 1864.
>
> Hon. James C. Conkling.
>
> My dear Sir: You ask me to send some word to be read at your meeting. A paper has come over the mountains in which the Protector at Monterey puts three questions to me, and I will use your invitation to answer them. They are sharply put, and I take them in their order.
>
> But first his doctrine, for he has one. He says that a house which cannot stand as one may stand as two, and the two be neighbors. I said three years ago what I think of that, and I have seen nothing since to change it. Physically speaking, we cannot separate. A husband and wife may be divorced and go out of each other's reach; the parts of this country cannot. They must remain face to face, with one river between them to the sea. Can aliens make treaties easier than friends can make laws? The Protector thinks so, and he has an interest in thinking so, for his own columns stand on our soil while he writes.
>
> He asks about Missouri. I wrote the words he prints, and I do not take them back. Things there are as bad as I said. But he was born in that country, and he knows who began it. The men of the river counties held the Governor's chair in 1861 and tried to carry the State out against the will of its people, and when they failed they took to the brush. I have told my general there to use no measures so strong as to "harass and persecute the people" beyond need. The order for the border counties was a hard one, and it has been suspended. He asks what I intend for the States that are to come back, and when the army goes home. I answered that in December, in a proclamation he has read: when a tenth of the voters of a State take the oath and set up a government, I will know it for the State, and an army has no errand where the laws are obeyed. One thing more belongs in the Protector's column, and he has left it out. A fire burns longer when a neighbor carries wood to it.
>
> He asks next about Port Royal. It is true that the land was sold, and that men from Boston bought more of it than the people who had worked it. It is also true that this Government has opened schools on those islands, has put the musket into those men's hands, and has by my own order set land apart for them in lots that the head of a family can pay for. No government did so much for them before, and this one did it under fire. I do not call it finished. I call it begun.
>
> He asks last about Illinois. I shall not argue that vote here, among the men who cast it. But I have said for some years what I think the fathers meant by their Declaration. They meant to set up a standard for free society, constantly looked to, constantly labored for, and even though never perfectly attained, constantly approximated. A people that has hung such a mark upon its wall may be shown on any day how far short of it they stand. The Protector has shown us, and I thank him for the measurement.
>
> Now I would ask him two questions for his three. When Oregon came to us in 1861 with an article in her constitution shutting out the black man, this Congress told her to strike it out before she could be a State. The Protector's Articles let her keep it, and she has it yet, under his flag. Which of us two fences the black man out of Oregon? And what mark has his Republic hung up, that it could ever be found short of? He tells us himself that it promises no equality. A government that promises nothing cannot be called a hypocrite. Neither can anybody hope in it.
>
> Yours very truly,
>
> A. LINCOLN.
