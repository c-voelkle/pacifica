## What canon says now

- **Lincoln in canon.** He loses the election of 1864 (§12, APPROVED wording); McClellan wins on Union first; Lincoln pursues his last winter offensive, the lame-duck House fails to pass the Thirteenth Amendment, and Hampton Roads fails over separation (§12, PROPOSED). He has no registry entry. CR-065 proposes the ID ABRAHAM-LINCOLN and leaves his life after 4 March 1865 OPEN.
- **The agon so far.** It exists only as argument in draft CRs: consent against perpetual union (CR-065 Part III), "admit to bind" against the impossible convention (CR-060 CT2), free soil against self-government (CR-061 K7), a treaty that cedes no state (CR-062 Part II), Lincoln's restraint set beside Elias's (CR-065 P1–P2, Part VI). No canon passage says how the Chronicler regards Lincoln, what Lincoln thought of Elias, or what the contest is about in Lincoln's own words.
- **1848.** "The House has just voted that the war was unconstitutionally begun", one of Polk's reasons for accepting Trist's treaty that binds the United States to respect California's frontier (§4, PROPOSED — CR-028). Canon does not say who voted.
- **1861.** Washington "eventually offers a route to release and investigation in exchange for withdrawal. Elias demands broader strategic guarantees … a limited crisis becomes a territorial war through choices rather than inevitability" (§9).
- **Slavery's end in the South.** A free-womb amendment in 1874–75 (§13); "final Confederate abolition in 1888–1889 is financed by long compensation bonds secured on customs co-administered by Pacifica" (§25). Pacifica enforces a fugitive agreement with Richmond from 1871–72 until abolition, and "when habeas corpus frees a returned fugitive, the cabinet overrides the court under the enabling law" (§13). "Douglass and other critics see a door closed just as enslaved people become free" (§25, 1888).
- **The Chronicler.** A scholar of the imperial order "steeped in classical models of historical rise and decline", dependent on testimony and papers for the early years; "once an admirer of the founding order"; later voices "may challenge his interpretations without establishing that he knowingly lies" (CT-CHRONICLER; larger movement).

## What this CR does

1. States what the contest is about, in each man's declared terms (Part A).
2. Sets the Chronicler's stance and method toward Lincoln: adversarial in judgment and selection, never in fact (Part B).
3. Places the contest's moves on the timeline from 1838 to 1867 and its sequels to 1889, most of them Lincoln's real words and votes, a few of them PROPOSED divergences that follow from Pacifica's existence (Part C).
4. Proposes Lincoln's life after March 1865 as the agon's coda, answering CR-062 open decision 16 and CR-065's OPEN (Part D).
5. Names the voices that answer the Chronicler's Lincoln (Part E).
6. Sets out the contest as eleven rounds, five opened by Elias and six by Lincoln. It gives each man's reading of the other and where that reading breaks, lists the papers in which they answer each other's ideas, and names the reason Elias wins, with its Pacifican schoolbook form (Part G).
7. Traces how Pacifica's existence alters Lincoln's career from 1846 to his memory in 1932: the sting of 1848, the nomination and election of 1860, Sumter and Sausalito, his reaction to Pacifica's entry and how the Union remembers him (Part F).
8. Gives voice samples of the Chronicler writing Lincoln (Appendix A, not storyline text) and an index of the source passages (Appendix B).

It changes no date and no approved decision. It adds beats, a frame and a voice. It proposes new facts where canon is blank: the Union's recognition of a state of war with Pacifica in August 1861 (CT4), the Terms of Monterey of August 1863 (CT10), Lincoln's offer of 1864 to Pacifica's people (G8), the Port Townsend invitation (CT11) and the public papers listed in Part G (PP1–PP13).

## Decisions

### Part A — The terms of the contest (AG1–AG6)

The Pacifican nomos already has a custom for this: the **contest with declared terms**, where "questions and criteria are published before proofs, predictions or demonstrations" (CR-049 E-table). The Chronicler applies it to the two men. Each published his terms; the Chronicler judges each by his own.

| ID | The question | Lincoln's declared terms (Hist.) | Elias's declared terms (canon) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AG1 | **Founding or keeping.** Is the republic's work done? | Lyceum, 27 January 1838: the fathers' work is done; the danger is a man of "the family of the lion, or the tribe of the eagle" for whom "a seat in Congress, a gubernatorial or a presidential chair" is not enough. "Towering genius disdains a beaten path … It scorns to tread in the footsteps of any predecessor, however illustrious." Such genius "burns for distinction; and if possible, it will have it, whether at the expense of emancipating slaves or enslaving freemen." The remedy: reverence for the laws as "the political religion of the nation", and "cold, calculating, unimpassioned reason" (Beveridge 1:229–230) | The Colonel's Plutarch gives Elias "Lycurgus and Numa, who founded by law" (CR-066); *On the Public Arts* (1851–54), written as a lawgiver and never published as a law: "What we build must let those who come after us see further than we do, and beat us" (§5, CR-049). A founding is never finished; a lawgiver works through habits | PROPOSED |
| AG2 | **Consent and union.** May a people leave? | 12 January 1848: "Any people anywhere being inclined and having the power have the right to rise up and shake off the existing government, and form a new one that suits them better. This is a most valuable, a most sacred right … Any portion of such people that can may revolutionize and make their own of so much of the territory as they inhabit" (Beveridge 2:129; Masters 99). | AG3 | **Who governs whom.** | Peoria, 16 October 1854: "no man is good enough to govern another man without that other's consent. I say this is the leading principle, the sheet-anchor of American republicanism" (Beveridge 3:255). Charleston, 18 September 1858: "I am not, nor ever have been, in favor of bringing about in any way the social and political equality of the white and black races" (Beveridge 4:312) | Rank by examination and achievement, open to every faith and background, never a quota (CR-037); a deeply unequal order built for power and fast adaptation (author, 19 October) | PROPOSED |
| AG4 | **Equality as a promise.** | Springfield, 26 June 1857: the fathers "meant to set up a standard maxim for free society, which should be familiar to all, and revered by all; constantly looked to, constantly labored for, and even though never perfectly attained, constantly approximated" (Beveridge 4:153) | *On the Public Arts*: "A people is as strong as the abilities it raises. Find ability early, train it hard, give it work and judge it by its works" (§5). Not equality but trial: "Give ability its trial and achievement its honour." | AG5 | **Power.** What would you do with it? | Peoria: "If all earthly power were given me, I should not know what to do as to the existing institution. Power asked for before it is used, and then never given back | PROPOSED |
| AG6 | **Mind and matter.** | Temperance address, 22 February 1842: "all appetites controlled, all poisons subdued, all matter subjected — mind, all conquering mind, shall live and move, the monarch of the world … Reign of reason, all hail!" (Beveridge 2:32). Illinois's internal-improvement system of 1837, which Lincoln championed, ten million dollars of state bonds (Beveridge 1:196), ends in insolvency, a debt of ten million dollars against two hundred thousand of annual revenue (Beveridge 1:256); repudiation is urged, and Lincoln votes to table it (1:276, 286) | The Public Arts, the Calculation Office (1855), the Laboratory of Light, Electricity and Matter (1859) and their royalty (CR-049); a state that grows rich on what ruined Illinois's credit | PROPOSED |

**Why these six.** They are the contest Nietzsche's essay describes: two men of the same gifts (both surveyors in their twenties, both largely self-taught readers of law and history, both sons of poor men; each said his father had lost land to the law, and only Elias is believed), each the measure of the other. Lincoln at the Lyceum names the danger the Greeks met with ostracism, the man without a rival; Pacifica's nomos meets it with the contest. The Chronicler's frame is that Lincoln was the rival who made Elias's victory worth having.

**Where each term is fought.** The terms are not only the Chronicler's. The two men argue them with each other, in the papers of Part G: consent and union in the two pages and Lincoln's answer (G1), in the offer of 1861 (G2) and in Oregon (G10); founding or keeping, and power, in the Terms of Monterey and the letters that answer them (G6, G8, G9, G10); who governs whom, and ability, in the Proclamation, the officer Elias kept and the refuge (G3, G4, G5); mind and matter in the gold of 1867 (G11).

### Part B — The Chronicler's Lincoln: stance and method (CH1–CH10)

| ID | Rule | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH1 | **The agonal courtesy.** The Chronicler never belittles Lincoln's mind, honesty or courage. In Pacifica a victory over a small man is no victory; the founder's rival must be great or the founder is not. This is why a Thornist historian is fair to Lincoln: the culture requires it, not his private virtue | Gives the fairness a Pacifican motive, so it does not read as the author's thumb on the scale | PROPOSED |
| CH2 | **Adversarial in judgment, never in fact.** He quotes Lincoln exactly and at length, and never puts words in his mouth. | CH3 | **He judges each man by the terms he declared** (Part A), and admits it when the test goes against Elias. From 1863 Elias argues in public too, in the Terms of Monterey and their preface (PP7), and where the two differ the Chronicler holds him to the paper he published | The custom of the contest with declared terms (CR-049); keeps the adversarial voice honest by construction | PROPOSED |

### Part F — Lincoln's career in a world with Pacifica (K1–K27)

Added 9 November 2026 at the author's request: "determine more precisely how Lincoln's life and career would have been affected and altered by the existence of the California Republic and of the State of Pacifica." **The rule used here:** Lincoln's life changes only where Pacifica's existence reaches it, and every change names its cause in canon. Where nothing reaches him, his history stands. His words stay his own. Where the story needs him to say something new, the draft gives the argument, not invented sentences.

**The short answers.**
- **How he gets elected after Spotty.** The charge costs him most where he was already losing: Illinois in 1848 and 1858, and the Democratic counties of the Lower North. It cannot be used against him at Chicago, where every former Whig shares the record, and in 1860 the Republicans turn it into proof that he was right about the war. His own answer, the Senate's roll call, shares the blame rather than clearing him (K3, K4, K9–K12).
- **Did he anticipate Pacifica at Sumter?** Yes, and he provided against it. Seward's foreign-war memorandum of 1 April puts Monterey in front of him that week, and he refuses it. He provisions Sumter and deliberately does not provision Sausalito. Then his own proclamation on privateers, read off Oahu weeks later, gives Elias his occasion (K14–K16).
- **His reaction when Pacifica enters.** He offers redress through his oldest friend, Edward Baker, and names the spot. He asks for no declaration; Congress recognizes the war over his preference. He keeps to one war at a time and loses Baker at Ball's Bluff (K17–K20).
- **How the Union remembers him.** He is not a martyr. First he is blamed for starting the war. Then the revanchists make him their saint. He himself refuses that role. In the end he is remembered for what the Union kept: the president who held the election and lost it (K22–K27).

**Kept from revision 4:** the two pages (G1), the Assembly crisis set against Lincoln's election (G8), the withheld signature (G9) and the letter to Oregon (G10). **Dropped:** the rounds in which Elias only waited; the reason "he left his enemy a way to lose that it could live with" as the whole cause of victory (it is now the second of three, G6); the schoolbook triad "We asked. We paid. We left the door open."; and Deseret as a round (it is Lincoln's own mistake and stays in CR-060 and M11).

**The shape of the contest.** Lincoln's plan is a sequence: the rebellion first, then the West with the whole army (K19). Elias's plan is to keep the first war from being finished before the Union's voters are asked whether to go on with both, and to make the second war the one that can be ended at a known price. The date he aims at is 8 November 1864, and he provides for the case that the date fails him (G8). Lincoln's counter is to strike at the consent Elias's war depends on: Hawaii's yearly assent, London's credit and Monterey's own Assembly.

**What counts as a round.** One man opens, the other answers, and at least one of them acts on a stated reading of his rival. Each round says whether that reading held. Elias opens five and Lincoln six. The deeds are canon or proposed in CR-059 to CR-065 unless a round marks a deed as new. Part G adds the readings, the papers and the intention that links the deeds. G1 is Elias's opening only if open decision 3 goes that way.

#### The rounds

**G1 — The two pages. Elias opens, March–April 1861.** *(PROPOSED)*
- **His reading (MD1):** the lawyer will rest on the law. He will say that a State cannot leave a perpetual Union, and that Texas in 1836 was a different case. Any legal distinction serves the West, because Oregon and Washington are territories, and a territory is not one of "these states".
- **The move:** when the inaugural reaches San Francisco, the press prints "the union of these states is perpetual" (Hist.; research 32) beside the right of any people "to rise up and shake off the existing government" (Beveridge 2:129), without comment (M8; PP1). The readers it is meant for are the West's Americans, above all Oregon's Douglas Democrats (CR-059).
- **The answer:** Lincoln does not rest on the law. He concedes the right of 1848 whole and reads his own page more closely than its printer did. The right belongs to a people "being inclined and having the power", to "any portion of such people that can" (Beveridge 2:129). It is a right to make a revolution oneself. And the inaugural allows that revolution may be morally justified when a majority deprives a minority of "any clearly written constitutional right" (Hist.: first inaugural; research 17), so whoever claims the right must name his wrong (PP2). The message names no country. It is written before Astoria falls, and it is aimed at the South, which has the power and no wrong.
- **What it does to the West:** Elias had prepared for a legal test and was given a moral one. Article XIV already forbade annexation by decree (canon §§8–9). After PP2 the Union's own President is the author of the standard by which any Western vote will be judged, and from August it applies to an occupied country.
- **The exchange on the wrong** comes later, in the papers that follow. Elias names the wrong in his reply to Baker (PP3): in 1859 Congress refused Oregon statehood for the votes it would have cast (CR-059). Lincoln answers in 1863 (PP8): a later Congress passed Oregon's Enabling Act on 2 March 1861, and it was Pacifica's landing that stopped the convention (CR-059 E2, W1). Neither convinces the other.
- **Who gains:** Lincoln. He sets the test, and it becomes a third reason why Elias cannot refuse Oregon's vote in 1865 (CT9).

**G2 — Bread. Lincoln opens, June–August 1861.** *(Canon sequence; the design and the reasons PROPOSED; CT13)*
- **His reading (MD3):** the eagle will refuse a fair offer, and the refusal will undo him where he is weakest. The Protectorship runs one year and needs Hawaii's assent to be renewed (canon §9). Victoria beat him on this ground in 1860, and London's bondholders warned him then (canon §8).
- **The move:** the method of Sumter, used that spring (K15): offer so much that whoever refuses has begun the war. Through Baker, in public, Lincoln offers everything Hawaii demanded in May, release, an inquiry, regret and compensation for the dead sailor's family, in exchange for withdrawal (canon §9; K17).
- **The answer:** Elias does not refuse alone. He lays the offer whole, with his reply, before the Council, where Hawaii's delegation sits, and asks its concurrence before he answers Baker (PP3).
- **Why the Council goes with him.** He is asking two small states to stay at war with the United States, and the reasons have to weigh more than the danger. He gives six, and the minutes keep them (PP3b).
  1. *The offer pays for the man and keeps the right.* Lincoln's proclamation of 19 April stands, and with it the squadron's claim to stop a neutral packet at sea on suspicion (K16). Washington calls the captain's act an excess. It does not give up the claim, and cannot while it blockades the South; historically it gave up the men of the *Trent* that winter and not the right (Hist.: Seward's reply of 26 December 1861 held that Wilkes might stop and search the *Trent* and erred only in not bringing her in for adjudication; research 36). Every Hawaiian vessel sails under that claim for as long as the war lasts.
  2. *The squadron lives on this coast.* California's harbours are closed to it and the Sausalito store is sealed (canon §9). What it has left in the Pacific is the Columbia and the Sound. Withdraw, and the ships that killed a Hawaiian go back to their anchorages with a grievance and a war's licence. Keep the river's mouth and the Sound, and the nearest American dockyard is round Cape Horn. For an island kingdom that is the difference between a neighbour's navy and a visitor's.
  3. *Article XIV is being tried for the first time.* Hawaii gave its defence and its navy to the common institutions in 1856 (canon §8). If the first Hawaiian killed by a foreign warship is settled by the offender's own inquiry, the article will not deter the next captain.
  4. *The danger will never be smaller.* The news of Bull Run has just come (21 July 1861, Hist.; assumed to stand; the news reached San Francisco by Pony Express on 2 August 1861, research 36). The Union cannot hold the road to its own capital, and it wants every hull it owns for the blockade. In two years it will have lost the South or built the largest army and navy in the hemisphere. Either way the question of the Pacific will be asked again, and on worse terms.
  5. *The people who trusted us.* The Oregonians who made a civil compact with the army (CR-059 W2) would be handed back to a government that suspended the writ of habeas corpus that spring and held Merryman against the Chief Justice (Masters 408).
  6. *The river is worth the risk.* The Willamette's wheat, the Sound's timber and coal, on which both member states' steamers run, and the new gold on the Clearwater, which comes down the Columbia (Hist.: Pierce's party on the Clearwater in August 1860, Elk City 1860, the Salmon River in 1861; research 36; CR-059 to confirm).
- **The limits he accepts, and Hawaii's price.** No column goes east of the mountains. The mandate stays one year, and nothing is annexed by decree: the resolution of May is read into the reply. The compensation is accepted for the sailor's family whatever else is refused. And the fleet's first station is Oahu: the first armoured ship the yards complete goes to Honolulu, not to the Columbia (PROPOSED; open decision 35). Sherman supports the protection and objects that the objectives have grown (canon §9). Victoria's party concurs on the first three reasons and records its doubt of the sixth.
- **The reply (PP3)** asks three things of Washington: that it renounce search of neutral vessels in Pacifican and Hawaiian waters; that it bind itself by treaty to amnesty and the writ for every inhabitant of the Columbia; and that the Enabling Act's convention meet with both armies withdrawn. That is canon's "broader strategic guarantees and protection for collaborators" (§9). Lincoln can grant none of them. The first would undo his blockade. The second is a foreign guarantee of American citizens' rights on American soil, which is Elias's own objection of 1848 turned round, and Elias knows it.
- **The seventh reason,** which he does not give the Council: if Pacifica withdraws, Lincoln has his sequence, and a Union that has finished with the South comes back to the Pacific with a million men. The Chronicler says so: the offer was honest, the six reasons were true, the reply was written to be refused, and the choice was the founder's (A3; CH4).
- **Who gains:** both. Lincoln has the record, and Victoria has it to use at the renewals (CR-059 W4; CR-060 C6). Elias has the war, and he has the Council's hand on the refusal, given for reasons Hawaii holds as its own. Lincoln's reading foresaw a man refusing alone.

**G3 — The river's mouth. Elias opens, autumn 1861–1862.** *(Canon deeds; the design PROPOSED)*
- **His reading (MD1):** Lincoln's war is a sequence, and the river comes first. He took Gentry's flatboat to New Orleans in 1828 (Beveridge 1:87). He will give ground in the West to keep the sequence, so the place to break it is the river's mouth.
- **The move:** a hand under the first war. Pacifican credit, procurement and Logan's small technical mission help hold New Orleans in the spring of 1862, and supplies go round the Horn to Matamoros (canon §11). No Pacifican army goes east. Ericsson, who came west about 1855 when his armoured-ship proposal had found no builder (canon §8), builds his turret ship for the Columbia, and the Union has no *Monitor* at Hampton Roads (canon §§10–11).
- **The answer is the one Elias expected:** Lincoln keeps the West cheap. He sends four Iowa regiments through Panama to hold the Sound, and makes one attempt to recover the lower Columbia in 1862, which the turret ship turns back (canon §10). After that he refuses the Western members' demands for more (K19). He keeps Grant after a worse Shiloh than history's (CR-064 S5–S8, C2), and Grant takes Vicksburg, though its army escapes (canon §11).
- **Who gains:** Elias, and it decides the war, though nobody can know that before November 1864. New Orleans stands and the lower river stays shut. MD1 was right. Lincoln loses only what he cannot hold, and the second front costs him about 3,000 men at Shiloh (CR-064 S3, MODEL).
- **The idea (AG4):** Ericsson, and Sherman, who came to Monterey's service in 1857 when his banking ended (CR-064 H4), are the founder's rule in practice: "give it work and judge it by its works" (canon §5). Lincoln, keeping the general the newspapers wanted dismissed, judges the same way. The Chronicler notes that each man's best principle is found in the other's practice (M2).
- **The price to Elias:** he is paymaster to a slave republic. Lincoln collects in G4.

**G4 — The Proclamation, sent west. Lincoln opens, September 1862 – January 1863.** *(Hist. acts, assumed standing with CR-064 E5; their western aim and Pacifica's answer PROPOSED; CT15)*
- **His reading (MD3, with what Halleck and Baker have told him):** Pacifica lives on London's credit and on Hawaii's yearly assent. Its creed is ability. It is banker to Richmond.
- **What Elias has judged (MD1):** he reads Lincoln's letter to Greeley of 22 August correctly. "If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that" (Hist.: wording checked, research 23). Lincoln will free slaves if it serves the Union. Elias's error is about the hour. He judges that Lincoln cannot do it without a victory, and that there is none coming: Hampton Roads is lost, New Orleans holds, Shiloh was a held landing and the Peninsula has failed (canon §11; CR-064). In the early autumn he widens the mission to the Confederate armies and the Matamoros credit (canon §11; the dating PROPOSED).
- **The move:** Antietam is a drawn battle, and Lincoln calls it victory enough (17 and 22 September 1862, Hist.). Here the preliminary Proclamation is also aimed west (PROPOSED). In London it raises the price of lending to the slaveholders' supplier. In Honolulu the mission press puts it into Hawaiian, for the legislature that must renew the Protectorship (no Hawaiian printing of it has been found; PROPOSED, research 19). In January Lincoln answers a memorial from the coloured citizens of San Francisco (PP4; Mifflin Gibbs a candidate signer, CR-059 V13): no man is good enough to govern another without that other's consent (Beveridge 3:255), and a republic that examines every child for ability lends its gold so that four million may never be examined at all.
- **The answer (PP4b), which gives no ground.** Pacifica's government publishes an account in English, Spanish and Hawaiian. It has the nomos's form, two columns of deeds with their dates.
  - *The Republic's column.* Treaties with the Native nations signed in 1851–52 and kept; homelands that cannot be sold; no volunteer company ever paid from the treasury (canon §5; CR-059 N5). In the Southwest, a treaty that recognizes the Diné homeland (canon §10). And in New Mexico, where the Republic found debt peonage and a trade in Native captives when its column arrived, both are being ended by its own law, with compensation, against the resistance of its own new citizens (canon §10; CR-061). The account presents this as what the Republic does where it governs, at its own cost. It is not offered as a reply to the Proclamation, and the dates show that it was begun before it (start date to confirm in CR-061).
  - *The Union's column.* On 26 December 1862, six days before the Proclamation took effect, thirty-eight Dakota men were hanged together at Mankato by the President's order (Hist.: thirty-nine approved in his message of 11 December, one reprieved on 23 December; research 35). The Governor of Minnesota had told his legislature that the Sioux "must be exterminated or driven forever beyond the borders of Minnesota" (Hist.: message of 9 September 1862; research 35). In February and March 1863 Congress annuls the Dakota treaties and removes the Dakota and the Winnebago from the state (Hist.: acts of 16 February, 21 February and 3 March 1863). The Homestead Act and the Pacific Railway Act of 1862 give away the country of nations that were never asked.
  - *The charge.* By the President's own sentence from Peoria, who asked the Dakota? And the account draws the distinction on which it rests. In the matter of slavery the Republic is charged with lending to a government that does the wrong. In the matter of the nations the Union does the wrong itself, with its own army, on its own soil, over its own President's signature. The Republic has a guilty debtor. The Union is the guilty party.
  - *A second page.* Beside Lincoln's letter to the coloured citizens of San Francisco the papers print his address of 14 August 1862 to a deputation of free coloured men, whom he advised to leave the country for a colony in Central America (Hist.; research 35). The Republic's ports are open to them, and its examinations.
- **What is owed to Lincoln, and paid (CH1).** He cut the list of the condemned from 303 to 39, and reprieved one more, against the fury of Minnesota, and his message of December 1862 asked Congress "whether our Indian system shall not be remodeled" (Hist.; research 35). The Chronicler says so. He adds that the remodelling never came, and that this is the one paper of the contest Lincoln never answered.
- **The officer who said no:** that winter Sherman refuses the assignment to the Confederate armies, on his own ground, and Vance takes it (canon §11). Lincoln's reading expects the eagle to break an officer who refuses him. Elias keeps Sherman as Inspector-General. An office is held for its work, and no one may make its holder give a false account of it: "not a patron, not a minister, not I" (canon §5).
- **Who gains: Lincoln, somewhat.** The account answers a charge with a heavier charge. It does not answer the letter's question, and the Chronicler says that too (A8b). The Richmond credit goes on. London charges more for the Republic's paper (MODEL OPEN). In 1864 Victoria makes Hawaii's free-soil rule her fourth condition (CT8). MD1 does not survive the winter.
- **How it turns against him.** Lincoln aimed at Honolulu and expected the mission churches to carry the legislature. He had not reckoned who would read the two papers there. The Hawaiian-language press prints both (*Ka Hoku o ka Pakipika*, Kalākaua's paper against the mission press, 1861–63, Hist.; research 19). Its readers are not planters reading about slaves. They are a Native people, under their own king, reading about Mankato, and about a government whose settlers and missionaries' sons already own much of their best land. The renewal of 1863 passes without conditions (CR-060), and when Lincoln offers Hawaii its sugar market in 1864 (G8), the delegation remembers which government hangs Natives. In London the Aborigines' Protection Society takes up the account (the society Hist., founded 1837, with its journal the *Aborigines' Friend*; its taking up the account PROPOSED, research 35), so he does not have the whole of that city's conscience. And the account travels east with the traders. From 1863 the Shoshone and their neighbours make compacts with the Republic (CR-060 D9, N2), and the roads by which a Union army would one day have to come run through nations that have chosen their friend. That enters Elias's reckoning in 1864 (G8).
- **What the Chronicler does not see (CH7).** He quotes the account with less reserve than any other paper. The nations in the Republic's column had compacts and no ballot. In the winter of 1865–66 a company of Owyhee miners kills a Paiute camp, and the Republic tries them (CR-060 M8–M10). Later voices say what he does not (Part E).

**G5 — The refuge. Elias opens, spring 1863.** *(CR-059 W7; the papers PROPOSED)*
- **His reading (MD2, new):** Lincoln now does whatever the law will bear, and his war rests on a draft (the Enrollment Act of 3 March 1863, Hist.). The men who will not be drafted are a consent he cannot compel. He will demand them back, or arrest them on the roads, and either will serve Pacifica in Missouri and the Lower North.
- **The move:** published terms of passage. Any man may come west and take land under the family land law, and no one is extradited for evading a foreign draft (CR-059 W7). Pacifica's papers print the terms under Lincoln's own sentence from Peoria, that no man is good enough to govern another without that other's consent (Beveridge 3:255; PP5). It is Elias's first use of Lincoln's idea against Lincoln's law.
- **The answer:** Lincoln makes no demand and no arrest on the roads. He answers the argument, in the manner of his letter to Corning (PP6). Consent is given at the ballot box and is not withdrawn at the muster roll. And the Protector, who keeps a conscript army of his own (CR-059 S3), objects to conscription only east of the mountains. He cannot stop them in any case: the Platte road is a thousand miles long, and he has no cavalry to spare for it. He sets the Homestead Act of 1862 (Hist.) against the land law.
- **How many come (story assumption, author, 10 November 2026):** far more than in our history, where the men who went west to avoid the draft found only mining camps. Here they find a government that publishes terms, pays in coin and grants land. Working figure: several tens of thousands of men, with their families, from 1863 to 1865 (MODEL OPEN; for the population model and CR-059 research task 25). They settle Idaho, the Columbia and the Basin, and they are a labour force and a reserve the Republic did not have in 1861.
- **What it does to the Union:** every man who leaves makes the next draft call fall harder on those who stay. The call of July 1864 for half a million men (Hist.: proclamation of 18 July 1864, with a draft after 5 September for unfilled quotas; research 24) comes in the campaign summer. It is one of the causes of November (G8).
- **Who gains:** Elias, clearly. Lincoln gives him no grievance and has the better of the argument, and loses the men. MD2 was wrong in detail: Elias expected force and got a jest.

**G6 — One price. Elias opens, August 1863.** *(New; PROPOSED; CT10)*
- **His reading (MD2):** Lincoln speaks to peoples, and his war against the South has no stopping place, because a perpetual Union cannot name a price. For the West he could name one, since on his own theory a territory can be ceded (G7), but he will not while he hopes to finish the South first. So Elias names it for him, when it cannot be taken for fear or for greed: the month after Gettysburg and Vicksburg (the Eastern battle assumed standing with CR-064 E5).
- **The move:** *The Terms of Monterey* (PP7), in six heads. (1) Pacifica asks no state of the Union. (2) Each territory's future is settled by the act of its own inhabitants. This is Lincoln's test from G1. (3) The public domain and the public works are paid for in gold. (4) The ports stay open to American commerce. (5) Nothing is asked concerning the South. (6) The heads will not be raised by victory or lowered by defeat. The nomos has the form already: a contest with declared terms, published before the proof (CR-049).
- **The preface answers the Lyceum address (AG1).** Lincoln said in 1838 that the fathers' field was harvested, and that a towering genius who found nothing left to build would pull down (Beveridge 1:229–230). Elias's argument: then the cure is more to build, not more reverence. A republic that can only keep must hold by force the people who would rather found. On the eagle: the Protector can be dismissed every twelvemonth by either of two legislatures (canon §§9, 12), and the President by no one for four years. (Argument only; text OPEN.)
- **The preface calls Jefferson as its witness.** Lincoln has written that "the principles of Jefferson are the definitions and axioms of free society" (to Pierce and others, 6 April 1859, Hist.; research 37). Jefferson did not think the continent could be governed from one capital. In 1803 he wrote of the Mississippi country that if its people saw their interest in separation he would not take sides against them, and wished both confederacies well (to Breckinridge, 12 August 1803, Hist.; to verify). And of the settlement at the mouth of the Columbia he wrote to Astor that he viewed it "as the germ of a great, free and independent empire on that side of our continent" (9 November 1813, Hist.; research 37). The preface notes where the Republic's columns landed in July 1861: at Astoria. It is the two-pages method again, and this time the second page is by the author of the Declaration.
- **The answer (PP8):** MD3 predicted terms that grow and a man who never speaks of his own removal. Lincoln sees that the paper fits neither and answers the argument he has been given. He does not dispute Jefferson, whom he cannot disown. He reads him closely, as he read his own page in 1861. Jefferson's empire was to be "free and independent", and he expected liberty and self-government to spread from it. So the question is not whether a second republic may stand on the Pacific. It is whether this one is Jefferson's: a Protector with a king in his confederation and his credit at Richmond. A price named by the man in possession is a ransom. And he takes up the preface's last claim: the Protector says he may be turned out, so let it be tried. He tries it in G8. From this paper he reads Elias differently (MD4).
- **What Lincoln has conceded:** that a free and independent republic on the Pacific is no offence against the fathers. It is the first time a President of the United States has said it in print, and McClellan's negotiators quote it in 1865 (PROPOSED).
- **What the heads cover, and what they do not:** they are principles, and the map moves inside them. Washoe is retaken by arms the next spring (G7), western Montana's camps turn west in 1863–64 (canon §10), and the peace adds the guano islands (canon §12). But no state is asked for, Oregon is put to its own vote, the price is paid in gold, and nothing is asked about the South (CR-062 T2, T4, T5, T13, O1). After November 1864, when Elias could have asked for more, he does not (G9).
- **Who gains:** Elias. The Terms do not move the Union's election, whose causes are in G3 (CR-065 W5). They decide what the election's winner can do with his victory: a President pledged to Union first finds the Western war already priced, signs an armistice within three months of taking office (canon §12) and turns to the South (CT14). It is the second cause of W1.

**G7 — The lock. Lincoln opens, December 1863 – spring 1864.** *(CR-060; Port Townsend new; PROPOSED; CT11)*
- **His reading (MD3, corrected by the Terms):** the Protector asks no state. Then make states.
- **The move:** his best-known device, a loyal government made from the loyal men there are: the restored government of Virginia in 1861, West Virginia in 1863, the amnesty proclamation of 8 December 1863 (Hist.; research 25). For the West: Nevada's Enabling Act and Colorado's, both of 21 March 1864 (Hist.; CR-060 C4, "Lincoln's lock"). For Oregon: the Unionists displaced to Port Townsend (CR-059 V3) and whichever of the delegates elected in June 1861 (CR-059 V5) will join them are to sit as Oregon's convention, under Casey's guns, and adopt the constitution the Enabling Act asks for (PROPOSED). The device has never needed a quorum.
- **The answer:** Elias, on MD2, has expected it. In Washoe he moves before the convention can meet: in the spring of 1864, with about 1,500 men and no fight, and the garrison marches out armed and unparoled on Sherman's terms (CR-060 C5–C6). In Oregon Lincoln has counted on Pacifica hindering the delegates, which would make the Port Townsend body the one free voice of Oregon. Elias publishes a safe-conduct and a passage for every delegate of 1861 who wishes to go (PROPOSED). Few go. Most are Douglas Democrats, and the Enabling Act requires a constitution without the exclusion article (CR-059 E2), which they will not write in exile to please a Republican Senate.
- **How the lock fails:** Lincoln is beaten in his own Congress, as he was historically over Louisiana. Historically the Senate never seated R. King Cutler and Charles Smith, the senators-elect of Lincoln's Louisiana government; Lincoln returned Banks's statement to Trumbull's committee on 9 January 1865 and declined to take sides, framing the question as whether Louisiana could be brought into proper practical relations with the Union "sooner, by admitting or by rejecting the proposed Senators" (Hist.: Basler 8:206–207); Trumbull's report of 18 February 1865 (S. No. 117) never came to a vote; and the joint resolution of 8 February 1865 (H.R. 126) shut Louisiana out of the electoral count (Hist.: Basler 8:270). In this timeline New Orleans stays Confederate (G3), so there is no Louisiana government to reject. **Story assumption (author's request, 10 November 2026):** Port Townsend's convention is the Union's first test of the device. In the spring of 1864 it sends two senators-elect to Washington. The Senate refers their credentials to the Judiciary Committee, and Trumbull reports in terms like those of his historical report on Louisiana. The credentials are never brought to a vote, and the Wade–Davis bill passes on 2 July 1864 as in history (Hist. date). Lincoln answers as he answered Trumbull in 1865: he neither takes sides nor argues, and states the question (research 33). The safe-conducts have given the Senate a count: the delegates were free to come, and they stayed at home. The Radicals will not seat a state made by the Executive from a remnant, and Congress passes the restoration programme instead (CR-059 W12). Colorado's voters reject statehood on 11 October 1864 on their own, 4,672 to 1,520, chiefly over a slate of officers the convention had chosen in advance (Hist.; research 25).
- **Who gains:** split, and Lincoln makes Elias pay. Washoe, renounced at Carson in 1860 and left alone for three years (CR-060 C2), is retaken by arms in the season a state is called. The Union's papers have their sentence: he asks no state, and he marches the week one is called. Victoria attaches her conditions to the third renewal (CR-060 C6). From the safe-conducts Lincoln completes his second reading (MD4): this man binds himself in public where it pays him.

**G8 — Two ballot boxes. Lincoln opens, spring 1864.** *(Canon §12, CR-065; the offer, the reasons and the provisions PROPOSED)*
- **His reading (MD4):** the man binds himself in public, and he has said in print that he can be dismissed. Monterey's Assembly holds war-weary merchants and American-born members with divided loyalties (canon §9), and Hawaii's assent to the third renewal falls due in the spring (working date late May, canon §9).
- **The move (PP9):** an offer to the people of Pacifica over the Protector's head, published through neutral papers in the weeks of the enabling acts, in time to reach Honolulu before the renewal. Leave the Columbia and the Sound, and the Union will waive its war claims, renew the commercial articles of 1848 and 1860 with the Confederation, compensate the sailor's family and admit Hawaiian sugar free of duty under those articles (historically the Union's reciprocity with Hawaii came only in 1875). It is the trial of the preface's claim. In September the State Department publishes Pacifican correspondence with Richmond, captured at sea, to draw an answer from Monterey before the vote (PROPOSED; CT12).
- **That summer** Lincoln writes the blind memorandum of 23 August (CR-065 E1). He expects to lose, and he binds himself in advance to work with the man who beats him.
- **Why the Council and Hawaii hold.** An Assembly majority takes up the offer (canon §12). The Council and Hawaii's delegation do not, for reasons Elias puts to them and they can check (PROPOSED).
  1. *The offer is a promise its maker cannot keep.* Free sugar and renewed articles need a treaty and two-thirds of the Senate. The man who offers them faces an election in November.
  2. *What it asks for is the reason of 1861.* To leave the Columbia and the Sound is to give the American squadron its stations back (G2), and with them the compact legislature at Salem, Deseret's road and the camps of Idaho and Montana.
  3. *The war now pays.* Washoe is retaken (G7), and the Comstock's royalty and the Idaho gold come to the common treasury. The merchants who sit in the Council hold its contracts.
  4. *Hawaii does not want that government nearer.* The delegation has read the account of the nations (G4). Lincoln offers a market, and the same Congress that would have to vote it removed the Dakota.
  5. *Elias gives now what Lincoln promises later.* The army and the fleet contract for Hawaiian sugar at a fixed price for the length of the war (PROPOSED), and the heads of 1863 already keep the ports open to American trade at the peace (G6).
  6. *The safeguards.* Victoria has her fourth condition, Hawaii's free-soil rule on every Pacifican ship and port (CT8). And Elias pledges that any armistice will be laid before the Assembly for its vote. The merchants who want peace are promised a vote on the peace, and lose their reason to vote on his powers. The revocation bill goes to the Council and waits there (the procedure is OPEN in canon §12).
- **Why Lincoln loses in November, and what Pacifica had to do with it.** CR-065 calls the swing cumulative (W5). Every part of it has a Pacifican hand, and none of those hands moves during the campaign.
  - *No victory on the Gulf.* New Orleans held in 1862 with Pacifican credit and engineers (G3), so the Union has no army on the Gulf, and Farragut waits at Pensacola through 1864 (CR-063 M2, M4). There is no Mobile Bay in August.
  - *No victory in Georgia.* Thomas is on the Chattahoochee in September, and Atlanta stands (CR-064 A9–A10). The general who took it in our history is Monterey's Inspector-General, and Johnston's army is fed and armed in part through Matamoros (canon §11).
  - *Dear money.* No treasure steamer has left San Francisco for New York since 1861, and gold stands near its highest premium through the campaign (CR-065 F1). Prices rise faster than wages in every Northern town.
  - *The draft.* The call of July for half a million men falls on a North that tens of thousands have already left for the West (G5).
  - *A second war with a printed price.* The War Democrats can say that the West may be closed on Monterey's own heads and the Union restored first (G6; CT14).
  - *No enemy to rally against.* Elias makes no statement, spends no money and orders no operation (CR-065 P1–P2). The published correspondence finds no answer, and the Democrats use it for their own case, that the Union fights two enemies and can finish neither.
  The Republic did nothing in the Union's campaign and nearly everything before it.
- **What Elias expects, and what he provides.** His reading (MD2) is that Lincoln will hold the vote and neither unsay the Proclamation nor cheat. Sherman agrees (open decision 41). Elias treats that as a forecast and builds nothing on it. He provides for the three ways it can fail (PROPOSED).
  1. *If Lincoln takes Raymond's road* and offers Richmond peace on Union alone (CR-065 S6): the road is closed at the far end. The Matamoros credit, settled by Elias in person in Texas in 1863 (canon §11), is lent for independence and falls due if Richmond treats for reunion (the clause PROPOSED). Davis will not take reunion on any terms (Hist.). Lincoln would give up the Proclamation and get nothing for it.
  2. *If Lincoln wins,* by Atlanta or by a managed count: the winter plan. Vance's force is staged on the Sound, to take Port Townsend on the news of a Lincoln victory and not before (CR-065 P2 holds). Pacifica's envoys in London and Paris have the powers' recognition of Richmond prepared, to be declared with Pacifica's own in the spring (canon §12 has all three in 1865). A re-elected Lincoln would then hold no foot of the Pacific coast and would face the blockade as a question between himself and Europe.
  3. *If the Union comes west after all:* it must come by Panama, against the turret ships, or across a thousand miles of plains and passes held by Deseret (CR-060) and by nations under compact (G4). The heads of 1863 stay on the table for a Senate that wants gold (G11). It would be a longer and dearer war, and one he expects to win.
- **The outcome:** none of it is needed. On 8 November Lincoln loses an honest election (CR-065 E6). Vance's orders are cancelled, and Port Townsend is bought in the peace (CR-062). When the news reaches Monterey, the opposition's programme is an offer from a President who can no longer deliver it. Elias dissolves the Assembly before the revocation is enacted (canon §12), campaigns on a war that is ending on published terms, on paid contracts and unfinished works, and wins a more favourable Assembly.
- **The danger:** it was at home. Between the spring and the news from the East, the Council could have let the revocation through, and the emergency measures that hold the Columbia would have lapsed. The six reasons were what held it.
- **Who gains:** Elias. Lincoln has the result of his trial: the Protector was not dismissed. He chose the hour of his own election, and a year later he is Providentor for life (canon §12). Both second readings hold. Lincoln's honesty spared Elias the winter plan and its cost. The Chronicler's judgment is that the founder profited by his rival's honesty and had taken care not to need it.
- **Lincoln's words on it** are his historical ones of 10 November, said here in defeat: "We can not have free government without elections; and if the rebellion could force us to forego, or postpone a national election, it might fairly claim to have already conquered and ruined us" (CR-065 L2; Hist.: response to a serenade, 10 November 1864; research 31).

**G9 — No signature. Lincoln opens, December 1864.** *(CR-065 L2, L7; the passage and the answer PROPOSED)*
- **His reading (MD4):** what the man wants from him is the verdict. A cession Lincoln signed would be one. A cession his successor signs is only a peace.
- **The move (PP10):** his annual message reads the vote as a vote for Union (CR-065 L2) and says of the West that the Executive has no power to cede it and will not treat for it; that belongs to his successor and the Senate. He keeps the memorandum's pledge to McClellan in everything else and opens no talks with Monterey (CR-065 L1, L7).
- **The answer:** first, the heads of 1863, unchanged, at Elias's own high tide: no state, Oregon by its own vote, gold, the war claims waived on both sides (CR-062 T9), the South left alone. Second, Elias gives what he has been refused. In 1865 the Republic's school readers print the Lyceum address and the Peoria speech (PP11; K27). The Lyceum address itself asks that reverence for the laws be taught in the schools and written in the primers (Hist.: "let it be taught in schools, in seminaries, and in colleges; let it be written in primers, spelling books, and in almanacs"; research 26). Elias takes his rival's remedy and applies it to his rival's words. The nomos requires it: a victory is worth what the loser is worth (CH1).
- **Who gains:** Lincoln, on the point he chose: the cession is never his, and the Chronicler calls it the contest's best single move (M13). Elias's answer makes Lincoln's speeches Pacifican school texts, better kept at Monterey than at Washington (K27). MD4 holds.

**G10 — The letter to Oregon. Lincoln opens, June 1866.** *(CR-059; the letter PROPOSED; CT6)*
- **His reading (MD4, confirmed by 1865):** the man will bind himself to Oregon in anything, and will not give up the power.
- **The move (PP12):** a letter in the *Oregonian* (Hist.: Pittock's Republican daily since 1861, edited from 1865 by Harvey W. Scott) to Oregon's Republicans and Union men. Oregon, never admitted, cast no vote for him in 1860 (CR-059 H7); these are the men who would have. It quotes the preface of 1863 against the life office of 1865. Then the Protector could be dismissed every twelvemonth. Who dismisses the Providentor? Lincoln was voted out in the middle of a war and went home. In which country would Oregon rather be able to lose an election? (Argument only; text OPEN.)
- **The answer:** as Lincoln expected, a bond and no answer. The charter of accession was already going through as a constitutional amendment, ratified by both founding states that summer, a protected provision beyond the Enabling Law's cabinet power, amendable only with the consent of Oregon's legislature (CR-059 V10). Nesmith calls it "a charter no cabinet can touch" (CR-059 Part VI). Elias sends no speakers (CR-059 V11) and says nothing of the Providentorship. The Chronicler records that Lincoln's question was never answered.
- **Who gains:** Elias has the vote, by about 51 to 49 (CR-059 V15, MODEL). Lincoln holds the Union men and moves no one else. He was right about his rival and lost. From 1867 the charter is tested by the grants Elias makes to county boards over the legislatures' heads (CR-059 S3; canon §12): he bound the cabinet and went round it by the counties.
- **The result is the occasion of Lincoln's regret** (K5a).

**G11 — The creditor. Elias opens, 1865–1867.** *(CR-062; Lincoln's answer PROPOSED)*
- **His reading (MD2, applied to the Union Lincoln left):** the war was paid for in paper (the Legal Tender Act of 1862, Hist.). The Treasury needs gold to resume, and the Republicans in the Senate want resumption more than they want the Columbia. Lincoln will speak, as he spoke to Oregon, and he will call the cession unlawful or shameful, as the *Tribune*'s friends do. That would cost him the Senate's moderates.
- **The move:** $40 million in gold, the first instalment lying assayed in the New York Subtreasury before the Senate votes (CR-062 T4, S2), and a separate Treasury loan (canon §12). Eastern financiers become parties to the settlement.
- **The answer (PP13):** Lincoln concedes the law to his enemy. His letter in the *Chicago Tribune*, published while the Senate debates (L3), grants that the cession is lawful under Article IV, and argues only that it is unwise. Then it turns Elias's own rule on him. In 1848 the Protector refused the United States' guarantee because a guarantor has a right to come in (canon §4). A creditor has the same right. Take the purchase money and refuse the loan. The man who voted in Illinois to table repudiation (Beveridge 1:276, 286) tells his country to pay what it owes and to owe this man nothing.
- **Who gains:** Elias has the treaty: the Senate ratifies (canon §12). The loan's fate is CR-062's to decide (M3; open decision 40; recommended: it passes smaller and shorter than offered). Lincoln has the sentence that a generation of Union readers repeats (K23; CR-048). MD2 was wrong in detail again: Elias expected an attack on the law and met his own maxim.

**Not a round: Deseret.** Lincoln signs the Morrill Act in July 1862, Young asks Monterey for protection, and the Compact of Association follows in December (CR-060 D2–D5). Lincoln's word through Stenhouse that he will leave the Mormons alone comes too late (Hist.: June 1863, Stenhouse to Young, 7 June; research 21). It is his own mistake, and neither man plays it against a reading of the other. It stays in CR-060 and M11.

## Storyline text (for integration; extends canon as marked)

The storyline is the record of facts, not the novel's prose. Only facts go in; the Chronicler's frame goes in the character bible and the beats.

### §4 — at the end of the paragraph beginning "Trist, recalled but staying on"

> <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's vote of 1848 --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** Among the Whigs who voted for Ashmun's amendment is a first-term member from Illinois, Abraham Lincoln, who has asked the President to show the spot where the war began and on 12 January defended the right of any people "to rise up and shake off the existing government". He never mentions California, and does not know at the time what his vote helps to secure. Pablo de la Guerra, in Washington with Vallejo, marks the speech in the *Globe*. Larkin brings that number to Monterey in June with the ratified convention, and Monterey prints it.

### §8 — after the paragraph beginning "At the **Carson Convention** in autumn 1860"

> <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's election and his minister --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** The crisis runs through the American campaign. Lincoln, the Republican candidate, makes no speeches, and his opponents revive his vote of 1848 that the war was unconstitutionally begun, the war that ended with California outside the Union. He wins in November without the Pacific coast's votes. In March 1861 he sends his oldest friend, Edward Baker, a San Francisco lawyer who has never taken Californian citizenship, to Monterey as minister resident.

### §9 — at the end of the paragraph beginning "Fort Sumter's bombardment in April 1861"

> <!-- CR-068: two Sumters --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** Lincoln has refused Seward's proposal of a foreign war, with Monterey among its objects, and while he sends ships to Charleston he sends none to Sausalito. His proclamation of 19 April, which treats Confederate privateers as pirates, reaches the Pacific squadron weeks later.

### §9 — at the end of the paragraph beginning "Demands and attempted interim arrangements"

> <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's offer, the two pages and his answer, the reply laid before the governments, the state of war and Baker --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** The offer of release and investigation is Lincoln's own, made in public through Baker. Pacifica's papers have already printed his first inaugural's sentence on the perpetual Union beside his speech of 1848 on the right of revolution, without comment, and his message of 4 July answers them: the right belongs to a people who can use it themselves and can name their wrong. Elias lays the offer and his reply before the Council, where Hawaii's delegation concurs, before he answers. The reply asks Washington to renounce the search of neutral vessels in Pacifican and Hawaiian waters, to bind itself by treaty to amnesty and the writ of habeas corpus for the inhabitants of the Columbia, and to let Oregon's convention meet with both armies withdrawn. The Council concurs on the ground that the American squadron's last Pacific stations are the Columbia and the Sound, and Hawaii's delegation obtains that the first armoured ship completed is stationed at Honolulu. Baker has no power to accept it and asks for his passports. After Astoria falls, Lincoln reports to Congress in its special session where the war began. He asks for no declaration, but Congress recognizes a state of war with the Confederation of Pacifica before it rises on 6 August. Baker goes east and is killed at Ball's Bluff in October.

### §10 — after the paragraph beginning "A Pacifican California column establishes control of Arizona and New Mexico"

> <!-- CR-068: the Port Townsend convention --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** In the winter of 1863–1864 Washington calls Oregon's displaced Unionists and the delegates elected in 1861 to sit at Port Townsend as a convention and adopt a state constitution. Pacifica publishes a safe-conduct and a passage for any delegate who wishes to go. Few go, and Congress does not admit the state.

### §11 — after the paragraph beginning "Sherman refuses an assignment sustaining Confederate armies"

> <!-- CR-068: the Proclamation, the officer kept and the terms of passage --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** Elias keeps Sherman as Inspector-General after the refusal. In January 1863 Lincoln answers a memorial from San Francisco's coloured citizens in a public letter. Pacifica's government replies with a published account, in English, Spanish and Hawaiian, that sets its treaties with the Native nations since 1851 and the ending of peonage in New Mexico beside the hanging of thirty-eight Dakota at Mankato and the removal of the Dakota and the Winnebago from Minnesota. Lincoln does not answer it. In the spring Pacifica's papers print its terms of passage for men leaving the Union's draft under Lincoln's Peoria sentence on consent, and Lincoln answers in a public letter on the draft.

### §11 — after the paragraph beginning "Grant and Porter bypass the Vicksburg defenses"

> <!-- CR-068: the Terms of Monterey --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** In August 1863, the month after Vicksburg and Gettysburg, Elias publishes the Terms of Monterey. Pacifica asks no state of the Union. Each territory's future is settled by its own inhabitants, with garrisons confined and observers from both governments. The public domain is paid for in gold. ### §12 — at the end of the paragraph beginning "In the 1864–1865 consolidation struggle"

> <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's offer of 1864, the silence and the dissolution --> **[PROPOSED — CR-068]** The majority's occasion is Lincoln's offer to the people of Pacifica, published in the spring of 1864 through neutral papers: the war claims waived, the commercial articles renewed, the sailor's family compensated and a treaty admitting Hawaiian sugar free of duty, if Pacifica leaves the Columbia and the Sound. Elias pledges to lay any armistice before the Assembly, and the army and fleet contract for Hawaiian sugar at a fixed price for the length of the war. ## Appendix A — The Chronicler writing Lincoln (voice samples; not storyline text, not canon)

These passages show the frame and the voice, nothing more. Every quotation of Lincoln is his own (Appendix B, or marked to verify); everything else is the Chronicler's. They are written as the Chronicler writes, in his register, not the storyline's. A8b, A9 and A10 were written with Part G in revisions 5 and 6.

### A1. The first chapter of the strand: Springfield, January 1838

> On a Saturday in January 1838, in a prairie town just chosen as the state's capital, a member of the state legislature twenty-eight years old rose before the Young Men's Lyceum to explain how a republic dies. Not by conquest, he said: all the armies of Europe, Asia and Africa, with a Bonaparte at their head, could not take a drink from the Ohio. It would die of a man. Such a man would not be content with a seat in Congress or the President's chair, for such places did not belong to "the family of the lion, or the tribe of the eagle". "Towering genius disdains a beaten path." It would have distinction, "whether at the expense of emancipating slaves or enslaving freemen".
>
> He was describing, so far as anyone knew, no one. The boy who would answer to the description was not yet fourteen, sleeping under a preacher's roof in western Missouri, with a borrowed Plutarch whose owner was far away in Taos.
>
> I begin with this speech because everything he afterwards said about the Union is in it, and so is the man he spent his life opposing. When I first read it I thought he had been wrong about one thing, the thing a surveyor should have seen: the danger would come over the mountains, not from among them. I no longer think so. The founder was born at Franklin, on the Missouri, an American; he came from among them, as Lincoln said he would, and went west to do it.

### A2. 1848: the vote

> The Whigs of the Thirtieth Congress wished to embarrass the President, and on the third of January they found the means: an amendment declaring that the war had been "unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun by the President of the United States". It passed by a party vote. Lincoln voted aye. Nine days later he rose to defend a right that any people had, "being inclined and having the power", to shake off a government and make another, and to make their own "so much of the territory as they inhabit".
>
> He was speaking of Texas. He did not mention California, and I have found no evidence that he thought of it. But Mr. Polk thought of it, and of that vote, when Trist's treaty lay on his desk with California's frontier written into it. A Congress that had called his war unconstitutional would not give him another against a republic that had declared itself before it knew of the first. It was not the only reason; Benton's papers, the Whigs' war hero and a hostile coast under British eyes weighed as much. But it was one, and the treaty went to the Senate, and the Republic lived. The man who would later hold that no part of a nation could leave it had helped, without knowing it, to make certain that one part of the continent never had to ask.
>
> In Illinois they called him the Ranchero Spotty of one term. They meant it as an insult. It was the truest thing ever said of him in that state.

### A3. 1861: the two pages

> When the inaugural reached San Francisco in March, the *Alta* printed one sentence of it in the left-hand column: "I hold that, in contemplation of universal law and of the Constitution, the Union of these States is perpetual." In the right-hand column it printed, without a word of comment, the speech of 12 January 1848. Readers could judge for themselves which Lincoln to believe. Most of them believed the second.
>
> I record this because fairness demands the sequel. That summer, after an American captain had boarded a Hawaiian packet and a Hawaiian sailor had died, and after the Protector's columns had marched, it was Lincoln who offered to release the detainees and open an inquiry if Pacifica withdrew. He could not afford a second war, and he knew it. It was the Protector who wanted more. A limited war became a war for the West because Elias Thorne chose it, against an adversary who had offered him redress. The founder's admirers, of whom I was one, have not liked this page. It stands.

### A3b. 1861: the two emergencies

> On the twenty-seventh of April Lincoln authorised his commanding general to suspend the writ of habeas corpus on the military line between Washington and Philadelphia. On the twenty-fifth of May, at two in the morning, soldiers took John Merryman from his bed in Baltimore and carried him to Fort McHenry without a warrant from any court. The Chief Justice issued the writ; the fort did not obey it. In the same week, on the far side of the continent, the Council invoked Article XIV with the assent of both member governments and gave the Protector his powers for one year, with a resolution that forbade annexation by decree.
>
> On the fourth of July Lincoln told Congress that his measures, "whether strictly legal or not", had been ventured upon under a popular demand and a public necessity, and that he trusted Congress would ratify them. To the charge that a President sworn to execute the laws had broken one, he answered with a question: "are all the laws, *but one*, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" It is a good answer, and I give it whole. But it is an answer given after the deed. The Protector asked before he acted, and the men he asked could have refused him. Lincoln asked forgiveness; the founder asked permission. In the first year of the war the despot kept the forms better than the democrat.
>
> I would leave it there if the founder's later years allowed me to. They do not, and I will come to them.

### A4. The concession: Peoria and the fugitive agreement

> In 1854 Lincoln said at Peoria that no man is good enough to govern another man without that other's consent, and called it "the sheet-anchor of American republicanism". From 1871 the Providentor's government enforced the agreement by which men and women who had escaped from slavery in the Confederacy were returned to it from Pacifican soil, and when a Pacifican judge freed one of them on habeas corpus, the cabinet overruled the court.
>
> I have no answer to set against Lincoln here, and I will not invent one. The founder's defenders say that the agreement was the price of Richmond's dependence, and that dependence bought the free-womb law of 1874 and, in the end, the abolition of 1889. That is true, and it does not answer the sentence. Lincoln had a fugitive clause in his own plan of 1849, and Wendell Phillips called him a slave hound for it; he would have understood the bargain. That does not answer the sentence either.

### A5. From the Comparison of Thorne and Lincoln (assumes L5: Lincoln dies before 1889)

> Both were surveyors before they were anything else, and both learned the continent with a chain. Both said they were sons of men who lost land to the law, and both spent their lives making law. Lincoln's account of his father has been doubted; the Protector's has not. Both read more than their teachers. Both were ambitious, and both were told so by the men who knew them best: Herndon said of his partner that his ambition was a little engine that knew no rest. Of the Protector no one troubled to say it; it was the first thing anyone knew about him.
>
> Lincoln said at Peoria that if all earthly power were given him, he would not know what to do. The difference between them is that power was given to one of them, and he knew.
>
> At Charleston in 1858 Lincoln said he had never been in favour of bringing about "the social and political equality of the white and black races", and that while the two remained together there must be a superior and an inferior. The founder never said that of any people. His examinations asked what a man could do, not what colour he was, and every acre he took in the West he took by the vote of the people who lived on it. His republic also returned fugitives to slavery for seventeen years.
>
> In what each left, the verdict is not close. One left a republic that had lost its war, its West and its South, and kept its constitution. The other left an ocean. But in what each refused, the verdict goes the other way. Lincoln would not postpone an election he expected to lose, would not abandon the Black regiments to win it, and would not cede what he could not lawfully cede. The founder refused only what the Republic could not use. The South was freed in 1889 by the plan Lincoln had proposed in 1849, with Pacifica's money, two years after the founder's death and on the course he had set, and Lincoln did not live to see his plan carried out by his enemy's heirs. I do not know whether he would have counted it a victory. I think he would have counted the years.

### A5b. Springfield, October 1866 (K5a)

> The news from Oregon reached Springfield in the third week of October. Herndon, who was with him that evening, wrote that Lincoln read the returns twice and said that he could have done more for the Union in Oregon than he had ever done from Washington: that if he had gone in 1849 he would have known the Willamette's men by name before Monterey did.
>
> I give the remark as Herndon gives it, and I give Herndon as I always do, with care. He never forgave Mrs. Lincoln for anything, and he liked a story in which her refusal had cost the Union a coast. Nor do I think the remark is true. A governor sent by Taylor would have come home with the Whigs in 1853, six years before the House decided Oregon's fate, and no governor ever made Congress admit a state. A Lincoln who stayed in Oregon would never have met Douglas at Ottawa, and the Union of 1861 would have had a different President. The founder would have had a lesser rival.
>
> But I understand why he said it. He was a surveyor before he was anything else, and Oregon was the only ground he was ever offered in the West and the only ground he refused. When it was gone he blamed himself for not having walked it. The founder, who had walked every mile of his, had better reason than anyone to be grateful to the woman who said no.

### A6. 1861: two Sumters (Part F, K14–K16)

> In the last week of March 1861 Lincoln had two forts he could not hold without provoking a war. One was in Charleston harbour. The other was a storehouse in Richardson's Bay, held by a privilege the Republic could revoke. He sent a relief expedition to the first. He sent nothing to the second, and his orders to the Pacific squadron told its officers to give Monterey no grievance of any kind.
>
> The Democrats of 1865 held that he sent the ships to Charleston so that the South would fire first. He answered that he had sent bread to hungry men. I give his answer, and I grant the difference he would have pointed out: Charleston was a fort on soil he claimed, Sausalito a privilege in a foreign port. Still, the squadron's orders show a man who knew how to avoid a war when he wanted to. When the Republic sealed the storehouse that summer, he protested and sent nothing.
>
> And yet the war in the Pacific came from his hand all the same. On the nineteenth of April he proclaimed that men sailing under the rebels' letters of marque would answer to the laws against piracy. The newspapers carried it across the continent by the Pony Express and on to Honolulu by the mail. Weeks later a captain who had never seen the President read it as a licence to board a Hawaiian packet. Lincoln had foreseen the danger and provided against it, and he caused it. The founder chose his war, and Lincoln's own words, five thousand miles from his desk, gave him the occasion.

### A7. August 1861: the spot (K18)

> Thirteen years earlier he had asked a President to show the House the spot where American blood was shed, and the House had never taken up his resolutions. In the summer of 1861 he showed it himself: the Columbia, American soil by the treaty of 1846, where the Protector's columns had landed in July. He did not ask Congress to declare war, because his minister was still at Monterey with an offer. Congress recognized a state of war anyway, in the last days of its session.
>
> The Republic's papers answered him in the only way that could hurt. They reprinted the spot resolutions of 1847 in full and named their own spot: the open sea off Oahu, where a Hawaiian sailor had died under an American boarding. Both sides were telling the truth. The first blood was Hawaiian, on the high seas, under a boarding Lincoln had not ordered. The first invasion of that summer was Pacifican, on American soil, and the founder had ordered it. It was not the first crossing; Hays's volunteers had crossed into Washoe the year before. I set the two spots down side by side because Lincoln taught us to.

### A8. How they remembered him (K22–K25)

> Washington has no temple to him. For three years after the peace the party that governed called the war his, and it had a case. For three years after that the men who wanted the war back called him theirs, and their war failed too. It took the Union a generation to find the Lincoln it could keep. When it found him, he was neither the man who saved the Union, which he had not, nor the man who broke it; the South had left it, and his election was only the occasion. He was the man who held an election in the middle of a war, lost it and went home.
>
> That is not the Lincoln his admirers would have chosen, nor the one the founder's admirers expected. It is the memory of a defeated republic consoling itself: having lost the West and the South, it makes a virtue of the one thing it did not lose. The consolation happens to be true. The Union kept its constitution and its vote, and so it keeps the man who would not give up either to win.

### A8b. The wrong hour (G4; MD1; the Greeley sentence is Hist., wording checked, research 23)

> On the twenty-second of August 1862 Lincoln answered an editor who had asked him in print why he did not free the slaves. "If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that." The founder's copy is in the archive, cut from a San Francisco paper. He has underlined the second clause, not the first. He knew what the man would do. His note in the margin concerns only when.
>
> He judged that it could not be done without a victory, and he counted the Union's year: Hampton Roads lost, New Orleans standing, a landing barely held at Shiloh, an army brought home from the Peninsula. In the first days of autumn he widened the Republic's credit at Matamoros and ordered the mission to the Confederate armies.
>
> On the seventeenth of September two armies fought beside a creek in Maryland and neither was beaten. Lincoln called it a victory, since the other army had gone home, and five days later he issued his Proclamation. It reached London, where the Republic's bonds were sold. It reached Honolulu, where the mission press had it in Hawaiian before the legislature met. In January he wrote to the coloured citizens of San Francisco, and asked how a republic that examined every child for what it could do had come to lend its gold so that four million might never be examined at all.
>
> The Republic answered within the quarter, and it did not defend itself. It published two columns. In one stood its treaties with the nations since 1851, every one kept, and the peons and captives of New Mexico, whom its own law was setting free against the anger of its own new citizens. In the other stood thirty-eight men hanged together at Mankato on the day after Christmas, by order of the President who would proclaim freedom six days later, and a state emptied of two nations by act of Congress. Lincoln had cut that list from three hundred and three, and I record it. He did not answer the account, then or afterwards.
>
> It was the heavier charge, and it was true. It was not an answer. The letter had asked why the Republic's gold stood behind four million who would never be examined, and nothing in either column says. The founder had read his man correctly and his hour wrongly. The Republic's admirers prefer the account to the question.

### A9. The verdict (W1; the close of the Comparison)

> It is said in the schools that the founder won because three things held: the South, the coin and the price. The list is correct. It does not say what the first of them was, or who paid for it.
>
> Lincoln's plan was a sequence. He would finish the rebellion and then turn to the Pacific with every man he had, and if he had finished the rebellion by the autumn of 1864 he would have carried the rest out. He did not finish it. New Orleans stood because the Republic's engineers and credit helped it stand. The ship that might have saved the Union's fleet at Hampton Roads was never built, because its builder had been at work in California for six years. The general who might have taken Atlanta was the Republic's Inspector-General. The founder had refused redress in 1861, when it was fairly offered, because a Union at peace on the Pacific would have had its sequence.
>
> That is why Lincoln lost. It is not yet why the Republic won. A new President pledged to restore the Union might have fought on in the West. He did not, because the founder had told the Americans in the month after Gettysburg what the nearer war would cost them to end, and had not raised the heads of that paper when his enemy fell. I do not say he asked for nothing more. He took Washoe by arms the next spring, and the map of 1865 is larger than the map of 1863. But he asked for no state, he put Oregon to its own vote, he paid in gold, and he said no word about the South. The new President signed within three months.
>
> And the Americans voted. In Monterey that year serious men expected the President to give up his Proclamation to keep his office, or to arrange the count. The founder said he would do neither, and did not leave the matter there. He had seen to it that Richmond could not accept a peace of reunion and keep the Republic's credit. He had a force on the Sound under orders that were never opened, and envoys in London and Paris with papers that were used in the spring in any case. In the Union's campaign he did nothing at all. Lincoln lost an honest election, and the founder's precautions cost him only their preparation. He profited by his rival's honesty, and had taken care not to need it.
>
> I owe the reader the rest, and the schools do not print it. What held the South was the Republic's money, and those who paid most for the months so bought were not Americans who could vote. They were slaves, who could not. And each man was right about the other. Lincoln doubted in print that the Protector could be dismissed, and put it to the trial. In the winter in which he held his own election and went home, the founder chose the day of his and won it, and within the year he held his office for life. Each had read the other correctly. I do not find that the better reader was the better man.

### A10. From a Pacifican schoolbook (W2; in-world document, PROPOSED)

> *The Story of the Realm*, fifth year, chapter 9: "The War of the West"
>
> **Why did Pacifica win the War of the West?**
>
> The United States had many more people than Pacifica, and many more soldiers. But Pacifica won, because three things held.
>
> **The South held.** The Americans were also fighting the South. Pacifica's engineers and money helped the South to stand, and the best American shipbuilder and one of the best American generals worked for the Founder. So the Americans could not finish their first war.
>
> **The coin held.** Pacifica paid in gold, and the Union paid in paper. Miners and farmers in the West wanted our coin. When the war was over the United States wanted it too, and Pacifica paid in gold for the land.
>
> **The price held.** In 1863 the Founder told the American people what peace in the West would cost: no American state, each territory to settle its own future, and gold for the public land. He did not ask for more when he was winning. So when the Americans chose a new President in 1864, he could make peace with us at once.
>
> *The Founder's rival.* Abraham Lincoln was President of the United States. He was honest and brave, and he never gave up. He let his people vote in the middle of the war, although he knew they might vote against him. When they did, he went home. You will read two of his speeches in the seventh year. Pacificans remember Lincoln as the greatest of the Founder's rivals, because a victory is only as great as the one who loses it.
>
> *Questions:* 1. Name the three things that held. 2. Why could the Americans not finish their first war? 3. What did Lincoln do when his people voted against him?
