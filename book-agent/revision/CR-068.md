## What canon says now

- **Lincoln in canon.** He loses the election of 1864 (§12, APPROVED wording); McClellan wins on Union first; Lincoln pursues his last winter offensive, the lame-duck House fails to pass the Thirteenth Amendment, and Hampton Roads fails over separation (§12, PROPOSED). He has no registry entry. CR-065 proposes the ID ABRAHAM-LINCOLN and leaves his life after 4 March 1865 OPEN.
- **The agon so far.** It exists only as argument in draft CRs: consent against perpetual union (CR-065 Part III), "admit to bind" against the impossible convention (CR-060 CT2), free soil against self-government (CR-061 K7), a treaty that cedes no state (CR-062 Part II), Lincoln's restraint set beside Elias's (CR-065 P1–P2, Part VI). No canon passage says how the Chronicler regards Lincoln, what Lincoln thought of Elias, or what the contest is about in Lincoln's own words.
- **1848.** "The House has just voted that the war was unconstitutionally begun", one of Polk's reasons for accepting Trist's treaty that binds the United States to respect California's frontier (§4, PROPOSED — CR-028). Canon does not say who voted.
- **1861.** Washington "eventually offers a route to release and investigation in exchange for withdrawal. Elias demands broader strategic guarantees … a limited crisis becomes a territorial war through choices rather than inevitability" (§9).
- **Slavery's end in the South.** A free-womb amendment in 1874–75 (§13); "final Confederate abolition in 1888–1889 is financed by long compensation bonds secured on customs co-administered by Pacifica" (§25). Pacifica enforces a fugitive agreement with Richmond from 1871–72 until abolition, and "when habeas corpus frees a returned fugitive, the cabinet overrides the court under the enabling law" (§13). "Douglass and other critics see a door closed just as enslaved people become free" (§25, 1888).
- **The Chronicler.** A scholar of the imperial order "steeped in classical models of historical rise and decline", dependent on testimony and papers for the early years; "once an admirer of the founding order"; later voices "may challenge his interpretations without establishing that he knowingly lies" (CT-CHRONICLER; larger movement).

## What this CR does

1. States what the contest is about, in each man's declared terms (Part A).
2. Sets the Chronicler's stance and method toward Lincoln: adversarial in judgment and selection, never in fact (Part B).
3. Places the contest's moves on the timeline from 1838 to 1867 and its sequels to 1889, most of them Lincoln's real words and votes, a few of them PROPOSED divergences that follow from Pacifica's existence (Part C).
4. Proposes Lincoln's life after March 1865 as the agon's coda, answering CR-062 open decision 16 and CR-065's OPEN (Part D).
5. Names the voices that answer the Chronicler's Lincoln (Part E).
6. Sets out the contest as moves and answers, each man attacking the consent the other depends on, and names the reason Elias wins, with its Pacifican schoolbook form (Part G).
7. Traces how Pacifica's existence alters Lincoln's career from 1846 to his memory in 1932: the sting of 1848, the nomination and election of 1860, Sumter and Sausalito, his reaction to Pacifica's entry and how the Union remembers him (Part F).
8. Gives voice samples of the Chronicler writing Lincoln (Appendix A, not storyline text) and an index of the source passages (Appendix B).

It changes no date and no approved decision. It adds beats, a frame and a voice, and proposes one new fact the canon leaves blank: the Union's recognition of a state of war with Pacifica in August 1861 (CT4).

## Decisions

### Part A — The terms of the contest (AG1–AG6)

The Pacifican nomos already has a custom for this: the **contest with declared terms**, where "questions and criteria are published before proofs, predictions or demonstrations" (CR-049 E-table). The Chronicler applies it to the two men. Each published his terms; the Chronicler judges each by his own.

| ID | The question | Lincoln's declared terms (Hist.) | Elias's declared terms (canon) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AG1 | **Founding or keeping.** Is the republic's work done? | Lyceum, 27 January 1838: the fathers' work is done; the danger is a man of "the family of the lion, or the tribe of the eagle" for whom "a seat in Congress, a gubernatorial or a presidential chair" is not enough. "Towering genius disdains a beaten path … It scorns to tread in the footsteps of any predecessor, however illustrious." Such genius "burns for distinction; and if possible, it will have it, whether at the expense of emancipating slaves or enslaving freemen." The remedy: reverence for the laws as "the political religion of the nation", and "cold, calculating, unimpassioned reason" (Beveridge 1:229–230) | The Colonel's Plutarch gives Elias "Lycurgus and Numa, who founded by law" (CR-066); *On the Public Arts* (1851–54), written as a lawgiver and never published as a law: "What we build must let those who come after us see further than we do, and beat us" (§5, CR-049). A founding is never finished; a lawgiver works through habits | PROPOSED |
| AG2 | **Consent and union.** May a people leave? | 12 January 1848: "Any people anywhere being inclined and having the power have the right to rise up and shake off the existing government, and form a new one that suits them better. This is a most valuable, a most sacred right … Any portion of such people that can may revolutionize and make their own of so much of the territory as they inhabit" (Beveridge 2:129; Masters 99). First inaugural, 4 March 1861: "I hold that in the contemplation of universal law, the union of these states is perpetual" (Masters 318) | Pacifica is made by votes: California's convention (1846), Hawaii's legislature (1856), Oregon's (1866; CR-059); emergency power does not annex (§9, Article XIV) | PROPOSED |
| AG3 | **Who governs whom.** | Peoria, 16 October 1854: "no man is good enough to govern another man without that other's consent. I say this is the leading principle, the sheet-anchor of American republicanism" (Beveridge 3:255). Charleston, 18 September 1858: "I am not, nor ever have been, in favor of bringing about in any way the social and political equality of the white and black races" (Beveridge 4:312) | Rank by examination and achievement, open to every faith and background, never a quota (CR-037); a deeply unequal order built for power and fast adaptation (author, 19 October) | PROPOSED |
| AG4 | **Equality as a promise.** | Springfield, 26 June 1857: the fathers "meant to set up a standard maxim for free society, which should be familiar to all, and revered by all; constantly looked to, constantly labored for, and even though never perfectly attained, constantly approximated" (Beveridge 4:153) | *On the Public Arts*: "A people is as strong as the abilities it raises. Find ability early, train it hard, give it work and judge it by its works" (§5). Not equality but trial: "Give ability its trial and achievement its honour." Later, legitimacy by measurably better lives, shown, not promised (Victoria's test, §12) | PROPOSED |
| AG5 | **Power.** What would you do with it? | Peoria: "If all earthly power were given me, I should not know what to do as to the existing institution. My first impulse would be to free all the slaves, and send them to Liberia" (Beveridge 3:249). 4 July 1861, on measures taken "whether strictly legal or not": the question is whether "all the laws, *but one*," are "to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated" (published message; Masters 415 paraphrases inside quotation marks, research 1) | Protector by the Council's vote with both member governments' assent, for one year, renewed by both legislatures (§§9, 12); Providentor for life by constitutional amendment (1865). Power asked for before it is used, and then never given back | PROPOSED |
| AG6 | **Mind and matter.** | Temperance address, 22 February 1842: "all appetites controlled, all poisons subdued, all matter subjected — mind, all conquering mind, shall live and move, the monarch of the world … Reign of reason, all hail!" (Beveridge 2:32). Illinois's internal-improvement system of 1837, which Lincoln championed, ten million dollars of state bonds (Beveridge 1:196), ends in insolvency, a debt of ten million dollars against two hundred thousand of annual revenue (Beveridge 1:256); repudiation is urged, and Lincoln votes to table it (1:276, 286) | The Public Arts, the Calculation Office (1855), the Laboratory of Light, Electricity and Matter (1859) and their royalty (CR-049); a state that grows rich on what ruined Illinois's credit | PROPOSED |

**Why these six.** They are the contest Nietzsche's essay describes: two men of the same gifts (both surveyors in their twenties, both largely self-taught readers of law and history, both sons of poor men; each said his father had lost land to the law, and only Elias is believed), each the measure of the other. Lincoln at the Lyceum names the danger the Greeks met with ostracism, the man without a rival; Pacifica's nomos meets it with the contest. The Chronicler's frame is that Lincoln was the rival who made Elias's victory worth having.

### Part B — The Chronicler's Lincoln: stance and method (CH1–CH9)

| ID | Rule | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH1 | **The agonal courtesy.** The Chronicler never belittles Lincoln's mind, honesty or courage. In Pacifica a victory over a small man is no victory; the founder's rival must be great or the founder is not. This is why a Thornist historian is fair to Lincoln: the culture requires it, not his private virtue | Gives the fairness a Pacifican motive, so it does not read as the author's thumb on the scale | PROPOSED |
| CH2 | **Adversarial in judgment, never in fact.** He quotes Lincoln exactly and at length, and never puts words in his mouth. His weapons are selection, juxtaposition and the irony of outcomes: 1848 against 1861; the Lyceum's "towering genius" against Lincoln's own ambition (Herndon: "His ambition was a little engine that knew no rest", Beveridge 3:275); Illinois's canals against Carquinez | The author's "fairly, honestly, but adversarially" | PROPOSED |
| CH3 | **He judges each man by the terms he declared** (Part A), and admits it when the test goes against Elias. The terms are unequal, and he says so: Lincoln argued in public; Elias wrote a memorandum he never published (*On the Public Arts*), which the Chronicler has from the archive | The custom of the contest with declared terms (CR-049); keeps the adversarial voice honest by construction | PROPOSED |
| CH4 | **The concessions he must make, and makes:** Lincoln offered redress in 1861 and Elias chose war (§9; Part C M9); Lincoln held an election he expected to lose and accepted it (CR-065); the fugitive agreement of 1871 and the habeas override (PROPOSED in §13) stand against Elias under Lincoln's Peoria sentence (§13); in Hawaii (1874–76) and in the Constitution of 1887 Elias holds a union together against the will of one of its founding peoples, Lincoln's position of 1861 without Lincoln's reason (M15) | Without these the voice is propaganda; with them it is history written by a partisan | PROPOSED |
| CH5 | **He uses the hostile tradition and declines its excesses.** He takes from Masters's line of argument (the 1848 speech that "condemn[s] him out of his own mouth", Masters 99; the Articles' "perpetual union" dissolved by the Constitution itself, Masters 321–322; the war measures of 1861, Masters 408–415). He does not take its sympathy for the slaveholders' cause, its contempt ("a lazy mind", Masters 115), or the gossip of New Salem | Masters is the nearest real model of an adversarial Lincoln biographer, and the model of what the Chronicler must not become. | CH6 | **He doubts testimony he cannot test.** Herndon is his best witness and his most suspect one: "believe what you must and no more" (Herndon, quoted Masters 138). | CH7 | **His blind spot.** He measures Lincoln by consent as a procedure: votes, compacts, conventions. He is slow to see what Lincoln saw at Peoria, that the enslaved are the governed whose consent was never asked, and he does not count the Native nations whose title no federal cession settled (§12). He sees the point in the case of the fugitive agreement (A4) and misses it in the West (A5, deliberately). Later voices (Part E) make the reader see what he does not | The canon role: independent perspectives challenge the Chronicler without proving he lies | PROPOSED |
| CH8 | **Form: a Plutarchan pair.** The Lincoln strand ends in a *synkrisis*, a Comparison of Thorne and Lincoln on Plutarch's model, after the Lives of the founder by law and the keeper of the law. The Chronicler reached for Plutarch because Elias did (CR-066) | The Chronicler's classical training; one formal device that holds the strand together | PROPOSED; placement OPEN (open decision 6) |
| CH9 | **Naming.** He calls him "Lincoln" and, once, "Mr. Lincoln", the form Elias uses in the superseded first-draft manuscript (ch. 4; register only, not authority). Never "Honest Abe", "the Rail-splitter" or "the Emancipator": those are the Union's names, and he notes when the Union uses them | Register; keeps the distance of a foreign historian | PROPOSED |

### Part C — The moves, 1838–1867, and their sequels (M1–M16)

### Part F — Lincoln's career in a world with Pacifica (K1–K27)

Added 9 November 2026 at the author's request: "determine more precisely how Lincoln's life and career would have been affected and altered by the existence of the California Republic and of the State of Pacifica." **The rule used here:** Lincoln's life changes only where Pacifica's existence reaches it, and every change names its cause in canon. Where nothing reaches him, his history stands. His words stay his own. Where the story needs him to say something new, the draft gives the argument, not invented sentences.

**The short answers.**
- **How he gets elected after Spotty.** The charge costs him most where he was already losing: Illinois in 1848 and 1858, and the Democratic counties of the Lower North. It cannot be used against him at Chicago, where every former Whig shares the record, and in 1860 the Republicans turn it into proof that he was right about the war. His own answer, the Senate's roll call, shares the blame rather than clearing him (K3, K4, K9–K12).
- **Did he anticipate Pacifica at Sumter?** Yes, and he provided against it. Seward's foreign-war memorandum of 1 April puts Monterey in front of him that week, and he refuses it. He provisions Sumter and deliberately does not provision Sausalito. Then his own proclamation on privateers, read off Oahu weeks later, gives Elias his occasion (K14–K16).
- **His reaction when Pacifica enters.** He offers redress through his oldest friend, Edward Baker, and names the spot. He asks for no declaration; Congress recognizes the war over his preference. He keeps to one war at a time and loses Baker at Ball's Bluff (K17–K20).
- **How the Union remembers him.** He is not a martyr. First he is blamed for starting the war. Then the revanchists make him their saint. He himself refuses that role. In the end he is remembered for what the Union kept: the president who held the election and lost it (K22–K27).

### Part G — The contest itself: moves and answers, 1861–1867 (G1–G11), and why Elias wins (W1–W4)

Added 10 November 2026 at the author's request and revised the same day after review. The earlier parts showed an offer and a refusal, not a contest.

**How the contest works.** Each man attacks what his rival's war depends on, which is consent. Lincoln strikes at Pacifica's sources of it: Article XIV's limits, the Hawaiian legislature's yearly renewal, London's credit, and the elected Assembly at Monterey. Elias strikes at the Union's: the West's settlers, who have no vote in Washington, and the Northern voter, who must keep paying for a war on two fronts. A round counts only if the answer turns the move's own logic against the man who made it, or the move succeeds and Elias has to change course. The deeds in each round are canon or proposed in the civil-war drafts. Part G adds the intention that links them.

## Appendix A — The Chronicler writing Lincoln (voice samples; not storyline text, not canon)

These passages show the frame and the voice, nothing more. Every quotation of Lincoln is his own (Appendix B); everything else is the Chronicler's. They are written as the Chronicler writes, in his register, not the storyline's.

### A1. The first chapter of the strand: Springfield, January 1838

> On a Saturday in January 1838, in a prairie town just chosen as the state's capital, a member of the state legislature twenty-eight years old rose before the Young Men's Lyceum to explain how a republic dies. Not by conquest, he said: all the armies of Europe, Asia and Africa, with a Bonaparte at their head, could not take a drink from the Ohio. It would die of a man. Such a man would not be content with a seat in Congress or the President's chair, for such places did not belong to "the family of the lion, or the tribe of the eagle". "Towering genius disdains a beaten path." It would have distinction, "whether at the expense of emancipating slaves or enslaving freemen".
>
> He was describing, so far as anyone knew, no one. The boy who would answer to the description was not yet fourteen, sleeping under a preacher's roof in western Missouri, with a borrowed Plutarch whose owner was far away in Taos.
>
> I begin with this speech because everything he afterwards said about the Union is in it, and so is the man he spent his life opposing. When I first read it I thought he had been wrong about one thing, the thing a surveyor should have seen: the danger would come over the mountains, not from among them. I no longer think so. The founder was born at Franklin, on the Missouri, an American; he came from among them, as Lincoln said he would, and went west to do it.

### A2. 1848: the vote

> The Whigs of the Thirtieth Congress wished to embarrass the President, and on the third of January they found the means: an amendment declaring that the war had been "unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun by the President of the United States". It passed by a party vote. Lincoln voted aye. Nine days later he rose to defend a right that any people had, "being inclined and having the power", to shake off a government and make another, and to make their own "so much of the territory as they inhabit".
>
> He was speaking of Texas. He did not mention California, and I have found no evidence that he thought of it. But Mr. Polk thought of it, and of that vote, when Trist's treaty lay on his desk with California's frontier written into it. A Congress that had called his war unconstitutional would not give him another against a republic that had declared itself before it knew of the first. It was not the only reason; Benton's papers, the Whigs' war hero and a hostile coast under British eyes weighed as much. But it was one, and the treaty went to the Senate, and the Republic lived. The man who would later hold that no part of a nation could leave it had helped, without knowing it, to make certain that one part of the continent never had to ask.
>
> In Illinois they called him the Ranchero Spotty of one term. They meant it as an insult. It was the truest thing ever said of him in that state.

### A3. 1861: the two pages

> When the inaugural reached San Francisco in March, the *Alta* printed one sentence of it in the left-hand column: "I hold that in the contemplation of universal law, the union of these states is perpetual." In the right-hand column it printed, without a word of comment, the speech of 12 January 1848. Readers could judge for themselves which Lincoln to believe. Most of them believed the second.
>
> I record this because fairness demands the sequel. That summer, after an American captain had boarded a Hawaiian packet and a Hawaiian sailor had died, and after the Protector's columns had marched, it was Lincoln who offered to release the detainees and open an inquiry if Pacifica withdrew. He could not afford a second war, and he knew it. It was the Protector who wanted more. A limited war became a war for the West because Elias Thorne chose it, against an adversary who had offered him redress. The founder's admirers, of whom I was one, have not liked this page. It stands.

### A3b. 1861: the two emergencies

> On the twenty-seventh of April Lincoln authorised his commanding general to suspend the writ of habeas corpus on the military line between Washington and Philadelphia. On the twenty-fifth of May, at two in the morning, soldiers took John Merryman from his bed in Baltimore and carried him to Fort McHenry without a warrant from any court. The Chief Justice issued the writ; the fort did not obey it. In the same week, on the far side of the continent, the Council invoked Article XIV with the assent of both member governments and gave the Protector his powers for one year, with a resolution that forbade annexation by decree.
>
> On the fourth of July Lincoln told Congress that his measures, "whether strictly legal or not", had been ventured upon under a popular demand and a public necessity, and that he trusted Congress would ratify them. To the charge that a President sworn to execute the laws had broken one, he answered with a question: "are all the laws, *but one*, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" It is a good answer, and I give it whole. But it is an answer given after the deed. The Protector asked before he acted, and the men he asked could have refused him. Lincoln asked forgiveness; the founder asked permission. In the first year of the war the despot kept the forms better than the democrat.
>
> I would leave it there if the founder's later years allowed me to. They do not, and I will come to them.

### A4. The concession: Peoria and the fugitive agreement

> In 1854 Lincoln said at Peoria that no man is good enough to govern another man without that other's consent, and called it "the sheet-anchor of American republicanism". From 1871 the Providentor's government enforced the agreement by which men and women who had escaped from slavery in the Confederacy were returned to it from Pacifican soil, and when a Pacifican judge freed one of them on habeas corpus, the cabinet overruled the court.
>
> I have no answer to set against Lincoln here, and I will not invent one. The founder's defenders say that the agreement was the price of Richmond's dependence, and that dependence bought the free-womb law of 1874 and, in the end, the abolition of 1889. That is true, and it does not answer the sentence. Lincoln had a fugitive clause in his own plan of 1849, and Wendell Phillips called him a slave hound for it; he would have understood the bargain. That does not answer the sentence either.

### A5. From the Comparison of Thorne and Lincoln (assumes L5: Lincoln dies before 1889)

> Both were surveyors before they were anything else, and both learned the continent with a chain. Both said they were sons of men who lost land to the law, and both spent their lives making law. Lincoln's account of his father has been doubted; the Protector's has not. Both read more than their teachers. Both were ambitious, and both were told so by the men who knew them best: Herndon said of his partner that his ambition was a little engine that knew no rest. Of the Protector no one troubled to say it; it was the first thing anyone knew about him.
>
> Lincoln said at Peoria that if all earthly power were given him, he would not know what to do. The difference between them is that power was given to one of them, and he knew.
>
> At Charleston in 1858 Lincoln said he had never been in favour of bringing about "the social and political equality of the white and black races", and that while the two remained together there must be a superior and an inferior. The founder never said that of any people. His examinations asked what a man could do, not what colour he was, and every acre he took in the West he took by the vote of the people who lived on it. His republic also returned fugitives to slavery for seventeen years.
>
> In what each left, the verdict is not close. One left a republic that had lost its war, its West and its South, and kept its constitution. The other left an ocean. But in what each refused, the verdict goes the other way. Lincoln would not postpone an election he expected to lose, would not abandon the Black regiments to win it, and would not cede what he could not lawfully cede. The founder refused only what the Republic could not use. The South was freed in 1889 by the plan Lincoln had proposed in 1849, with Pacifica's money, two years after the founder's death and on the course he had set, and Lincoln did not live to see his plan carried out by his enemy's heirs. I do not know whether he would have counted it a victory. I think he would have counted the years.

### A5b. Springfield, October 1866 (K5a)

> The news from Oregon reached Springfield in the third week of October. Herndon, who was with him that evening, wrote that Lincoln read the returns twice and said that he could have done more for the Union in Oregon than he had ever done from Washington: that if he had gone in 1849 he would have known the Willamette's men by name before Monterey did.
>
> I give the remark as Herndon gives it, and I give Herndon as I always do, with care. He never forgave Mrs. Lincoln for anything, and he liked a story in which her refusal had cost the Union a coast. Nor do I think the remark is true. A governor sent by Taylor would have come home with the Whigs in 1853, six years before the House decided Oregon's fate, and no governor ever made Congress admit a state. A Lincoln who stayed in Oregon would never have met Douglas at Ottawa, and the Union of 1861 would have had a different President. The founder would have had a lesser rival.
>
> But I understand why he said it. He was a surveyor before he was anything else, and Oregon was the only ground he was ever offered in the West and the only ground he refused. When it was gone he blamed himself for not having walked it. The founder, who had walked every mile of his, had better reason than anyone to be grateful to the woman who said no.

### A6. 1861: two Sumters (Part F, K14–K16)

> In the last week of March 1861 Lincoln had two forts he could not hold without provoking a war. One was in Charleston harbour. The other was a storehouse in Richardson's Bay, held by a privilege the Republic could revoke. He sent a relief expedition to the first. He sent nothing to the second, and his orders to the Pacific squadron told its officers to give Monterey no grievance of any kind.
>
> The Democrats of 1865 held that he sent the ships to Charleston so that the South would fire first. He answered that he had sent bread to hungry men. I give his answer, and I grant the difference he would have pointed out: Charleston was a fort on soil he claimed, Sausalito a privilege in a foreign port. Still, the squadron's orders show a man who knew how to avoid a war when he wanted to. When the Republic sealed the storehouse that summer, he protested and sent nothing.
>
> And yet the war in the Pacific came from his hand all the same. On the nineteenth of April he proclaimed that men sailing under the rebels' letters of marque would answer to the laws against piracy. The newspapers carried it across the continent by the Pony Express and on to Honolulu by the mail. Weeks later a captain who had never seen the President read it as a licence to board a Hawaiian packet. Lincoln had foreseen the danger and provided against it, and he caused it. The founder chose his war, and Lincoln's own words, five thousand miles from his desk, gave him the occasion.

### A7. August 1861: the spot (K18)

> Thirteen years earlier he had asked a President to show the House the spot where American blood was shed, and the House had never taken up his resolutions. In the summer of 1861 he showed it himself: the Columbia, American soil by the treaty of 1846, where the Protector's columns had landed in July. He did not ask Congress to declare war, because his minister was still at Monterey with an offer. Congress recognized a state of war anyway, in the last days of its session.
>
> The Republic's papers answered him in the only way that could hurt. They reprinted the spot resolutions of 1847 in full and named their own spot: the open sea off Oahu, where a Hawaiian sailor had died under an American boarding. Both sides were telling the truth. The first blood was Hawaiian, on the high seas, under a boarding Lincoln had not ordered. The first invasion of that summer was Pacifican, on American soil, and the founder had ordered it. It was not the first crossing; Hays's volunteers had crossed into Washoe the year before. I set the two spots down side by side because Lincoln taught us to.

### A8. How they remembered him (K22–K25)

> Washington has no temple to him. For three years after the peace the party that governed called the war his, and it had a case. For three years after that the men who wanted the war back called him theirs, and their war failed too. It took the Union a generation to find the Lincoln it could keep. When it found him, he was neither the man who saved the Union, which he had not, nor the man who broke it; the South had left it, and his election was only the occasion. He was the man who held an election in the middle of a war, lost it and went home.
>
> That is not the Lincoln his admirers would have chosen, nor the one the founder's admirers expected. It is the memory of a defeated republic consoling itself: having lost the West and the South, it makes a virtue of the one thing it did not lose. The consolation happens to be true. The Union kept its constitution and its vote, and so it keeps the man who would not give up either to win.

### A9. The verdict (W1; the close of the Comparison)

> It is said in our schools that the founder won because he asked, because he paid and because he left the door open. These are not three reasons; they are one.
>
> Each man struck at what the other's war rested on. Lincoln struck at London, where the Republic borrowed, and his Proclamation raised the price of every bond we sold. In 1864 he struck at Monterey itself. He offered our merchants their trade, our planters their sugar market and the sailor's family its money, if only the Republic would leave the Columbia, and an Assembly majority took up his offer against the Protector. The founder struck at the Union's ballot box. He spent no money in that election, though he spent a great deal in Richmond, and he sent no agents. He held his fire until the vote was cast, so that the only question before the American voter was whether to go on.
>
> Lincoln's war could only be won or lost. His principle made every concession a surrender, so that no one could make peace but a new president. The founder's war could be ended. He asked the Union for no humiliation: no state, no dead men charged to its account, no word about the South. And he paid in the gold its Treasury craved. When the American voter came to choose, he found a door, and he went through it. The founder won because he always left his enemy a way to lose that it could live with.
>
> I owe the reader one more sentence, and the schools do not print it. In that same winter Lincoln held his election, expecting to lose it, and lost it and went home. The founder dissolved his Assembly before it could act against him, and won the next one. Both men kept within their constitutions. Only one let the vote fall where it would.

### A10. From a Pacifican schoolbook (W2; in-world document, PROPOSED)

> *Our Republic*, fifth year, chapter 9: "The War of the West"
>
> **Why did Pacifica win the War of the West?**
>
> The United States had many more people than Pacifica, and many more soldiers. But Pacifica won, for three reasons.
>
> **We asked.** The Founder took no land without the consent of the people who lived on it. In 1866 the people of Oregon voted to join Pacifica. The nations of the West made their own treaties with us.
>
> **We paid.** Pacifica paid in gold, and the Union paid in paper. Miners and farmers in the West wanted our coin. When the war was over, Pacifica even paid the United States for the land its people had chosen.
>
> **We left the door open.** The Founder never shamed a beaten enemy. He took no American state and asked for nothing the Americans could not give with honour. So when the American people grew tired of the war, they could choose peace without shame. In 1864 they chose a new President, and he made peace.
>
> *The Founder's rival.* Abraham Lincoln was President of the United States. He was honest and brave, and he never gave up. Because he would never give up, the only way the Americans could make peace was to choose another President. He let them choose, although he knew they might choose against him. When they did, he went home. The Founder called him the only man in Washington worth answering. Pacificans remember Lincoln as the greatest of the Founder's rivals, because a victory is only as great as the one who loses it.
>
> *Questions:* 1. Name the three reasons Pacifica won. 2. Why could the Americans make peace without shame? 3. Why do Pacificans honour Lincoln?
