## What canon says now

- **Lincoln in canon.** He loses the election of 1864 (§12, APPROVED wording); McClellan wins on Union first; Lincoln pursues his last winter offensive, the lame-duck House fails to pass the Thirteenth Amendment, and Hampton Roads fails over separation (§12, PROPOSED). He has no registry entry. CR-065 proposes the ID ABRAHAM-LINCOLN and leaves his life after 4 March 1865 OPEN.
- **The agon so far.** It exists only as argument in draft CRs: consent against perpetual union (CR-065 Part III), "admit to bind" against the impossible convention (CR-060 CT2), free soil against self-government (CR-061 K7), a treaty that cedes no state (CR-062 Part II), Lincoln's restraint set beside Elias's (CR-065 P1–P2, Part VI). No canon passage says how the Chronicler regards Lincoln, what Lincoln thought of Elias, or what the contest is about in Lincoln's own words.
- **1848.** "The House has just voted that the war was unconstitutionally begun", one of Polk's reasons for accepting Trist's treaty that binds the United States to respect California's frontier (§4, PROPOSED — CR-028). Canon does not say who voted.
- **1861.** Washington "eventually offers a route to release and investigation in exchange for withdrawal. Elias demands broader strategic guarantees … a limited crisis becomes a territorial war through choices rather than inevitability" (§9).
- **Slavery's end in the South.** A free-womb amendment in 1874–75 (§13); "final Confederate abolition in 1888–1889 is financed by long compensation bonds secured on customs co-administered by Pacifica" (§25). Pacifica enforces a fugitive agreement with Richmond from 1871–72 until abolition, and "when habeas corpus frees a returned fugitive, the cabinet overrides the court under the enabling law" (§13). "Douglass and other critics see a door closed just as enslaved people become free" (§25, 1888).
- **The Chronicler.** A scholar of the imperial order "steeped in classical models of historical rise and decline", dependent on testimony and papers for the early years; "once an admirer of the founding order"; later voices "may challenge his interpretations without establishing that he knowingly lies" (CT-CHRONICLER; larger movement).

## What this CR does

1. States what the contest is about, in each man's declared terms: seven questions (Part A).
2. Sets the Chronicler's stance and method toward Lincoln: adversarial in judgment and selection, never in fact (Part B).
3. Places the moves on the timeline from 1838 to 1889, most of them Lincoln's real words and votes (Part C).
4. Gives Lincoln his life after March 1865: Springfield, the public letters, and one meeting in 1869. Lincoln dies in November 1872 (Part D).
5. Names the voices that answer the Chronicler's Lincoln (Part E).
6. Traces how Pacifica's existence alters Lincoln's career from 1846 and his memory to 1932 (Part F).
7. Sets out the contest in thirteen rounds, six opened by Elias, six by Lincoln and one by a petitioner, with each man's reading of the other, the papers in which they answer each other, and the reason Elias wins (Part G).
8. Says how the contest is told: the rules of the founder's conduct, five acts and twenty-two beats, which beats are heavy, what each move leaves behind, and where the telling changes scale and pace (Part H).
9. Gives voice samples of the Chronicler writing Lincoln ([CR-068-samples.md](CR-068-samples.md); not storyline text), and keeps the research list and the index of source passages in [CR-068-sources.md](CR-068-sources.md).

It changes no date and no approved decision of its own; it rests on two changes CR-060 makes to approved text (Nevada's admission, CT10; Bear River, CT15), it replaces one clause that canon marks PROPOSED, the override of 1871 (CT18), and it amends canon §§5 and 8 on the Republic's debt to London (CT26). It adds beats, a frame and a voice, and it proposes new facts where canon is blank: the Union's recognition of a state of war in August 1861 (CT4), the armistice offer of 1862 (CT22), the printed terms of the Richmond credit and the answer at Honolulu (CT21, CT24), the Terms of Monterey and their two reservations (CT10, CT23), *Two Houses* (CT20), the Port Townsend convention (CT11), Lincoln's offer of 1864 to Pacifica's people (G8), the blind vote (CT17), the ransom article of 1871 and free navigation as the guarantor's condition that year (CT18; G12), Richmond's debt in London (CT28), Lincoln's death (CT19) and the public papers listed in Part G (PP1–PP14).

## Decisions

### Part A — The terms of the contest (AG1–AG7)

The Pacifican nomos already has a custom for this: the **contest with declared terms**, where "questions and criteria are published before proofs, predictions or demonstrations" (CR-049 E-table). The Chronicler applies it to the two men. Each published his terms; the Chronicler judges each by his own.

| ID | The question | Lincoln's declared terms (Hist.) | Elias's declared terms (canon) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AG1 | **Founding or keeping.** Is the republic's work done? | Lyceum, 27 January 1838: the fathers' work is done; the danger is a man of "the family of the lion, or the tribe of the eagle" for whom "a seat in Congress, a gubernatorial or a presidential chair" is not enough. "Towering genius disdains a beaten path … It scorns to tread in the footsteps of any predecessor, however illustrious." Such genius "burns for distinction; and if possible, it will have it, whether at the expense of emancipating slaves or enslaving freemen." The remedy: reverence for the laws as "the political religion of the nation", and "cold, calculating, unimpassioned reason" (Beveridge 1:229–230) | The Colonel's Plutarch gives Elias "Lycurgus and Numa, who founded by law" (CR-066); *On the Public Arts* (1851–54), written as a lawgiver and never published as a law: "What we build must let those who come after us see further than we do, and beat us" (§5, CR-049). **Why these terms.** They are the contest Nietzsche's essay describes: two men of the same gifts (both surveyors in their twenties, both largely self-taught readers of law and history, both sons of poor men; each said his father had lost land to the law, and only Elias is believed), each the measure of the other. Lincoln at the Lyceum names the danger the Greeks met with ostracism, the man without a rival; Pacifica's nomos meets it with the contest. The Chronicler's frame is that Lincoln was the rival who made Elias's victory worth having.

**Where each term is fought.** The terms are not only the Chronicler's. The two men argue them with each other, in the papers of Part G: consent and union in the two pages and Lincoln's answer (G1), in the offer of 1861 (G2), in Lincoln's refusal of 1864 (G6b), in the withheld cession (G9) and in Oregon (G10); founding or keeping, and power, in the Terms of Monterey and the letters that answer them (G6, G8, G9, G10); who governs whom, and ability, in the Proclamation, the officer Elias kept, the refuge and the writ (G3, G4, G5, G12); mind and matter in the gold of 1867 (G11). One house or two (AG7) is fought in the reasons of 1861 (G2), the armistice offer of 1862 (G4) and *Two Houses* (G6b).

### Part B — The Chronicler's Lincoln: stance and method (CH1–CH11)

| ID | Rule | Why | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| CH1 | **The agonal courtesy.** The Chronicler never belittles Lincoln's mind, honesty or courage. In Pacifica a victory over a small man is no victory; the founder's rival must be great or the founder is not. This is why a Thornist historian is fair to Lincoln: the culture requires it, not his private virtue | Gives the fairness a Pacifican motive, so it does not read as the author's thumb on the scale | APPROVED |
| CH2 | **Adversarial in judgment, never in fact.** He quotes Lincoln exactly and at length, and never puts words in his mouth. His weapons are selection, juxtaposition and the irony of outcomes: 1848 against 1861; the Lyceum's "towering genius" against Lincoln's own ambition (Herndon: "His ambition was a little engine that knew no rest", Beveridge 3:275); Illinois's canals against Carquinez | The author's "fairly, honestly, but adversarially" | APPROVED |
| CH3 | **He judges each man by the terms he declared** (Part A), and admits it when the test goes against Elias. The terms are unequal, and he says so: Lincoln argued in public; Elias wrote a memorandum he never published (*On the Public Arts*), which the Chronicler has from the archive. From 1861 Elias argues in public too: in the seven reasons (PP3b), the answer at Honolulu (PP4c), the Terms of Monterey and their preface (PP7), *Two Houses* (PP8b) and the answer to Oregon (PP12b), and where the two differ the Chronicler holds him to the paper he published | The custom of the contest with declared terms (CR-049); keeps the adversarial voice honest by construction | APPROVED |
| CH4 | **The concessions he must make, and makes:** Lincoln offered redress in 1861 and Elias chose war (§9; Part C M9); Lincoln held an election he expected to lose and accepted it (CR-065); the border that the fugitive agreement shuts from 1871 stands against Elias under Lincoln's Peoria sentence (§13; G12); in Hawaii (1874–76) and in the Constitution of 1887 Elias holds a union together against the will of one of its founding peoples, Lincoln's position of 1861 without Lincoln's reason (M15); and the design that won the war kept a slaveholding republic in the field, at the cost of the enslaved (W1, W4) | Without these the voice is propaganda; with them it is history written by a partisan | APPROVED |
| CH5 | **He uses the hostile tradition and declines its excesses.** He takes from Masters's line of argument (the 1848 speech that "condemn[s] him out of his own mouth", Masters 99; the Articles' "perpetual union" dissolved by the Constitution itself, Masters 321–322; the war measures of 1861, Masters 408–415). He does not take its sympathy for the slaveholders' cause, its contempt ("a lazy mind", Masters 115), or the gossip of New Salem | Masters is the nearest real model of an adversarial Lincoln biographer, and the model of what the Chronicler must not become. The Chronicler never cites Masters: the book is from 1931 in our world and cannot exist in his. He takes the arguments from the Union's own Democrats of 1861–68 (K22) | APPROVED |
| CH6 | **He doubts testimony he cannot test.** Herndon is his best witness and his most suspect one: "believe what you must and no more" (Herndon, quoted Masters 138). | CH7 | **His blind spot.** He measures Lincoln by consent as a procedure: votes, compacts, conventions. He is slow to see what Lincoln saw at Peoria, that the enslaved are the governed whose consent was never asked, and he does not count the Native nations whose title no federal cession settled (§12). He sees the point in the case of the fugitive agreement (A4) and misses it in the West (A5, deliberately). No voice in the strand says what he does not see; the reader is left to see it | The canon role: independent perspectives challenge the Chronicler without proving he lies | APPROVED |
| CH8 | **Form: a Plutarchan pair.** The Lincoln strand ends in a *synkrisis*, a Comparison of Thorne and Lincoln on Plutarch's model, after the Lives of the founder by law and the keeper of the law. The Chronicler reached for Plutarch because Elias did (CR-066) | The Chronicler's classical training; one formal device that holds the strand together | APPROVED: a set piece, placed after Lincoln's death |
| CH9 | **Naming.** He calls him "Lincoln" and, once, "Mr. Lincoln", the form Elias uses in the superseded first-draft manuscript (ch. 4; register only, not authority). He records where each reading failed (MD1–MD4), the founder's included | A contest of minds needs each mind's picture of the other on the page; without the documents it would be the Chronicler's invention | APPROVED |

### Part C — The moves, 1838–1867, and their sequels (M1–M16)

| ID | When | The move | Where it sits | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M1 | 27 January 1838 | **The Lyceum address** is the strand's epigraph and its first chapter. The Chronicler reads it as a portrait drawn twenty years in advance: a towering genius who "scorns to tread in the footsteps of any predecessor", and the danger that comes not from "all the armies of Europe, Asia, and Africa" but from within (Beveridge 1:227–230). Lincoln was wrong about where it would come from: across the mountains, not from within. | M2 | 1832–36; 1844–45 | **The parallel lives.** Lincoln's first circular (9 March 1832): his ambition is "that of being truly esteemed by my fellow-men, by rendering myself worthy of their esteem", and he has "been too familiar with disappointments to be very much chagrined" (Beveridge 1:117). Deputy surveyor under John Calhoun (1:132; from 1833, Hist.; to verify). | M4 | 1848 | **Lincoln votes against paying for California.** Historically he voted against an amendment paying "the expenses of Fremont and Stockton in California and for Stockton's government in that territory" (Beveridge 2:139 n.4), and on 10 August 1848 against continuing Frémont's survey "to find a route for railways to the Pacific", 28 to 128 (2:155 n.). Here Stockton governed nothing and Frémont has been court-martialled for Cahuenga (§4); the amendments' objects shift to the Cahuenga claims and an Oregon survey. The House Journal (research 3) shows what they paid for: the first, the Senate's eleventh amendment to the army bill (H.R. 618), $200,000 for the costs of the operations under Stockton and Frémont, Stockton's civil government and the battalion's pay as mounted riflemen, rejected 40 to 110 (pp. 1251–52); the second, $30,000 to continue Frémont's surveys "in Oregon and California" and find "practicable lines of communication, by railroads or otherwise" to the Pacific, rejected 28 to 128 (p. 1233). | M5 | 10 January 1849 | **Lincoln's plan for the District of Columbia.** Children "born of slave mothers" after 1 January 1850 to be free, apprenticed and educated by the mothers' owners; owners who free a slave paid from the Treasury on a valuation board's figure; fugitives returned; the whole in force only if the District's white men vote for it (Beveridge 2:184–185). ### Part F — Lincoln's career in a world with Pacifica (K1–K27)

The author's brief: "determine more precisely how Lincoln's life and career would have been affected and altered by the existence of the California Republic and of the State of Pacifica." **The rule used here:** Lincoln's life changes only where Pacifica's existence reaches it, and every change names its cause in canon. Where nothing reaches him, his history stands. His words stay his own. Where the story needs him to say something new, the draft gives the argument, not invented sentences.

**The short answers.**
- **How he gets elected after Spotty.** The charge costs him most where he was already losing: Illinois in 1848 and 1858, and the Democratic counties of the Lower North. It cannot be used against him at Chicago, where every former Whig shares the record, and in 1860 the Republicans turn it into proof that he was right about the war. His own answer, the Senate's roll call, shares the blame rather than clearing him (K3, K4, K9–K12).
- **Did he anticipate Pacifica at Sumter?** Yes, and he provided against it. Seward's foreign-war memorandum of 1 April puts Monterey in front of him that week, and he refuses it. He provisions Sumter and deliberately does not provision Sausalito. Then his own proclamation on privateers, read off Oahu weeks later, gives Elias his occasion (K14–K16).
- **His reaction when Pacifica enters.** He offers redress through his oldest friend, Edward Baker, and names the spot. He asks for no declaration; Congress recognizes the war over his preference. He keeps to one war at a time and loses Baker at Ball's Bluff (K17–K20).
- **How the Union remembers him.** He is not a martyr. First he is blamed for starting the war. Then the revanchists make him their saint. He himself refuses that role. In the end he is remembered for what the Union kept: the president who held the election and lost it (K22–K27).

### Part G — The contest itself: the rounds, 1861–1871 (G1–G12, with G6b), the two readings (MD1–MD4), the papers (PP1–PP14), and why Elias wins (W1–W4)

The author's brief for this part: "an almost even number of openings by each actor, reasons aligning with causes, each contestant modeling the other and surprising the other by acting differently from what the other's model would predict, both using the best of their known stratagems, both engaging each other's ideas explicitly."

**The shape of the contest.** Lincoln's plan is a sequence: the rebellion first, then the West with the whole army (K19). Elias's plan is to keep the first war from being finished before the Union's voters are asked whether to go on with both, and to make the second war the one that can be ended at a known price. The date he aims at is 8 November 1864, and he provides for the case that the date fails him (G8). Lincoln's counter is to strike at the consent Elias's war depends on: Hawaii's yearly assent and Monterey's own Assembly. Every part of Elias's plan named here is published by him before he acts on it (O1).

**What counts as a round.** One man opens, the other answers, and at least one of them acts on a stated reading of his rival. Each round says whether that reading held. Elias opens six and Lincoln six, and a petitioner one (G12). The deeds are canon or proposed in CR-059 to CR-065 unless a round marks a deed as new. Part G adds the readings, the papers and the intention that links the deeds. G1 is Elias's opening: he orders the two pages.

#### The rounds

**G1 — The two pages. Elias opens, March–April 1861.** *(New)*
- **His reading (MD1):** the lawyer will rest on the law. He will say that a State cannot leave a perpetual Union, and that Texas in 1836 was a different case. Any legal distinction serves the West, because Oregon and Washington are territories, and a territory is not one of "these states".
- **The move:** when the inaugural reaches San Francisco, the press prints "the union of these states is perpetual" (Hist.; research 32) beside the right of any people "to rise up and shake off the existing government" (Beveridge 2:129), without comment (M8; PP1). The readers it is meant for are the West's Americans, above all Oregon's Douglas Democrats (CR-059).
- **The answer:** Lincoln does not rest on the law. He concedes the right of 1848 whole and reads his own page more closely than its printer did. The right belongs to a people "being inclined and having the power", to "any portion of such people that can" (Beveridge 2:129). It is a right to make a revolution oneself. And the inaugural allows that revolution may be morally justified when a majority deprives a minority of "any clearly written constitutional right" (Hist.: first inaugural; research 17), so whoever claims the right must name his wrong (PP2). The message names no country. It is written before Astoria falls, and it is aimed at the South, which has the power and no wrong.
- **What it does to the West:** Elias had prepared for a legal test and was given a moral one. Article XIV already forbade annexation by decree (canon §§8–9). After PP2 the Union's own President is the author of the standard by which any Western vote will be judged, and from August it applies to an occupied country.
- **The exchange on the wrong** comes later, in the papers that follow. Elias names the wrong in his reply to Baker (PP3): in 1859 Congress refused Oregon statehood for the votes it would have cast (CR-059). Lincoln answers in 1863 (PP8): a later Congress passed Oregon's Enabling Act on 2 March 1861, and it was Pacifica's landing that stopped the convention (CR-059 E2, W1). Neither convinces the other.
- **Who gains:** Lincoln. He sets the test, and it becomes a third reason why Elias cannot refuse Oregon's vote in 1865 (CT9).

**G2 — Bread. Lincoln opens, June–August 1861.** *(Canon sequence; the design and the reasons new; CT13)*
- **His reading (MD3):** the eagle will refuse a fair offer, and the refusal will undo him where he is weakest. The Protectorship runs one year and needs Hawaii's assent to be renewed (canon §9). Victoria beat him on this ground in 1860 (canon §8).
- **The move:** the method of Sumter, used that spring (K15): offer so much that whoever refuses has begun the war. Through Baker, in public, Lincoln offers everything Hawaii demanded in May, release, an inquiry, regret and compensation for the dead sailor's family, in exchange for withdrawal (canon §9; K17).
- **The answer:** Elias does not refuse alone, and he does not hide why. He lays the offer whole, with his reply, before the Council, where Hawaii's delegation sits, and asks its concurrence before he answers Baker (PP3). He tells the Council that if it prefers the offer he will accept it, and it may name another Protector to carry out the withdrawal (new).
- **Why the Council goes with him.** He is asking two small states to stay at war with the United States, and the reasons have to weigh more than the danger. He gives seven, and they are printed with the reply (PP3b). With them he lays before the Council the preliminary Southern dealings whose disclosure Victoria required in May (canon §9), and he owns the two pages of the spring as printed by his order.
  1. *The offer pays for the man and keeps the right.* Lincoln's proclamation of 19 April stands, and with it the squadron's claim to stop a neutral packet at sea on suspicion (K16). Washington calls the captain's act an excess. It does not give up the claim, and cannot while it blockades the South; historically it gave up the men of the *Trent* that winter and not the right (Hist.: Seward's reply of 26 December 1861 held that Wilkes might stop and search the *Trent* and erred only in not bringing her in for adjudication; research 36). Every Hawaiian vessel sails under that claim for as long as the war lasts.
  2. *The squadron lives on this coast.* California's harbours are closed to it and the Sausalito store is sealed (canon §9). What it has left in the Pacific is the Columbia and the Sound. Withdraw, and the ships that killed a Hawaiian go back to their anchorages with a grievance and a war's licence. Keep the river's mouth and the Sound, and the nearest American dockyard is round Cape Horn. For an island kingdom that is the difference between a neighbour's navy and a visitor's.
  3. *Article XIV is being tried for the first time.* Hawaii gave its defence and its navy to the common institutions in 1856 (canon §8). If the first Hawaiian killed by a foreign warship is settled by the offender's own inquiry, the article will not deter the next captain.
  4. *The danger will never be smaller.* The news of Bull Run has just come (21 July 1861, Hist.; assumed to stand; the news reached San Francisco by Pony Express on 2 August 1861, research 36). The Union cannot hold the road to its own capital, and it wants every hull it owns for the blockade. In two years it will have lost the South or built the largest army and navy in the hemisphere. Either way the question of the Pacific will be asked again, and on worse terms.
  5. *The people who trusted us.* The Oregonians who made a civil compact with the army (CR-059 W2) would be handed back to a government that suspended the writ of habeas corpus that spring and held Merryman against the Chief Justice (Masters 408).
  6. *The river is worth the risk.* The Willamette's wheat, the Sound's timber and coal, on which both member states' steamers run, and the new gold on the Clearwater, which comes down the Columbia (Hist.: Pierce's party on the Clearwater in August 1860, Elk City 1860, the Salmon River in 1861; research 36; CR-059 to confirm).
  7. *No conquest on this continent.* If the Republic withdraws, the Union has its sequence: the South first, and then the Pacific with a million men. He gives this to the Council as his own first reason and Hawaii's last, and he states it as a rule. The Republic will not see one American people conquered by another, because it would be next. It will send no army east, and it will lend to those who hold their own ground (G3).
- **The limits he accepts, and Hawaii's price.** No column goes east of the mountains. The mandate stays one year, and nothing is annexed by decree: the resolution of May is read into the reply. The compensation is accepted for the sailor's family whatever else is refused. And the fleet's first station is Oahu: the first armoured ship the yards complete goes to Honolulu, not to the Columbia (new). Sherman supports the protection and objects that the objectives have grown (canon §9). Victoria's party concurs on the first three reasons, records its doubt of the sixth and enters the seventh as the Protector's own.
- **The reply (PP3)** asks three things of Washington: that it renounce search of neutral vessels in Pacifican and Hawaiian waters; that it bind itself by treaty to amnesty and the writ for every inhabitant of the Columbia; and that the Enabling Act's convention meet with both armies withdrawn. That is canon's "broader strategic guarantees and protection for collaborators" (§9). Lincoln can grant none of them. The first would undo his blockade. The second is a foreign guarantee of American citizens' rights on American soil, which is Elias's own objection of 1848 turned round. Elias knows it, and says so to Baker. He does not expect the reply to be accepted, and he says so to Baker when he hands it to him, with his reasons (B5). The terms are ones he would keep if they were taken.
- **What the Chronicler says of it:** the offer was honest, the seven reasons were true and were all given, the reply asked more than Lincoln could grant, and the choice was the founder's (A3; CH4). He chose a war for the West against an adversary who had offered him redress, and he told his Council, his enemy's minister and the public why.
- **Who gains:** both. Lincoln has the record, and Victoria has it to use at the renewals (CR-059 W4; CR-060 C6). Elias has the war, and he has the Council's hand on the refusal, given for reasons Hawaii holds as its own. Lincoln's reading foresaw a man refusing alone, for reasons he would not own.

**G3 — The river's mouth. Elias opens, autumn 1861–1862.** *(Canon deeds; the design new)*
- **His reading (MD1):** Lincoln's war is a sequence, and the river comes first. He took Gentry's flatboat to New Orleans in 1828 (Beveridge 1:87). He will give ground in the West to keep the sequence, so the place to break it is the river's mouth.
- **The move:** a hand under the first war, held out in the open: the seventh reason of August 1861 has said that the Republic will lend to those who hold their own ground (G2). Pacifican credit, procurement and Logan's small technical mission help hold New Orleans in the spring of 1862, and supplies go round the Horn to Matamoros (canon §11). No Pacifican army goes east. Ericsson, who came west about 1855 when his armoured-ship proposal had found no builder (canon §8), builds his turret ship for the Columbia, and the Union has no *Monitor* at Hampton Roads (canon §§10–11).
- **The answer is the one Elias expected:** Lincoln keeps the West cheap. He sends four Iowa regiments through Panama to hold the Sound, and makes one attempt to recover the lower Columbia in 1862, which the turret ship turns back (canon §10). After that he refuses the Western members' demands for more (K19). He keeps Grant after a worse Shiloh than history's (CR-064 S5–S8, C2), and Grant takes Vicksburg, though its army escapes (canon §11).
- **Who gains:** Elias, and it decides the war, though nobody can know that before November 1864. New Orleans stands and the lower river stays shut. MD1 was right. Lincoln loses only what he cannot hold, and the second front costs him the four Iowa regiments he diverts to the Sound (CR-064 S3).
- **The idea (AG4):** Ericsson, and Sherman, who came to Monterey's service in 1857 when his banking ended (CR-064 H4), are the founder's rule in practice: "give it work and judge it by its works" (canon §5). Lincoln, keeping the general the newspapers wanted dismissed, judges the same way. The Chronicler notes that each man's best principle is found in the other's practice (M2).
- **The price to Elias:** he is paymaster to a slave republic. Lincoln collects in G4.

**G4 — The Proclamation, sent west. Lincoln opens, September 1862 – January 1863.** *(Hist. acts, assumed standing with CR-064 E5; their western aim and Pacifica's answer new; CT15)*
- **His reading (MD3, with what Halleck and Baker have told him):** Pacifica lives on Hawaii's yearly assent. Its creed is ability. It is banker to Richmond.
- **Before it, the armistice offer (PP3c; new; CT22).** Elias dates the offer at Monterey in the last week of April 1862. It reaches Washington in early June, while McClellan's army lies within sight of Richmond and the government expects to end the rebellion that summer (the Peninsula campaign is assumed to stand; CR-064; the lag follows the mail times of research 38). The Union, the Confederacy and the Republic are to stand on the lines they hold, and commissioners of all three are to meet at Havana, a neutral port each can reach. He offers it as a belligerent and says so, and he asks nothing for the Republic that the Terms of the next year do not ask. Washington is too sure of winning to accept mediation from a power about a twentieth of its size, and it does not answer Monterey at all. The answer is a circular of the State Department to its ministers abroad, printed in the newspapers: the United States will hear no proposal from any foreign government concerning an insurrection of its own citizens, and least of all from one whose soldiers stand on American soil. By the time the circular reaches Monterey in August, the Seven Days have driven McClellan from Richmond (Hist., assumed standing), and it reads differently. The model is Seward's refusal of the French offer early in 1863 (Hist.; to verify, research 43). Only after the refusal does Elias widen the credit, and he publishes its condition: the war advances end on the day both governments accept an armistice.
- **What Elias has judged (MD1):** he reads Lincoln's letter to Greeley of 22 August correctly. "If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that" (Hist.: wording checked, research 23). Lincoln will free slaves if it serves the Union. Elias's error is about the hour. He judges that Lincoln cannot do it without a victory, and that there is none coming: Hampton Roads is lost, New Orleans holds, Shiloh was a held landing and the Peninsula has failed (canon §11; CR-064). Early in September, his offer refused, he widens the mission to the Confederate armies and the Matamoros credit (canon §11; the dating new), under the published condition.
- **The move:** Antietam is a drawn battle, and Lincoln calls it victory enough (17 and 22 September 1862, Hist.). Here the preliminary Proclamation is also aimed west (new). In Honolulu the mission press puts it into Hawaiian, for the legislature that must renew the Protectorship (no Hawaiian printing of it has been found; new, research 19). In January Lincoln answers a memorial from the coloured citizens of San Francisco (PP4; Mifflin Gibbs a candidate signer, CR-059 V13): no man is good enough to govern another without that other's consent (Beveridge 3:255), and a republic that examines every child for ability lends its gold so that four million may never be examined at all.
- **The answer, in two parts.** The government's account comes first in time and second in weight. The founder's own answer is given in person at Honolulu (below), and it answers the letter's question, which the account does not. **The account (PP4b)** is published in February and March in English, Spanish and Hawaiian. It has the nomos's form, two columns of deeds with their dates.
  - *The Republic's column.* Treaties with the Native nations signed in 1851–52 and kept; homelands that cannot be sold; no volunteer company ever paid from the treasury (canon §5; CR-059 N5). In the Southwest, a treaty that recognizes the Diné homeland (canon §10). And in New Mexico, where the Republic found debt peonage and a trade in Native captives when its column arrived, both are being ended by its own law, with compensation, against the resistance of its own new citizens (canon §10; CR-061). The account presents this as what the Republic does where it governs, at its own cost. It is not offered as a reply to the Proclamation, and the dates show that it was begun before it (start date to confirm in CR-061).
  - *The Union's column.* On 26 December 1862, six days before the Proclamation took effect, thirty-eight Dakota men were hanged together at Mankato by the President's order (Hist.: thirty-nine approved in his message of 11 December, one reprieved on 23 December; research 35). The Governor of Minnesota had told his legislature that the Sioux "must be exterminated or driven forever beyond the borders of Minnesota" (Hist.: message of 9 September 1862; research 35). In February and March 1863 Congress annuls the Dakota treaties and removes the Dakota and the Winnebago from the state (Hist.: acts of 16 February, 21 February and 3 March 1863). The Homestead Act and the Pacific Railway Act of 1862 give away the country of nations that were never asked.
  - *The charge.* By the President's own sentence from Peoria, who asked the Dakota? And the account draws the distinction on which it rests. In the matter of slavery the Republic is charged with lending to a government that does the wrong. In the matter of the nations the Union does the wrong itself, with its own army, on its own soil, over its own President's signature. The Republic has a guilty debtor. The Union is the guilty party.
  - *A second page.* Beside Lincoln's letter to the coloured citizens of San Francisco the papers print his address of 14 August 1862 to a deputation of free coloured men, whom he advised to leave the country for a colony in Central America (Hist.; research 35). The Republic's ports are open to them, and its examinations.
- **The answer at Honolulu (PP4c; new; CT21, CT24; approved 11 November 2026).** Hawaii's legislature has put the renewal over (CT16), and the office is running toward its last day. Elias has been home from Texas since March (canon §11; B11; the calendar is CT24). When the news of the postponement reaches Monterey in the first days of May he sails for Honolulu, leaving his capital in the last weeks of his term for the chamber that can end it, and it is a risk he chose. In the third week of May he stands before the legislature that can let his office lapse. He begins in Hawaiian, which he speaks imperfectly (CR-066, CT-ELIAS), and answers the question Lincoln put to San Francisco's coloured citizens: why the Republic's gold stands behind four million who will never be examined.
  - *What he says (argument only; text OPEN).* The gold is lent. It goes out on printed terms, at interest and on security, and it comes back in gold (B11). It is lent so that the South shall not be conquered (G2, the seventh reason): a Union that has conquered eleven States will come to the Pacific next with the army that did it, and Hawaii's safety, like California's, rests on the Americans being two peoples. He does not say that the loan helps the slave, and he does not claim the enslaved were asked. The Republic forbids slavery on its own soil, as Hawaii's constitution does on Hawaii's, and it does not reform a foreign country with its treasury. He concedes Lincoln's point as far as it goes: four million will not be examined, and the Republic's gold is behind the government that holds them.
  - *What he offers.* He speaks as a creditor. Slaves are a security the Republic's law forbids it to hold, and a country that leaves four million untrained is a poorer debtor than it need be. When Richmond, at peace, chooses to end slavery by free birth or by purchase at a valuation, which is the method the President proposed for the District of Columbia in 1849 and for the whole Union in December 1862 (M5, M10), the Republic will lend for it, or stand surety for it, on the customs. He makes this no condition of the present credit.
  - *What he binds.* Nothing on Richmond. The contract settled in Texas keeps its three terms (B11): the credit is lent for independence, its war advances end on an armistice both governments accept, and it falls due if Richmond treats for reunion. "Ends" means only that: what has been lent is still owed, and commerce and later credit are free (CR-062 A3, A6). The offer of a later loan is printed with them. Canon carries the rest: Richmond adopts the free womb by its own amendment in 1874–75, after Grant's war (§13). In 1888–89, pressed by its London creditors to refinance, it issues the compensation bonds on the Republic's guarantee, secured on customs the Republic helps to administer (§25; CT28). The Republic's own loan is repaid with interest.
  - *What it does.* The renewal passes narrowly, in the last week of the term (CT16). It passes on a man who crossed an ocean to be questioned by the body that could dismiss him. The reason he gives is the seventh of 1861, which Victoria's party then entered as the Protector's own (G2); what carries the vote is that he came and conceded the point. The account of the nations is the second thing Honolulu has read.
- **What is owed to Lincoln, and paid (CH1).** He cut the list of the condemned from 303 to 39, and reprieved one more, against the fury of Minnesota, and his message of December 1862 asked Congress "whether our Indian system shall not be remodeled" (Hist.; research 35). The Chronicler says so. He adds that the remodelling never came, and that this is the one paper of the contest Lincoln never answered.
- **The officer who said no:** that winter Sherman refuses the assignment to the Confederate armies, on his own ground, and Vance takes it (canon §11). Lincoln's reading expects the eagle to break an officer who refuses him. Elias keeps Sherman as Inspector-General. An office is held for its work, and no one may make its holder give a false account of it: "not a patron, not a minister, not I" (canon §5).
- **Who gains: Lincoln, outright, for a season (TL7).** His stroke costs Elias Sherman for the Southern mission, and some weeks in which the Protectorship might have lapsed. The account answers a charge with a heavier charge and leaves the letter's question standing, and the Chronicler says so (A8b). Then the founder answers the question himself, before the body that can dismiss him, and his answer concedes the point (O3). Lincoln's rejoinder comes in the autumn (PP8): the Protector tells Hawaii that the slave is no business of his, and lends to the master; the Proclamation frees him now. Neither convinces the other. MD1 does not survive the winter.
- **How it turns against him.** Lincoln aimed at Honolulu and expected the mission churches to carry the legislature. He had not reckoned who would read the two papers there. The Hawaiian-language press prints both (*Ka Hoku o ka Pakipika*, Kalākaua's paper against the mission press, 1861–63, Hist.; research 19). Its readers are not planters reading about slaves. They are a Native people, under their own king, reading about Mankato, and about a government whose settlers and missionaries' sons already own much of their best land. The renewal of 1863 passes late and without conditions (CR-060; CT16), and when Lincoln offers Hawaii its sugar market in 1864 (G8), the delegation remembers which government hangs Natives. And the account travels east with the traders. From 1863 the Shoshone and their neighbours make compacts with the Republic (CR-060 D9, N2), and the roads by which a Union army would one day have to come run through nations that have chosen their friend. That enters Elias's reckoning in 1864 (G8).
- **What the Chronicler does not see (CH7).** He quotes the account with less reserve than any other paper. The nations in the Republic's column had compacts and no ballot. In the winter of 1865–66 a company of Owyhee miners kills a Paiute camp, and the Republic tries them (CR-060 M8–M10). No later voice in this strand says what he does not; the reader is left to see it.

**G5 — The refuge. Elias opens, spring 1863.** *(CR-059 W7; the papers new)*
- **His reading (MD2, new):** Lincoln now does whatever the law will bear, and his war rests on a draft (the Enrollment Act of 3 March 1863, Hist.). The men who will not be drafted are a consent he cannot compel. He will demand them back, or arrest them on the roads, and either will serve Pacifica in Missouri and the Lower North.
- **The move:** published terms of passage, offered by name to men who will not be made to fight their own countrymen. Any man may come west and take land under the family land law, and no one is extradited for evading a foreign draft (CR-059 W7). The government prints the terms, in its gazette and in the papers, under Lincoln's own sentence from Peoria, that no man is good enough to govern another without that other's consent (Beveridge 3:255; PP5). It is Elias's first use of Lincoln's idea against Lincoln's law.
- **The answer:** Lincoln makes no demand and no arrest on the roads. He answers the argument, in the manner of his letter to Corning (PP6). Consent is given at the ballot box and is not withdrawn at the muster roll. And the Protector, who keeps a conscript army of his own (CR-059 S3), objects to conscription only east of the mountains. He cannot stop them in any case: the Platte road is a thousand miles long, and he has no cavalry to spare for it. He sets the Homestead Act of 1862 (Hist.) against the land law.
- **How many come (story assumption):** far more than in our history, where the men who went west to avoid the draft found only mining camps. Here they find a government that publishes terms, pays in coin and grants land. Working figure: several tens of thousands of men, with their families, from 1863 to 1865 (MODEL OPEN; for the population model and CR-059 research task 25). They settle Idaho, the Columbia and the Basin, and they are a labour force and a reserve the Republic did not have in 1861.
- **What it does to the Union:** every man who leaves makes the next draft call fall harder on those who stay. The call of July 1864 for half a million men (Hist.: proclamation of 18 July 1864, with a draft after 5 September for unfilled quotas; research 24) comes in the campaign summer. It is one of the causes of November (G8).
- **Who gains:** Elias, clearly. Lincoln gives him no grievance and has the better of the argument, and loses the men. MD2 was wrong in detail: Elias expected force and got a jest.

**G6 — One price. Elias opens, August 1863.** *(New; CT10)*
- **His reading (MD2):** Lincoln speaks to peoples, and his war against the South has no stopping place, because a perpetual Union cannot name a price. For the West he could name one, since on his own theory a territory can be ceded (G7), but he will not while he hopes to finish the South first. So Elias names it for him, when it cannot be taken for fear or for greed: the month after Gettysburg and Vicksburg (the Eastern battle assumed standing with CR-064 E5).
- **The move:** *The Terms of Monterey* (PP7), in six heads. (1) Pacifica asks no state of the Union. (2) Each territory's future is settled by the act of its own inhabitants. This is Lincoln's test from G1. (3) The public domain and the public works are paid for in gold. (4) The ports stay open to American commerce. (5) Nothing is asked concerning the South as a term of this peace. (6) The heads will not be raised by victory or lowered by defeat. The nomos has the form already: a contest with declared terms, published before the proof (CR-049). **Two reservations are printed under the heads, in the same type (new; CT23).** (a) If Congress passes an act to make a state of any territory before the peace, the Republic holds itself free to occupy that territory. (b) The heads stand, unchanged, for any President of the United States who will treat on them. If on the fourth of March 1865 the Union has sworn a President who will not, the Republic will take the last American station on the Sound. The Republic deals with the government at Richmond as a government, and will recognize it by treaty whoever is President: that waits on no armistice and is no term of this peace. Its credit there is lent on the terms printed in Texas (B11). Whatever the Republic does afterwards in this war has been printed here first.
- **The preface answers the Lyceum address (AG1).** Lincoln said in 1838 that the fathers' field was harvested, and that a towering genius who found nothing left to build would pull down (Beveridge 1:229–230). Elias's argument: then the cure is more to build, not more reverence. A republic that can only keep must hold by force the people who would rather found. On the eagle: the Protector can be dismissed every twelvemonth by either of two legislatures (canon §§9, 12), and the President by no one for four years. (Argument only; text OPEN.)
- **The preface calls Jefferson as its witness.** Lincoln has written that "the principles of Jefferson are the definitions and axioms of free society" (to Pierce and others, 6 April 1859, Hist.; research 37). Jefferson did not think the continent could be governed from one capital. In 1803 he wrote of the Mississippi country that if its people saw their interest in separation he would not take sides against them, and wished both confederacies well (to Breckinridge, 12 August 1803, Hist.; to verify). And of the settlement at the mouth of the Columbia he wrote to Astor that he viewed it "as the germ of a great, free and independent empire on that side of our continent" (9 November 1813, Hist.; research 37). The preface notes where the Republic's columns landed in July 1861: at Astoria. It is the two-pages method again, and this time the second page is by the author of the Declaration.
- **The answer (PP8):** MD3 predicted terms that grow and a man who never speaks of his own removal. Lincoln sees that the paper fits neither and answers the argument he has been given. He does not dispute Jefferson, whom he cannot disown. He reads him closely, as he read his own page in 1861. Jefferson's empire was to be "free and independent", and he expected liberty and self-government to spread from it. So the question is not whether a second republic may stand on the Pacific. It is whether this one is Jefferson's: a Protector with a king in his confederation and his credit at Richmond. A price named by the man in possession is a ransom. And he takes up the preface's last claim: the Protector says he may be turned out, so let it be tried. He tries it in G8. From this paper he reads Elias differently (MD4). He also answers Honolulu: the Protector calls the slave no business of his and lends to the master, and the Proclamation frees him now. And he brings the charge he has carried since 1858. A house divided against itself cannot stand (Beveridge 4:160), and the Protector, born on the Missouri, lends gold to keep his mother's house divided. Elias answers that winter (G6b).
- **What Lincoln has conceded:** that a free and independent republic on the Pacific is no offence against the fathers. It is the first time a President of the United States has said it in print, and McClellan's negotiators quote it in 1865 (new).
- **What the heads cover, and what they do not:** they are principles, and the map moves inside them. Washoe is retaken by arms the next spring, under the first reservation (G7), western Montana's camps turn west in 1863–64 (canon §10), and the peace adds the guano islands (canon §12). But no state is asked for, Oregon is put to its own vote, the price is paid in gold, and nothing is asked about the South (CR-062 T2, T4, T5, T13, O1). After November 1864, when Elias could have asked for more, he does not (G9).
- **Who gains:** Elias. The Terms do not move the Union's election, whose causes are in G3 (CR-065 W5). They decide what the election's winner can do with his victory: a President pledged to Union first finds the Western war already priced, signs an armistice within three months of taking office (canon §12) and turns to the South (CT14). It is the second cause of W1.

**G6b — Two houses. Elias opens, January 1864 (suggested).** *(New; rebuilt in revision 18 on the author's design of 11 November and approved; the facts cited are Hist. and were checked against the books named unless marked; CT20, CT27; the text is A12)*
- **His reading (MD2):** Lincoln answers an argument with an argument, and he is a reasonable man. The charge of the divided house is the strongest thing he has said. Elias does not answer it with a counter-charge. He owns it, and proposes the thing he is charged with as the better course for the Union. He expects a refusal (MD2: the man will not unsay what he has proclaimed) and wants the offer on the record before the Republic acts further (O1).
- **The move (PP8b):** *Two Houses*, a letter over his name. He was born at Franklin, in the Boonslick (canon §1), and he does keep the house divided, because both halves will prosper better apart. He states his doctrine as he stated it in 1861 (G2) and calls Jefferson: "keep them in union if it be for their good, but separate them if it be better" (to Breckinridge, 1803; research 37). He writes as a sovereign creditor to the head of a government, and he asks nothing for the slave and reproaches the Union with nothing. The argument has six parts.
  1. *What reunion by arms would be.* Missouri never left. It has been under martial law since August 1861 (Hist.; Fellman 66). On 5 October 1863 the President wrote of it: "Each man feels an impulse to kill his neighbor, lest he be first killed by him. Revenge and retaliation follow" (Hist.: to Drake and others, Basler 6:500, as quoted in Fellman 85). Six weeks before, his general ordered the people out of Jackson, Cass and Bates counties and half of Vernon, and about 20,000 left (Hist.: General Orders No. 11; Fellman 95). If a State that stayed is held so in the third year, eleven that fought will be held so for a generation.
  2. *What the Proclamation needs.* Freedom carried south by an army lasts as long as the army stays, because the North has not agreed to receive the freed. In June 1862 the President's own State voted by more than two to one to keep Black settlers out (Hist.; Voegeli 17), and his message of December 1862 asked whether the North could not "decide for itself, whether to receive them" (Hist.: Basler 5:535–536, as quoted in Voegeli 66–67). He states this as a fact about the Union's voters and makes no charge of it.
  3. *The Union quit of slavery.* Parted from the South, the Union owes the master no fugitive law, no seats in Congress counted on his slaves and no soldier to guard him: the old abolitionists' case for disunion (Hist.: Garrison's "No Union with Slaveholders", which named the surrender of fugitives, the slave representation in Congress and the suppression of slave risings as the North's part in slavery; checked, research 60). What slavery remains inside the Union's lines it can end by the President's own plan of compensation of December 1862 (M10).
  4. *The Union's own prosperity.* In the first regular session after the Southern members left, Congress passed the Homestead Act (signed 20 May 1862) and the Pacific Railway Act (signed 1 July 1862). Southern opposition in the Senate had stopped homestead bills through the 1850s, Buchanan vetoed the bill of 1860, and no Pacific railway bill could pass while the South opposed a central route (Hist.; checked, research 60). The war is paid for in paper (the Legal Tender Act of 1862, Hist.) and fought by drafted men (the Enrollment Act, Hist.), and gold stands at a premium in New York (CR-065 F1). At peace the coast's gold goes to New York again, the Republic pays in coin for the public domain of the West on the printed Terms (G6), and its ports stay open. This is the part addressed to the conservative men who would have let the "erring sisters go in peace" in 1860–61, to the Peace Democrats and to the Free-Soilers, and he names none of them. (Hist.: Greeley's *Tribune* argued in November and December 1860 that the cotton States should be let go in peace; the wording "erring sisters" was not found in a source and is the author's, research 60.)
  5. *The river.* If the President treats, the Republic will make free navigation of the Mississippi a condition of any further credit it gives at Richmond (new). It answers the inaugural's "one river". It is part of the offer and falls with it.
  6. *Finality.* The three governments are to guarantee one another's bounds as the peace fixes them, so that one separation does not license the next (new). It too is part of the offer and falls with it.
- **What becomes of the offer.** Lincoln rejects it whole (author, 11 November 2026). The war then goes worse for the North (G8), and in 1865 McClellan accepts terms less favourable to the Union than those refused in 1864: the armistice conventions of March to June 1865 (canon §12) carry no article on the river and no guarantee of bounds. Richmond keeps its tolls on the river until the treaty of 1871 (canon §13; K21; G12).
- **What he asks.** What he asked in the spring of 1862 (PP3c): an armistice on the lines held, and commissioners of the three governments. It is asked apart from the Terms of Monterey, whose fifth head asks nothing concerning the South (G6).
- **What he leaves out, by the author's decision.** Free movement of people between the houses, which constituencies on both sides have voted against. And any condition of free birth on Richmond: it would press Richmond too soon, and it would take away the cause of Grant's war (canon §13).
- **The answer (PP8c; argument only, approved; text OPEN):** Lincoln grants much, and refuses, as Elias's reading expected.
  - *What he grants.* That Missouri is as bad as he wrote; that his State voted as it did; that Congress has done in one year what it could not do in twenty. He does not dispute the gold or the river.
  - *Two houses.* He answers with his own published words: "Physically speaking, we cannot separate", and "Can aliens make treaties easier than friends can make laws?" (first inaugural; Hist., wording checked, research 57). The man who offers to be a good neighbour has his columns on American soil.
  - *The ground he will not sell.* The Southern States left because they lost an election. If a minority may leave whenever it loses, no election binds anyone, and the next minority will leave the remnant. The first inaugural said: "Plainly, the central idea of secession, is the essence of anarchy", and that if a minority secedes rather than acquiesce, "a minority of their own will secede from them". The message of 4 July 1861 said "that ballots are the rightful, and peaceful, successors of bullets" (all Hist.; wording checked against published texts, research 57; Basler's pages not confirmed). A guarantee of bounds by three governments is a treaty, and a treaty holds while the signers find it convenient. Every material thing the Protector offers may be true, and the Union is still not his to sell or the President's to part with.
  - *The Proclamation.* He will not unsay it for a river and a tariff of gold (CR-065 S6; G8).
- **Who gains:** Elias has put a reasonable offer in print in January of the Union's election year, before its parties have named their candidates, and the Union later settles for less. Its arguments are ones the Union's own Democrats and merchants already make (CT14; CT27). Lincoln has the refusal, on the ground he will stand on in November: he held the election, lost it and went home (G8). The Chronicler prints both letters whole and says that the offer was honest, that it was in the offerer's interest, and that he claims no part for it in the vote of November (CT14; CH3, CH4).
- **A tension left to the reader.** "Cannot the north decide for itself, whether to receive them?" is quoted by the founder as a fact about the Union. That spring Victoria takes control of entry at Hawaii's ports as her condition for the third renewal (CT8), and in 1888 she closes the Realm's door to the unskilled (canon §25). Nobody in the strand sets the two side by side (CH7).

**G7 — The lock. Lincoln opens, December 1863 – spring 1864.** *(CR-060; Port Townsend new; CT11)*
- **His reading (MD3, corrected by the Terms):** the Protector asks no state. Then make states.
- **The move:** his best-known device, a loyal government made from the loyal men there are: the restored government of Virginia in 1861, West Virginia in 1863, the amnesty proclamation of 8 December 1863 (Hist.; research 25). For the West: Nevada's Enabling Act and Colorado's, both of 21 March 1864 (Hist.; CR-060 C4, "Lincoln's lock"). For Oregon: the Unionists displaced to Port Townsend (CR-059 V3) and whichever of the delegates elected in June 1861 (CR-059 V5) will join them are to sit as Oregon's convention, under Casey's guns, and adopt the constitution the Enabling Act asks for (new). The device has never needed a quorum.
- **The answer:** Elias, on MD2, has expected it. In Washoe he does what the first reservation said he would (G6), and moves before the convention can meet: in the spring of 1864, with about 1,500 men and no fight, and the garrison marches out armed and unparoled on Sherman's terms (CR-060 C5–C6). In Oregon Lincoln has counted on Pacifica hindering the delegates, which would make the Port Townsend body the one free voice of Oregon. Elias publishes a safe-conduct and a passage for every delegate of 1861 who wishes to go (new). Few go. Most are Douglas Democrats, and the Enabling Act requires a constitution without the exclusion article (CR-059 E2), which they will not write in exile to please a Republican Senate.
- **How the lock fails:** Lincoln is beaten in his own Congress, as he was historically over Louisiana. Historically the Senate never seated R. King Cutler and Charles Smith, the senators-elect of Lincoln's Louisiana government; Lincoln returned Banks's statement to Trumbull's committee on 9 January 1865 and declined to take sides, framing the question as whether Louisiana could be brought into proper practical relations with the Union "sooner, by admitting or by rejecting the proposed Senators" (Hist.: Basler 8:206–207); Trumbull's report of 18 February 1865 (S. No. 117) never came to a vote; and the joint resolution of 8 February 1865 (H.R. 126) shut Louisiana out of the electoral count (Hist.: Basler 8:270). In this timeline New Orleans stays Confederate (G3), so there is no Louisiana government to reject. **Story assumption:** Port Townsend's convention is the Union's first test of the device. In the spring of 1864 it adopts a constitution and sends it to Washington with a memorial for admission. The Enabling Act requires that the people ratify the constitution (CR-059 E2), and the only poll the convention can hold is among the refugees and the garrison on the Sound. The Senate refers the memorial and the poll to committee. No bill of admission is brought to a vote, and the Wade–Davis bill passes on 2 July 1864 as in history (Hist. date). Lincoln answers as he answered Trumbull in 1865: he neither takes sides nor argues, and states the question (research 33). The safe-conducts have given the Senate a count: the delegates were free to come, and they stayed at home. The Radicals will not seat a state made by the Executive from a remnant, and Congress passes the restoration programme instead (CR-059 W12). Colorado's voters reject statehood on 11 October 1864 on their own, 4,672 to 1,520, chiefly over a slate of officers the convention had chosen in advance (Hist.; research 25).
- **Who gains:** split. Washoe, renounced at Carson in 1860 and left alone for three years (CR-060 C2), is retaken by arms in the season a state is called. The reservation had been in print for eight months, and Lincoln signed the Enabling Act knowing it. The Union's papers still have their sentence, that the man who asks no state marches the week one is called, and it is half true. Victoria attaches her conditions to the third renewal (CR-060 C6; CT8). From the safe-conducts Lincoln completes his second reading (MD4): this man binds himself in public where it pays him.

**G8 — Two ballot boxes. Lincoln opens, spring 1864.** *(Canon §12, CR-065; the offer, the reasons and the provisions new)*
- **His reading (MD4):** the man binds himself in public, and he has said in print that he can be dismissed. Monterey's Assembly holds war-weary merchants and American-born members with divided loyalties (canon §9), and Hawaii's assent to the third renewal falls due in the spring (working date late May, canon §9).
- **The move (PP9):** an offer to the people of Pacifica over the Protector's head, published through neutral papers in the weeks of the enabling acts, in time to reach Honolulu before the renewal. Leave the Columbia and the Sound, and the Union will waive its war claims, renew the commercial articles of 1848 and 1860 with the Confederation, compensate the sailor's family and admit Hawaiian sugar free of duty under those articles (historically the Union's reciprocity with Hawaii came only in 1875). It is the trial of the preface's claim. In September the State Department publishes Pacifican correspondence with Richmond, captured at sea, to draw an answer from Monterey before the vote (new; CT12).
- **That summer** Lincoln writes the blind memorandum of 23 August (CR-065 E1). He expects to lose, and he binds himself in advance to work with the man who beats him.
- **Why the Council and Hawaii hold.** An Assembly majority takes up the offer (canon §12). The Council and Hawaii's delegation do not, for reasons Elias puts to them and they can check (new).
  1. *The offer is a promise its maker cannot keep.* Free sugar and renewed articles need a treaty and two-thirds of the Senate. The man who offers them faces an election in November.
  2. *What it asks for is the reason of 1861.* To leave the Columbia and the Sound is to give the American squadron its stations back (G2), and with them the compact legislature at Salem, Deseret's road and the camps of Idaho and Montana.
  3. *The war now pays.* Washoe is retaken (G7), and the Comstock's royalty and the Idaho gold come to the common treasury. The merchants who sit in the Council hold its contracts.
  4. *Hawaii does not want that government nearer.* The delegation has read the account of the nations (G4). Lincoln offers a market, and the same Congress that would have to vote it removed the Dakota.
  5. *Elias gives now what Lincoln promises later.* The army and the fleet contract for Hawaiian sugar at a fixed price for the length of the war (new), and the heads of 1863 already keep the ports open to American trade at the peace (G6).
  6. *The safeguards.* Victoria has her fourth condition for the third renewal: Hawaii decides who lands and settles in the kingdom, and keeps its own quarantine (CT8). And Elias pledges that any armistice will be laid before the Assembly for its vote. The merchants who want peace are promised a vote on the peace, and lose their reason to vote on his powers. The majority brings in the revocation bill in the spring and holds it through the summer, waiting on the Union's campaign: only a re-elected Lincoln can deliver what the offer promises. In the first days of November it gives notice that it will take the final vote when the American result is known (the procedure is OPEN in canon §12; new).
- **Why Lincoln loses in November, and what Pacifica had to do with it.** CR-065 calls the swing cumulative (W5). Every part of it has a Pacifican hand, and none of those hands moves during the campaign.
  - *No victory on the Gulf.* New Orleans held in 1862 with Pacifican credit and engineers (G3), so the Union has no army on the Gulf, and Farragut waits at Pensacola through 1864 (CR-063 M2, M4). There is no Mobile Bay in August.
  - *No victory in Georgia.* Thomas is on the Chattahoochee in September, and Atlanta stands (CR-064 A9–A10). The general who took it in our history is Monterey's Inspector-General, and Johnston's army is fed and armed in part through Matamoros (canon §11).
  - *Dear money.* No treasure steamer has left San Francisco for New York since 1861, and gold stands near its highest premium through the campaign (CR-065 F1). Prices rise faster than wages in every Northern town.
  - *The draft.* The call of July for half a million men falls on a North that tens of thousands have already left for the West (G5).
  - *A second war with a printed price.* The War Democrats can say that the West may be closed on Monterey's own heads and the Union restored first (G6; CT14).
  - *No enemy to rally against.* After Lincoln's refusal in February (G6b) Elias makes no statement, spends no money and orders no operation (CR-065 P1–P2; CT27). The published correspondence finds no answer, and the Democrats use it for their own case, that the Union fights two enemies and can finish neither.
  From the conventions to the vote the Republic did nothing in the Union's campaign, and nearly everything before it.
- **What Elias expects, and what he provides.** His reading (MD2) is that Lincoln will hold the vote and neither unsay the Proclamation nor cheat. Sherman agrees. Elias treats that as a forecast and builds nothing on it. He provides for the three ways it can fail, and each provision is already in print (new).
  1. *If Lincoln takes Raymond's road* and offers Richmond peace on Union alone (CR-065 S6): the road is closed at the far end. The Matamoros credit, settled by Elias in person in Texas in 1863 (canon §11), is lent for independence and falls due if Richmond treats for reunion (the clause new; printed in Texas and again with the answer at Honolulu, B11, PP4c). Davis will not take reunion on any terms (Hist.). Lincoln would give up the Proclamation and get nothing for it.
  2. *If Lincoln wins,* by Atlanta or by a managed count: the winter plan, announced in the second reservation of 1863 (G6). Vance's force is staged on the Sound, to take Port Townsend after the fourth of March if a President who will not treat is sworn that day, and not before (CR-065 P2 holds). Pacifica's envoys in London and Paris have the powers' recognition of Richmond prepared, to follow Pacifica's own, which comes in the spring whoever is President (canon §12 has all three in 1865, Pacifica's first). A re-elected Lincoln would then hold no foot of the Pacific coast and would face the blockade as a question between himself and Europe. *What "will not treat" means:* the test is printed in the Council's resolution of 12 November 1864, with the plan; the reservation of 1863 says only "a President who will not treat". A President will treat if his inaugural address, or a note from his Secretary of State received at Monterey, offers to name commissioners on the heads of 1863. Vance does not judge it. His packet orders him to be ready, and he moves only on a second order from Monterey, which cannot be written before the inaugural has been read there, some ten days after the fourth of March (research 38). If neither an inaugural nor a note has made the offer by the first of May, the second order goes. CR-065 M1 gives McClellan's inaugural no sentence on the West; the author will revise that draft.
  3. *If the Union comes west after all:* it must come by Panama, against the turret ships, or across a thousand miles of plains and passes held by Deseret (CR-060) and by nations under compact (G4). The heads of 1863 stay on the table for a Senate that wants gold (G11). It would be a longer and dearer war, and one he expects to win.
- **The blind vote (CT17).** The Union votes on Tuesday 8 November. Monterey knows the day, and knows that the result cannot reach the coast for some days. The earliest it could come is about the 15th, by the Union's wire to Denver, a rider over the mountains to Salt Lake and Deseret's wire west; it reaches San Francisco on Thursday the 17th (all of it subject to research 38). The safe course is to wait: Elias's own forecast is that Lincoln has lost, and the news will take the American offer out of the majority's hands. He does not wait.
  1. *What he asks.* The Council appointed him in 1861 by two-thirds of its members (canon §§8–9). On Wednesday the 9th he asks it to vote again on Saturday the 12th, before any result can arrive: would it appoint him today? He sets himself the same two-thirds, and pledges to lay down the office that night if he falls short. Nothing in the Articles requires the vote, and it changes no law. It binds him by his own word, given in the chamber and printed. His ground: the Americans are not to choose the Republic's government.
  2. *The numbers.* The Council has eighteen members, nine from each state (canon §8 says only that the delegations are equal; the number is new). He needs twelve. Seven votes against him end his office, and there can be no tie.
  3. *He is advised against it.* Sherman tells him that Lincoln will prove to have held an honest election and lost it, and that he has only to wait (V4). It is a friend's advice, and it is right about Lincoln.
  4. *He lays the winter plan on the table.* The second reservation said what the Republic would do after the fourth of March (G6). He now gives the Council the particulars: Vance's force and its orders, the envoys' powers in London and Paris, the cost. If Lincoln has won, this is the war they are voting for. If he has lost, the heads of 1863 stand as printed.
  5. *He says beforehand what he will do in either case.* With fewer than twelve votes he goes that night, the Council names another Protector or none, and he will not call on the army. With twelve he will dissolve the Assembly as soon as the Union's result is known, whatever it is, and ask the electors for an Assembly to make the peace or the winter's war with. He says this before the vote, so that the dissolution is an appeal he has announced and not a way out.
  6. *Hawaii's price is named before the roll is called.* Victoria is at Honolulu, and her delegation at Monterey acts on her standing instructions (canon §8 has a resident Hawaiian delegation). Its head states the condition before any member votes: any armistice is to be laid before Hawaii's legislature as well as the Assembly, and binds the Confederation only with its assent. The peace itself already needs both founding states (CR-062 O6); what the condition adds is the armistice. Elias accepts it in the chamber, and Honolulu ratifies it afterwards. Her reasons are a ruler's. The sugar treaty needs two-thirds of a Senate she cannot reach. She does not want the American squadron back on the Columbia. And a Protector confirmed by her delegation's votes owes her what no American treaty would. The instrument is a clause of the Council's resolution of 12 November 1864, carried at the peace into the protected provisions of the Enabling Law beside the entry condition (CT8).
  7. *California votes first and divides.* Five of its nine vote for him. The four against are two constitutional men and two peace men. The first two hold the opinion that called the Confederation one man's rule in 1856, when California ratified the Articles narrowly (canon §8), and they will not confirm a one-year office in its fourth year. The other two want the American offer heard before anything else is settled. He has carried his own state by one vote, and five is not twelve.
  8. *The division.* Hawaii gives him seven of its nine, with the mission party the two. Twelve to six, the bare two-thirds. The vote that could have undone him was California's: with one more against him there, Hawaii's seven would not have been enough.
  9. *The Assembly's bill* is not touched by the vote. The majority still holds it. When the news comes the offer is dead and the majority is not: it still threatens to revoke his measures and restrict the programme (canon §12). What stops it is the dissolution he has announced, and the electors then judge him within sixty days (canon §8).
- **The outcome.** The news comes afterwards: on 8 November Lincoln lost an honest election (CR-065 E6). No second order goes to Vance: the President sworn on 4 March will treat, the force is stood down, and Port Townsend is bought in the peace (CR-062). The Assembly's majority is still there, with an offer whose author can no longer deliver it. Elias now dissolves the Assembly, as he told the Council he would, before the bill is enacted (canon §12); the new one sits when the armistice comes in the spring, and it is laid before it as he pledged. He campaigns on a war that is ending on published terms, on paid contracts and unfinished works, and wins a more favourable Assembly.
- **The mirror.** On 23 August Lincoln bound himself in writing before he knew the result (CR-065 E1). In November Elias did the same before his Council. The Chronicler sets the two papers side by side. One man expected to lose and pledged himself to his successor. The other expected to win and would not take the win as a gift.
- **The danger:** it was at home, and in November he chose it. Seven votes would have ended it, and he had promised to go.
- **Who gains:** Elias. Lincoln has the result of his trial, and it is not the one he looked for. The Protector was not dismissed, and he had offered himself for dismissal when he could have waited to be saved. A year later he is Providentor for life (canon §12). Lincoln's second reading holds in the main and fails in this (MD4). The Chronicler's judgment is that the founder would not owe his office to his rival's defeat, and saw to it that he did not.
- **Lincoln's words on it** are his historical ones of 10 November, said here in defeat: "We can not have free government without elections; and if the rebellion could force us to forego, or postpone a national election, it might fairly claim to have already conquered and ruined us" (CR-065 L2; Hist.: response to a serenade, 10 November 1864; research 31).

**G9 — No signature. Lincoln opens, December 1864.** *(CR-065 L2, L7; the passage and the answer new)*
- **His reading (MD4):** what the man wants from him is the verdict. A cession Lincoln signed would be one. A cession his successor signs is only a peace.
- **The move (PP10):** his annual message reads the vote as a vote for Union (CR-065 L2) and says of the West that the Executive has no power to cede it and will not treat for it; that belongs to his successor and the Senate. He keeps the memorandum's pledge to McClellan in everything else and opens no talks with Monterey (CR-065 L1, L7).
- **The answer:** first, the heads of 1863, unchanged, at Elias's own high tide: no state, Oregon by its own vote, gold, the war claims waived on both sides (CR-062 T9), the South left alone. Second, he settles what the Republic will remember of the contest (PP11; K27). From 1865 its schools read *Two Houses*, the founder's letter to the President. Lincoln's own speeches, the Lyceum address and Peoria, go to the history courses of the colleges. The founder does his rival the scholar's honour. The readers do not print Lincoln's reply. The winner writes the history, as winners do, and the Chronicler says so (W4).
- **Who gains:** Lincoln, on the point he chose: the cession is never his, and the Chronicler calls it the contest's best single move (M13). Elias has the memory: within a generation the Pacifican public knows Lincoln about as well as Americans of our world know Davis or Lee, and knows him through the founder's paper (K27). MD4 holds.

**G10 — The letter to Oregon. Lincoln opens, June 1866.** *(CR-059; the letter new; CT6)*
- **His reading (MD4, confirmed by 1865):** the man will bind himself to Oregon in anything, and will not give up the power.
- **The move (PP12):** a letter in the *Oregonian* (Hist.: Pittock's Republican daily since 1861, edited from 1865 by Harvey W. Scott) to Oregon's Republicans and Union men. Oregon, never admitted, cast no vote for him in 1860 (CR-059 H7); these are the men who would have. It quotes the preface of 1863 against the life office of 1865. Then the Protector could be dismissed every twelvemonth. Who dismisses the Providentor? Lincoln was voted out in the middle of a war and went home. In which country would Oregon rather be able to lose an election? (Argument only; text OPEN.)
- **The answer:** a bond, as Lincoln expected, and an answer, which he did not expect (PP12b; argument only, text OPEN). The charter of accession was already going through as a constitutional amendment, ratified by both founding states that summer, a protected provision beyond the Enabling Law's cabinet power, amendable only with the consent of Oregon's legislature (CR-059 V10). Nesmith calls it "a charter no cabinet can touch" (CR-059 Part VI). Elias sends no speakers (CR-059 V11). But he answers the letter in print, over his name. He does not pretend that Oregon could dismiss him. The office was asked for in public and voted by both legislatures after the peace; a founder's work is not a magistrate's term; and what Oregon can hold him to is the charter, which he cannot touch. The Chronicler records that the answer concedes Lincoln's point.
- **Who gains:** Elias has the vote, by about 51 to 49 (CR-059 V15, MODEL). Lincoln holds the Union men and moves no one else. He was right about his rival and lost. From 1867 the charter is tested by the grants Elias makes to county boards over the legislatures' heads (CR-059 S3; canon §12): he bound the cabinet and went round it by the counties.
- **The result is the occasion of Lincoln's regret** (K5a).

**G11 — The creditor. Elias opens, 1865–1867.** *(CR-062; Lincoln's answer new)*
- **His reading (MD2, applied to the Union Lincoln left):** the war was paid for in paper (the Legal Tender Act of 1862, Hist.). The Treasury needs gold to resume, and the Republicans in the Senate want resumption more than they want the Columbia. Lincoln will speak, as he spoke to Oregon, and he will call the cession unlawful or shameful, as the *Tribune*'s friends do. That would cost him the Senate's moderates.
- **The move:** $40 million in gold, the first instalment lying assayed in the New York Subtreasury before the Senate votes (CR-062 T4, S2), and a separate Treasury loan (canon §12). Eastern financiers become parties to the settlement. The purchase money is the founder's payment to the country he was born in for the land he has taken from it, and he calls it that. The loan is a creditor's hold on that country, and the Chronicler calls it that.
- **The answer (PP13):** Lincoln concedes the law to his enemy. His letter in the *Chicago Tribune*, published while the Senate debates (L3), grants that the cession is lawful under Article IV, and argues only that it is unwise. Then it turns Elias's own rule on him. In 1848 the Protector refused the United States' guarantee because a guarantor has a right to come in (canon §4). A creditor has the same right. Take the purchase money and refuse the loan. The man who voted in Illinois to table repudiation (Beveridge 1:276, 286) tells his country to pay what it owes and to owe this man nothing.
- **Who gains:** Elias has the treaty: the Senate ratifies (canon §12). The loan passes smaller and shorter than offered (CR-062 M3 is to follow). Lincoln has the sentence that a generation of Union readers repeats (K23; CR-048). MD2 was wrong in detail again: Elias expected an attack on the law and met his own maxim.

**G12 — The writ. A petitioner opens, autumn 1871.** *(Changes canon §13's proposed override; the petitioner's claim, the letter to Springfield and Lincoln's opinion new; CT18)*
- **Who opens.** Neither man. A person who has got past the border, has reached San Francisco and is held for return under the new fugitive agreement petitions a Pacifican court for the writ, and says what the enslaved have said since 1863: free by Lincoln's paper (K26). The petitioner is Bass Reeves, born a slave in Arkansas in 1838 and raised in Texas, who in our history escaped into the Indian Territory during the war and was later a deputy United States marshal (Hist.; to verify, research 42). Here Texas is still slave country in 1871, and he goes west. He gets through the controls at the Texas line and reaches San Francisco, where Richmond's consul claims him under the agreement, and that brings him before a court there. It is the only round opened by one of those whom neither man asked.
- **The letter to Springfield.** The coloured citizens of San Francisco, who wrote to Lincoln in 1863 and had his reply (PP4), write again and ask the firm of Lincoln & Herndon for an opinion.
- **Lincoln's move (PP14; argument only, text OPEN).** He tells counsel not to plead his Proclamation: it was a war measure of a government whose writ does not run at San Francisco, and a foreign court owes it nothing. Plead the Republic's own law. Its prohibition of slavery stands (canon §13). And in 1863 the Republic printed its terms of passage under the President's own sentence from Peoria, that no man is good enough to govern another without that other's consent (PP5; G5). The founder made that sentence his measure of the Union's draft. Lincoln makes it the Republic's measure. He sets his own paper aside to win a client's case on his enemy's statutes. It is G11's stratagem, used for one person.
- **The answer: hospitality (new; CT18).** The judge grants the writ. That evening the ministers have ready the override that the agreement with Richmond calls for, under the enabling law. The Providentor does not let it be moved. He has read the brief, which is built on his own statutes and his own printed terms, and he answers it with a rule older than either. The man reached the Republic and stood before its court. He is the Republic's guest, and the Republic does not give up a guest. He says so in print, over his name. And he pays for his guest. The Republic offers Richmond the valuation of the petitioner, and by a convention of that year the fugitive agreement gains an article: a person who reaches a Pacifican court is ransomed at a board's valuation and is not returned (new). *The board:* three members sitting at San Francisco, a judge named by the Republic, Richmond's consul, and an umpire the two choose from the neutral consuls, as in the mixed commissions of the time (Hist. pattern; to verify). It is the method of the President's plan of 1849 (Beveridge 2:184–185), which he named at Honolulu (PP4c). Sherman had put in writing that he would oppose that use of a delegated power (canon §13), and has nothing left to oppose.
- **The cabinet's reaction, and Victoria's support (CT25).** Hospitality without a limit would end the settlement of 1871. The Republic's credit was lent for Richmond's independence (B11; X14), its guarantee of 1871 secures that credit, and the fugitive agreement is Richmond's price for the association treaties (new; canon §13 gives the agreement and the treaties in the same years and does not link them). So the cabinet does the pragmatic thing. Over the winter of 1871–72 it sets travel-paper controls at the Texas line, the railway and the Gulf ports (canon §13), so that few will reach a court and the ransom stays payable. Victoria supports the controls, through her standing instructions to Hawaii's delegation: they are her own entry policy of 1864 applied to the continent. The founder lets his cabinet do it, and it is his government's act (O5). The Republic is hard at its border and keeps faith with every guest who reaches it. The Chronicler adds what a ransom concedes: to pay an owner at a valuation is to treat his claim as property. What that leaves out is for the critics of 1888 to say (canon §25).
- **The same year.** In the spring Richmond reopens the Mississippi in the treaty that Pacifica guarantees (K21). Elias makes free navigation the guarantor's condition (new). Lincoln has his river from his enemy's hand in the year in which his client goes free, and the Chronicler counts both among the founder's payments to the country he was born in.
- **Who gains.** Both, the only time. Lincoln wins a case in his enemy's court on his enemy's law, and takes no fee. Elias answers a brief built on his own printed terms with his own hospitality, and pays for it himself. The rival's last move makes the victor better, which is what the nomos says a rival is for (CH1). In 1861 the Chronicler wrote that the despot kept the forms better than the democrat, and promised to come to the founder's later years (A3b). He comes to them in Hawaii (M15), and not here.

**Not a round: Deseret.** Lincoln signs the Morrill Act in July 1862, Young asks Monterey for protection, and the Compact of Association follows in December (CR-060 D2–D5). Lincoln's word through Stenhouse that he will leave the Mormons alone comes too late (Hist.: June 1863, Stenhouse to Young, 7 June; research 21). It is his own mistake, and neither man plays it against a reading of the other. It stays in CR-060 and M11.

**Where Elias could have lost.**
- **Monterey, August 1861 (G2).** If he had refused Lincoln's offer alone, or had found no reasons Hawaii could hold as its own, Hawaii would have had every reason to withhold the first renewal.
- **The autumn of 1862 (G4).** He widened his commitment to Richmond weeks before the Proclamation made it a reproach. In May 1863 Honolulu could have let the office lapse (CT16).
- **Monterey, November 1864 (G8).** Seven votes of eighteen would have sent him home on the day he named, and he had promised to go.
- **Georgia, September 1864.** If Thomas had taken Atlanta before the vote, Lincoln would probably have won it, and Elias would have had the winter plan and a longer war (G8).

**Where Lincoln could have won.** In 1862, if New Orleans had fallen (G3): the sequence would have held, and the West would have met the whole army in 1865. In 1864, in Georgia. Not by Raymond's road, which Elias had closed at Richmond, and not surely by managing the count, for which Elias had the winter plan (G8). Lincoln refused both in any case. The Chronicler's judgment is that the two refusals cost Lincoln nothing he could have kept, and are the more to his credit because he did not know it.

### Part H — The telling of the contest: the founder's conduct, five acts, uneven stakes, a chain of consequences, changes of scale (O1–O6, TL1–TL10, B1–B21 with B12b, X1–X16)

The author's three principles for the telling: "The stakes are unevenly distributed. Each move changes what can happen next. The telling changes scale and pace." And the aim: "The idea is for Elias to emerge from the contest as a victorious Pindaric hero."

**What this part is.** Part G is the ledger: who did what, on what reading, with what paper. Part H says how the contest is told, and it is what the beat sheet is generated from. Where the two differ on emphasis, Part H governs.

#### The founder's conduct (O1–O6)

The Pacifican nomos already has the form, the contest with declared terms (CR-049), and these rules apply it to the founder himself. The reading behind them is Pindar's (research 54; none of his wording is quoted): the founder of a city is the highest subject; a victory counts when it is won by a deed done in the open and at risk; measure in victory is the rarest excellence; the rival's worth is the victor's crown; and the song ends with a warning to the man who has won. The rules change how the founder wins. They leave what he wants alone: the war for the West, the hand under Richmond, the dissolution, the life office and the shut border are all still his.

**Two asymmetries run under the table.** The first is attention. From 1861 to 1864 Lincoln is the only opponent Elias has, and Elias is the second of Lincoln's wars: Hay's diary gives the Pacific an hour in a week (new; text to be written). What Elias wants of him is to be answered (MD4), and that is why. After March 1865 it turns over: Lincoln has nothing but time and writes to Oregon, to the Senate and to a court at San Francisco, and the Providentor answers twice: Oregon in 1866 (PP12b) and the brief of 1871 (G12). ### §9 — at the end of the paragraph beginning "Demands and attempted interim arrangements"

> <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's offer, the two pages and his answer, the reply laid before the governments, the state of war and Baker --> **[APPROVED — CR-068]** The offer of release and investigation is Lincoln's own, made in public through Baker. Pacifica's papers have already printed his first inaugural's sentence on the perpetual Union beside his speech of 1848 on the right of revolution, without comment, and his message of 4 July answers them: the right belongs to a people who can use it themselves and can name their wrong. Elias lays the offer and his reply before the Council before he answers, with seven reasons that are printed with the reply; the last is that the Republic will not see the South conquered and the Pacific taken next. He offers to accept and stand down if the Council prefers the offer. ### §11 — after the paragraph beginning "Sherman refuses an assignment sustaining Confederate armies"

> <!-- CR-068: the armistice offer, the Proclamation, the answer at Honolulu, the officer kept and the terms of passage --> **[APPROVED — CR-068]** In the spring of 1862 Elias proposes a general armistice of the three governments on the lines they hold. Washington does not answer him, and a published circular of the State Department says that the United States will hear no proposal from a foreign power. He then widens the Republic's credit at Richmond and publishes its condition, that its war advances end when both governments accept an armistice. Elias keeps Sherman as Inspector-General after Sherman refuses the Southern assignment. In January 1863 Lincoln answers a memorial from San Francisco's coloured citizens in a public letter. Pacifica's government replies with a published account, in English, Spanish and Hawaiian, that sets its treaties with the Native nations since 1851 and the ending of peonage in New Mexico beside the hanging of thirty-eight Dakota at Mankato and the removal of the Dakota and the Winnebago from Minnesota. Lincoln does not answer it. In Texas, in February 1863, Elias has the terms of the credit printed: it is lent for independence and falls due if Richmond treats for reunion. He is back at Monterey in March. In April Hawaii's legislature puts the renewal of the Protectorship over. In May he goes to Honolulu and tells the legislature that the credit is lent at interest so that the South is not conquered and the Pacific taken next, that the Republic makes no condition about slavery, and that it will lend or stand surety on the customs for compensated emancipation when Richmond asks; the renewal passes in the last week of the term. In the spring Pacifica's government prints its terms of passage for men leaving the Union's draft under Lincoln's Peoria sentence on consent, and Lincoln answers in a public letter on the draft.

### §11 — after the paragraph beginning "Grant and Porter bypass the Vicksburg defenses"

> <!-- CR-068: the Terms of Monterey --> **[APPROVED — CR-068]** In August 1863, the month after Vicksburg and Gettysburg, Elias publishes the Terms of Monterey. Pacifica asks no state of the Union. Each territory's future is settled by its own inhabitants. The public domain is paid for in gold. The ports stay open to American commerce. Nothing is asked concerning the South. The terms will be neither raised nor lowered by the fortunes of the war. Two reservations are printed with them: the Republic holds itself free to occupy any territory that Congress passes an act to make a state before the peace, and if on 4 March 1865 the Union has a President who will not treat on the heads, it will take the last American station on the Sound. Its recognition of the government at Richmond is no term of this peace and waits on no armistice. A preface addressed to the people of the United States answers Lincoln's Lyceum address of 1838 and cites Jefferson's letter to Astor on a free and independent empire on the Pacific. Lincoln replies in a public letter that autumn. He does not dispute Jefferson, and he charges the Protector with keeping the American house divided. In the winter Elias answers over his own name in a letter called *Two Houses*, which argues that the Union would prosper better parted from the South, offers free navigation of the Mississippi as a condition of any further credit from the Republic at Richmond and a guarantee of bounds by the three governments, and asks again for an armistice and commissioners. Lincoln refuses early in 1864: a minority that may leave after losing an election ends government by election. The offer lapses, and neither the river nor the guarantee is in the armistice of 1865.

### §12 — at the end of the paragraph beginning "In the 1864–1865 consolidation struggle"

> <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's offer of 1864, the blind vote and the dissolution --> **[APPROVED — CR-068]** The majority's occasion is Lincoln's offer to the people of Pacifica, published in the spring of 1864 through neutral papers: the war claims waived, the commercial articles renewed, the sailor's family compensated and a treaty admitting Hawaiian sugar free of duty, if Pacifica leaves the Columbia and the Sound. Victoria uses it to win her conditions for the third renewal, among them Hawaii's control of entry at its own ports. Elias pledges to lay any armistice before the Assembly, and the army and fleet contract for Hawaiian sugar at a fixed price for the length of the war. After Lincoln's refusal in February he makes no statement and orders no operation during the Union's campaign. After the Union has voted on 8 November and before the result can reach the Pacific, he asks the Council to confirm him by the two-thirds that appointed him, lays before it what the Republic will do if Lincoln is re-elected, undertakes to resign if he falls short, and announces that if he is confirmed he will dissolve the Assembly once the result is known. Twelve of the Council's eighteen members vote for him, five of California's nine and seven of Hawaii's, on Victoria's condition that any armistice also be laid before Hawaii's legislature. The news of Lincoln's defeat arrives afterwards. Elias then dissolves the Assembly, and the armistice of 1865 is laid before the new one. A force staged on the Sound to take Port Townsend is stood down in March 1865, when McClellan's inaugural shows that he will treat.

### CT17 — The blind vote, November 1864 (CR-065 P2; canon §§8, 12; G8; Part H B15)

### CT26 — The London pledge released by 1859 (canon §§5, 8; CR-025; CR-033; CR-035; G2, G4)

**Current:** "The republic's mining revenue finances infrastructure, but its debt to London remains. British bondholders hold claims secured by gold royalties and San Francisco customs … Whether he will retain the ability to pay becomes a question not only of wealth but of sovereignty" (§5). **Adapted:** the debt stays. Through the war the Republic is London's debtor and Richmond's creditor at once, and dearer credit in London is one cost of the Proclamation (G4, as revision 16 had it).
**Amended:** by 1859 the Republic has redeemed the pledge of its gold royalties and of San Francisco's customs. That much is decided; whether the whole debt is repaid by then is left to the model, which the author expects to allow it. The Republic enters the crisis of 1860 and the war with no foreign creditor holding its revenues. The motive is the founder's own: his father's holding was lost under Missouri law (CR-066), and in 1848 he refused Buchanan's guarantee because "a guarantor has a right to come in" (canon §4). From 1871 the Republic co-administers Richmond's customs (canon §§13, 25) as London once held San Francisco's.
**What follows in this CR.** The bondholders' warning leaves 1860: Britain's refusal stands, and Victoria's coalition wins at Carson without it (G2). Lincoln's reading of 1862 has no London credit in it, the Proclamation costs the Republic nothing in London, and what it costs is Hawaii's renewal and Sherman (G4; the weights; B8; CT16). The bonds that Pacifica's companies sell in London, Paris and Amsterdam before 1914 are company debt and are not touched (canon §§19, 30).
**Other canon passages to reword at integration.** The debt is the "approximately £3 million of Mexican obligations to London bondholders" assumed at Querétaro (§4), and §4's "Debt gives British creditors a stake in the republic's survival and a dangerous lever over its finances" stays true until 1859. The CR-028 summary has "the bondholders" hold the settlement of 1848 "until secession"; from 1859 they no longer do. §9's "Naval purchases and equipment still depend on foreign credit and supplies" (1861) and §12's "foreign credit and diplomacy" need a word on whose credit, since CR-025 dropped the London tranche of 1862. CR-063 CT5 names "credit (in London)". The CT-ELIAS entry is to say that he cleared the pledge.
**What the financial model shows (story case, as saved on the branch; read on 11 November 2026, nothing changed).** The debt taken over in 1848 is $14.6m (£3m at $4.87; Events E01). With the model's own rules, 2% of the debt repaid each year and four-fifths of every surplus used to repay more, public debt falls from $17.0m at the end of 1850 to $3.40m at the end of 1858 and $1.59m at the end of 1859. The Treasury also issued about $3.15m of its own bonds in 1846–52, so $17.75m was issued in all and $16.16m has been repaid by the end of 1859. If repayments go to the London bonds first, the whole £3m is cleared in the course of 1859, with no lever changed and nothing taken from the works of the 1850s; what is left is $1.59m of the Treasury's own bonds. Clearing those as well would take $1.59m of the $3.29m Reserve Fund, which the war then draws on in 1861–62. Full repayment of London by 1859 is therefore feasible on the model as it stands. Not modelled: the price at which the bondholders would sell before maturity, and their consent.
**Left for integration:** the financial modelling (the size of the debt, the schedule of redemption, and what it takes in the 1850s from Carquinez, the Calculation Office of 1855 and the Laboratory of 1859); the choice between releasing the pledge and repaying in full; the year and form of the redemption, and whether it is a beat; one sentence each for the Land Fund (CR-033) and the school's share (CR-035) after 1859; and the wording of §§5 and 8.
**Decision (author, 11 November 2026):** amend. At least the pledge is released by 1859; the modelling waits for integration.

**Decided by the author on 11 November and written into the rows in revision 18.**
- ***Two Houses* is a proposal and not an indictment.** Elias writes as a reasonable man trying to persuade another to do a reasonable thing, from the position of a sovereign creditor. He argues that disunion is good for the North: its Congress is free of the South's veto, it is quit of slavery's service, its trade and its gold come back with peace, and the three governments guarantee one another's bounds. The argument runs with the old abolitionists, the conservative business men who would have let the "erring sisters go in peace", the Peace Democrats and the Free-Soilers. He can make free navigation of the Mississippi a condition of the Richmond credit. Lincoln refuses it on the one ground that cannot be bought, that a minority which may leave after losing an election ends government by election.
- **Not in the proposal:** free movement between the houses ("too radical for this historical moment", and against the stated preferences of constituencies on both sides), and a free-birth condition on Richmond (it "puts too much pressure on Richmond too soon and removes the cause for Grant's War").
- The author is revising *Two Houses* and Lincoln's reply by hand.
