## What canon says now

- **The 1850s.** "Private bullion belongs to miners, merchants and investors who trade across borders." Pacifican banks "increasingly connect mineral production with domestic industry and overseas suppliers rather than providing an assured stream into the American financial system". The figures stay OPEN (§8).
- **The war.** Demands and interim arrangements "precede a July offensive on the working calendar" in 1861 (§9).
  - "The loss of dependable access to Californian gold and Pacifican credit becomes a continuing weakness in the Union's war finances … Gold can still reach Northern buyers through private and third-country transactions, including London; access costs more and depends on exchange, credit and confidence."
  - Customs duties and public-debt interest stay payable in coin.
  - **[OPEN]**: "alternate financial legislation, bullion and exchange flows, borrowing terms, price effects and the quantitative burden" (§9).
- **Ralston.** "Ralston's commercial relationships help bridge the gap between future public revenues and immediate payments to suppliers, workers and carriers."
  - **[OPEN]**: particular advances, security, interest, payment crises and dates.
  - "Ralston's historical 1864 Bank of California must not be silently placed in the early war."
  - An emergency advance "remains a possible incident" (§10).
  - CT-RALSTON: "His firm is not automatically a central bank or owner of public gold … A banking failure, rescue, monopoly or repetition of his historical fate is not approved by this role." The proposed dramatic interest is to "expand profitable influence while managing a dependence on public confidence".
- **The college.** Ralston College is founded in 1866 by San Francisco's merchant and banking houses of every faith and bears his name as its chief founder. "His fortune is the Comstock's silver" (§19, CR-050-R1). His later fate stays open.
- **The Gold Room.**
  - Gold stands "near its highest premium" through the 1864 campaign, and "no treasure steamer has left San Francisco for New York since 1861" (CR-068 G8).
  - By August 1864 the North has "gold at its wartime peak … and the Pacific coast and its gold are gone" (candidate §12, CR-065).
  - On McClellan's election "the premium falls sharply: the market prices peace and the return of the treasure steamers" (CR-065 F1, author).
  - *Two Houses* offers that at peace "the coast's gold goes to New York again" (CR-068 G6b).
- **At peace.** "Eastern financiers become participants in the settlement; Elias, once dispossessed by creditors, becomes creditor to the Union's bankers" (candidate §12).
- **Not in canon:**
  - when and how the Union's banks suspend;
  - what the premium does when the steamers stop;
  - whether Pacifica's banks keep paying in gold;
  - who in San Francisco decides where the coast's gold goes;
  - how the Republic's war bonds are taken up.

## What this CR does

1. It fixes the Union's money story from the moment the steamers stop:
   - a Treasury that stops paying its own notes before the banks suspend;
   - a higher premium;
   - a gold market that moves on Pacifican news;
   - a little relief by way of London, at a price.
2. It makes Ralston the man who keeps the coast's gold at home, at a cost he can count:
   - he keeps his doors open in the run of January 1862, with his own house at the head of the line;
   - he gives the Protector the Steamer Day Agreement in place of the export ban Elias wanted, so the Republic owes the houses for a measure it did not decree;
   - he leads San Francisco's houses in taking the Republic's war bonds in coin;
   - he refuses the temptation of the war, the Union's cheap paper bought with gold, and in 1864 publishes the New York offer he refused;
   - he parts with his partner over it in 1864 and founds the Bank of California;
   - at peace he lends the Republic's money to the Union's bankers as its agent.

   The college of 1866 then bears the name of a man the city has watched keep his word. His later fate stays open.

The model already carries the Pacifican side: F18, L17 and E14 (phase 5, approved on 14 November). The CR adds no workbook change.

## Scene (voice sample; not storyline text, not canon)

### S1. Steamer day: New York and San Francisco, winter 1861–62

The Chronicler's register, as in CR-068's samples. Lincoln's words to Meigs are his own; the remark on "two nations" is reported, its primary source still to verify (research task 6); the bankers' memorial is paraphrased, not quoted; the clerk is invented and unnamed.

> For ten years the mail steamer from Aspinwall had come up to her pier on the North River twice a month with the coast's gold in her strong room, and on the day she was due the banks' porters waited on the pier with handcarts. The last treasure she brought was in July 1861. After that she brought passengers, letters and San Francisco newspapers, and the porters stopped coming. A clerk at one of the Wall Street banks kept the arrivals in a column. In December he ruled it off: eleven steamers, no treasure.
>
> On the sixteenth of November the banks of New York agreed to take the third fifty millions of Mr. Chase's loan, and then told him how. They would credit the Treasury on their books and honour its drafts; they would not send the coin to the Sub-Treasury, because the coin did not come back. In August it had come back: the Treasury paid it to its contractors, the contractors banked it, and the steamers made up what went abroad. Now nothing made it up. That week the news came that a Union captain had taken two Confederate commissioners off a British mail steamer, and Wall Street remembered the boarding of a Hawaiian packet in the spring, and what it had cost in July. Mr. Chase would not have his loan in bank credit. For five weeks he paid the army in his own notes, in part and late. The custom house took them at their face, but the shops did not, and they went to a discount before a single bank had suspended. On the day after Christmas the government gave up the commissioners to Britain. On the thirtieth the banks of New York stopped paying in gold.
>
> On Friday the tenth Lincoln walked into the Quartermaster-General's office in great distress. "General, what shall I do? The people are impatient; Chase has no money and he tells me he can raise no more; the General of the Army has typhoid fever. The bottom is out of the tub. What shall I do?" That month, by one account, he spoke of the bare possibility of their being two nations. He meant the North and the South. The coast had gone in July, and he did not count it.
>
> On Saturday the eleventh a deputation of New York bankers, with James Gallatin at its head, met Mr. Chase and the committees of both houses at the Treasury. They asked for what they had asked before, that the Treasury keep its money in their banks and draw on it there. Their memorial asked for one thing more. Before the war, it said, the coast had sent New York forty millions a year in gold; no loan the banks could make would replace it; the steamers would sail again on the day the government made terms with Monterey. The conference broke up without agreement, and Mr. Chase carried the memorial to the President.
>
> On Monday the thirteenth gold was bought and sold for paper in New York for the first time. The bankers went home without an answer, and they had it on the twenty-fifth of February, when Lincoln signed the bill that made the Treasury's notes lawful money for every debt but the duties on imports and the interest on the public debt. The Union would pay for its war in paper rather than make terms for the coast's gold. From that day the price of gold in New York was the price of that choice, and it was quoted every hour the Exchange was open.
>
> On the first of March, the next steamer day at San Francisco, no ship cleared for New York either. That afternoon the houses of Montgomery Street signed William Ralston's agreement, and their coin stayed in their vaults, where the Republic's war bonds would find it in the spring.

### S2. The run on the Sub-Treasury: New York, December 1861

> The notes Mr. Chase paid the army with said on their face that the United States promised to pay the bearer on demand, at the office of the Assistant Treasurer in New York. In December the bearers came to be paid. They were contractors' clerks with satchels and brokers' runners with bundles, and among them were women who had brought a single five-dollar note that a husband in a camp on the Potomac had sent home in his letter, because no shop in Washington would take it at its face. On the sixteenth Britain's answer on the *Trent* reached New York. The next morning the line ran from the Sub-Treasury's steps east along Wall Street, past the doors of the banks that would not send their coin. John Cisco paid in gold all that day and the next, while the porters carried up the boxes and brought them down empty. On the afternoon of the eighteenth he shut the window and had a notice posted on the door. The office would receive the notes in payment of anything owed to the government. It would not redeem them. The banks of New York were still paying in gold, and would for twelve days more. The United States had stopped paying its own money first.

### S3. The run on Montgomery Street: San Francisco, January 1862

> The news that New York had stopped paying came up from Panama in the mail at the end of January, in the month the river had put Sacramento under water, and the next morning there was a crowd on Montgomery Street before the banks opened. It stood thickest at the doors of the houses that had partners in New York, and Donohoe, Ralston & Co., whose partner Kelly was in New York, had the longest line on the street. San Francisco remembered 1855, when Page, Bacon's house had closed its doors and two hundred firms had gone down with it. William Ralston opened an hour early. He had the coin carried up from the vault and stacked on the counters in columns, where it could be seen from the street through the open doors, and he told the tellers to pay every depositor in full and to pay fast. When the house across the street sent to say it was running short, he sent it coin in a handcart, and the other houses did the same for one another as the day went on. He kept his doors open past the hour, until the street was empty. By evening the men who had drawn their money in the morning were bringing it back, because it was heavy, and because it had been there. In 1855 the Republic's mint had carried the solvent houses through. In 1862 they carried themselves. The coin that had not gone to New York since July was still in their vaults. In February Ralston put it to the other houses that what the coin had done for them that week it could do every week, if they kept it at home, and he took their word to the Protector so that the Protector would not have to give an order; and on the first of March they signed.

**Why the scenes follow from the counterfactuals.** Each step is forced by the one before it:
1. No steamers since July means thinner reserves.
2. Thinner reserves and the memory of the Hawaiian packet mean the banks' terms in November.
3. The terms, which Chase refuses, mean an army paid late and in notes.
4. An unpaid army means a Treasury with three historical doors and one new one.
5. Lincoln walks through the paper door.

The two runs are forced the same way. The Treasury pays in notes redeemable in coin it no longer receives, so the first fright brings the notes back, and it has to shut its window (S2). The news of New York's suspension brings San Francisco's depositors to the counters of the houses with New York partners first. Their banks pay, because the coast's gold stayed in the city (S3).

Every historical date stays where it was:
- the third loan, 16 November;
- the commissioners given up, 26 December;
- Britain's answer on the *Trent* reaching New York, 16 December;
- the suspension, 30 December;
- Meigs, 10 January;
- Gallatin's deputation at the Treasury, 11 January;
- the first gold quotation, 13 January;
- the Legal Tender Act, 25 February.

What changes is why they happen and what they mean. The reader who knows the history sees the same days and the same words, and finds that they are about something else.

**What it does to the rest of the book:**
- It turns the Gold Room into the scoreboard of a choice Lincoln made, and CR-065 F1 then reads as the verdict on it.
- It gives Elias a party in New York before he writes the armistice offer of April 1862 (U8).
- It sets the Steamer Day Agreement in San Francisco against the bankers' terms in New York, two sets of bankers at the two ends of the same mail line.
- The two runs make the chapter a diptych. In December a government shuts its window on its own soldiers' wives; in January a banker keeps his doors open past the hour. Ralston earns the college's name in public, on the day the city could see it.
