## E1. Force: the Militia Act (1882) — E-1 APPROVED

**The problem.** Each member keeps a militia. Deseret's Nauvoo Legion already serves within the Defense Law (canon §10); the others are under their own governors' commissions. Kagoshima's lesson (Part D, D6) is that a unit left with its own armed men becomes a second government.

**The act (1882).**
- Every member militia becomes a **reserve of the common army**. Its officers hold confederal commissions; its arms are kept in confederal arsenals; it is called out by the Providentor, not the governor.
- **One examination for every commission (K-2, APPROVED).** The act puts every officer's commission before a board chaired by Sherman (E9.1): the militias' officers, and with them the officers of the regulars and of the Atlantic Auxiliary. This is canon's point that "the examinations reach the officer corps in mid-career" (CR-022, §19), and 1882 is its date. Many of the old fighting officers fail. Silas, an officer of the Auxiliary since 1872 and thirty years old, fails the mathematics and is capped below general rank, and Elias refuses to intervene (canon).
- **Deseret** loses nothing it had not already given: the Legion has defended the roads and the valley under the Compact since 1863 and served within the Defense Law since 1867 (§10, §12). The Church accepts the act, which costs it least of anyone.
- **California** resists: the Committee party's papers call it the founder's army. The act passes the confederal chambers on the Party's majorities, and California's legislature can only protest, because defence is a confederal competence (Articles of 1856, canon).

**The consequence no one intends.** The failed officers are not thrown out. At Sherman's request, and with Elias's consent, they are kept together as a command of their own, the inland command (E9.1). From 1882 to 1885 the Auxiliary's veteran battalions pass to the interior garrisons on land grants and come under the same command, and Silas commands on the Sierra line (CR-022 B3, dates amended by K-2). By 1887 they are Silas's veterans (CR-072 C4), and the Truce gives their command to Silas for life. The act removes the members' armed men and gathers them into one body with a grievance. It is the lesson of Kagoshima applied to the states and not to the army, misapplied for the first time in 1882 (Part D, D6, K-14), and the cause is an honourable act, not an oversight.

## E2. Money: the assumption (1875–1879) — E-2, E-3 APPROVED

**The lean years (1875–77).** The depression reaches the coast in 1875 and coin drains east (Hist.; CR-073 B3); the drought of 1876–77 ruins the southern ranges (Hist. for California); the members' railway and road bonds, issued on the grants of the 1860s, fall into arrears in Oregon, Columbia and the interior. The confederal credit is sound and the members' is not.
- **August 1875 (CR-073 E1, R1, R9).** The city's coin runs short. The Reserve Bank discounts the Bank of California's good paper at a penalty rate, the doors stay open and no run happens. The same month the Republic, meaning the Confederation (K-5, Part F), buys the Spring Valley Water Company at a published valuation. San Francisco's private money comes through the lean years intact, and owes it to the Confederation's institutions, not to California's.
- **The members' lesson.** The Confederation's Reserve Bank carried the coast's largest private bank through August 1875; no member could have. Oregon, Columbia and the interior, whose bonds are in arrears, see what the Confederation's credit is worth.

**The bargain (L3).** The Confederation offers to assume the members' debts and to guarantee their paper, as Tokyo did for the domains in 1871. The price is their accounts:
- member treasuries are audited by the Polytechnic's auditors;
- member treasurers and auditors are examined and posted (E3);
- member revenues other than the land tax pass to the Confederation, which pays the members a grant per head; the land tax is already the counties' (CR-021 B2).

**Banks are not in the bargain.** Banking has followed the currency to the centre since 1857 (CR-057 G13). The Bank of California, a California bank, has kept its reserves at the Reserve Bank and discounted there through the 1870s, and Sharon was broken by its collateral rule in January 1875 (CR-073 A1, B1, E1, G4). California's refusal in 1879 leaves its banks where they already are.

**The Assumption Act (1879)** goes through the confederal chambers on the Party's majorities and is offered to each member legislature together with the Commission Act (E3). A member takes both or neither (E-3).

**Who takes it.** Oregon, Columbia, Idaho, western Montana, Washoe and New Mexico take it in 1879–80; their legislatures are the Party's (CR-072 C2; Washoe's since November 1874, CR-073 G4). **California refuses**: its debt is small, its bankers came through 1875 on the Reserve Bank's discount, and it does not need rescuing. **Deseret refuses**: the Church holds no public debt worth the price, and Taylor will not open the Church's books to Carquinez's auditors.

**Model.** The member debts assumed and the grant per head are not in the workbook. They are small against the confederal debt (MODEL: a few million dollars), and a row in the next rebuild is enough. The Spring Valley purchase (about $9m, MODEL) is CR-073's row.

## E3. Men and law: the Commission Act and the codes (1879–1886)

### E3.1 The Commission Act (step one, 1879) — E-4 APPROVED

This is Part D's L1, the return of the registers. The office changes its nature and keeps its holder.
- Each member's chief executive accepts a **confederal commission** as **Commissioner of the Confederation** for his member. He is still elected by his people; from his confirmation by the Providentor he also executes confederal law in his member and answers to the Providentor for it.
- **The title travels down.** In 1887 the same title, Commissioner, passes to the officer of each of the 124 counties (CR-021 B3). Men who held it as governors in 1879 hear it given to county officers in 1887 and understand what has been done to them. The Chronicler need not say so.
- **It is presented as a rank (L2):** a confederal commission, a seat at the Providentor's council, a salary from the confederal treasury, precedence at Monterey and, from 1885, at Thalassia (L5).
- **The member treasurer and auditor** are thereafter examined and posted from outside the member, as county treasurers and auditors will be after 1887 (CR-037 E10). This is where the examination first reaches state offices.
- **Hawaii** is outside the act: its executive is the Queen, and from 1876 its business is in the Territorial Council's hands (E6). **California and Deseret** refuse it with the assumption (E2).

**The name (E-17, APPROVED 16 November).**
- **Formal title:** *An Act for the Common Execution of the Laws* (1879). It says what the act does, and nothing about what it will lead to. The Assumption Act of the same session is its schedule.
- **Common name:** **the Commission Act**, after the commissions it confers; in Spanish *la Ley de Comisión*.
- **What its opponents call it:** **the Collar.** The Committee party's papers coin it in San Francisco, and in Salt Lake the *Deseret News* takes it up. The governors who accept it wear the collar; the two members that refuse keep their necks free until 1887.
- **The ceremony (E-17, APPROVED).** Each Commissioner receives his commission from Elias in person at Monterey and swears the oath of the Confederation, not of his member. It is the 1879 echo of the lords of 1869 returning their registers to the throne (Part D, D1), and Itō, who was with Kido at Nagasaki in 1867 when the lesson began (D1), is told of it by letter.
- **Alternatives considered:** "the Act of Concurrence" (closer to the Japanese "return", but vaguer about what is given up); "the Governors' Act" (plain, but it names the men it diminishes).

### E3.2 Soberanes's memorandum (Part D, decision 9, APPROVED; winter 1878–79)

Back from Vladivostok, Soberanes reads the reports from Japan (Part D, D3) and writes the memorandum that turns them into the sequence of this part: money first, then the men, then the armed men, then the map, then the vote. It also proposes the Party's use (CR-072 C3). It is addressed to Elias alone and read by Victoria. Soberanes is 31. His motive is CR-072 C3's (the states' patronage posts go to the estates and their boards to the Party; as a historic land California keeps Spanish and the Court at Monterey) and Part G's (G6.2: inside the state the Californios could lose any session, inside the Realm they are guaranteed).

### E3.3 The codes (1872–1886) — E-6 APPROVED

- **Hist.** California adopted four codes in 1872 (civil, penal, civil procedure and political), drawn from David Dudley Field's New York drafts. Montana, Idaho and Dakota later adopted Field-based codes as well.
- **In this timeline.** The members of the interior and the Northwest adopt California's codes one after another in the 1870s and 1880s, through Party majorities in their legislatures. By 1887 the Realm's "single code" (CR-021 B2) is a consolidation of laws that most members already share, not a new law imposed.
- **What stays apart:** Deseret's marriage and church-property law (protected at accession; carried over as a personal guarantee in 1887, CR-072 C7), Spanish community property in California, Hawaiian land tenure (CR-021 B2).
- **The judges.** Member supreme courts sit alongside the Confederal Court from 1865 (CR-021 A). From 1880 their judges are offered places on the Confederal Court's circuits and, in 1887, seats in the Court of the Realm at Monterey or the county courts of first instance, with pensions for those who decline. The bar is offered admission to the Realm's courts without examination for those already practising.

## E4. The map: Judah's survey (1880–1886) — E-7, E-8, E-16 APPROVED

**The commission (P12).** In 1880 the Patrimony and its Development Fund are chartered (canon CR-057), with Ralston as first chairman (CR-073 R6). The Fund lends beside private capital against water districts, railway stations and land values, and needs a map of all three. Its survey is headed by **Theodore Judah**, aged 54. The cover is honest: the Fund does need the map. Its second use is known to Elias, Soberanes and Judah. Ralston, who as chairman signs Judah's appointment, works out the second use for himself and says nothing (E-16; E9.2).

**The rules (to be fixed in Part I):**
- **Watershed first.** Powell's report of 1878 on the arid lands argued that the West's political units should follow its drainage (Hist.; Powell was American; the idea is in the air of the West).
- **One railway station or port as seat**, within a day's travel of every part of the county.
- **Population** within bands; **language** respected where a county can be drawn round a Spanish- or Hawaiian-speaking district (CR-021 C2).
- **The historic lands keep their outline.** California's guarantees (Spanish, community property, the Mexican grants) are territorial, so its outer boundary survives as county lines; Hawaii's is the sea. Elsewhere **no county reproduces a member**, and **no county is named for one** (L8). Old member lines may be followed where watershed or railway put them, but no county's lines enclose a member whole.
- **Native homelands** are drawn round, not through (Part I, I4).

**The census of 1880, re-tabulated (K-3, APPROVED).** The confederal census of 1880 is taken by member and by the old counties, as before; the survey has only just begun. In 1884 Judah's office re-tabulates it by the districts of the first map and prints it with the map. Every confederal table from then on, of births, deaths, schools, revenue and freight, is printed by district. By 1887 the districts have three years of tables and a census behind them. The counties are tables before they are governments, and nobody can say the new lines were drawn in a night.

**Two maps (L9).**
- **The first map (1884):** about 130 districts, printed with the re-tabulated census.
- **The second map (1886):** the 124, after the Party's county committees have argued the seats and the lines inside the Party (E5). This is the map of the Constitution.

## E5. The old counties (1886–1887) — E-9 APPROVED

**The problem.** The roughly 200 boards of the old member counties are the Party's own base (CR-072 F). The 124 are fewer and larger. Some boards disappear into larger counties; some towns lose their courthouse.

**What the Party offers:**
- **Seats on the new board.** For the first two terms after 1887, each new county's board is elected by wards that follow the old county lines, so every old board's men have seats to stand for.
- **Debts.** The old counties' debts are assumed with the members' (E2).
- **The seat.** Where two towns want the county seat, the survey's rule (the railway station) decides, and the loser receives the county's court of first instance, its school of the examination or its hall (CR-038, the county halls of the 1890s). The fights are settled inside the Party's county committee, not in the street.
- **Discipline.** A board that fights the map loses its place in the queue for grants (CR-072 D).

**The western county-seat wars** (Hist.: armed fights over courthouses in Kansas, Colorado and elsewhere in the 1880s) are the warning. Pacifica has a few quarrels and no wars, because the quarrel has a Party to go to.

## E6. Hawaii (1874–1887): canon tension CT1 resolved — E-10 APPROVED

The outline's CT1 is settled by CR-027 (approved, 21 October 2026), which superseded CR-021's rows for 1874–1876:
- **1874:** the Puʻuloa Convention, a lawful fifty-year lease and defence treaty, signed by Victoria as Queen.
- **1874–76:** the confederal defence ordinances, illegal; **1876:** the appointed Territorial Council takes over the legislature's business, illegal; Hawaii's Supreme Court declares the ordinances void, and the judgment is ignored. Ruth resigns as Kuhina Nui.
- **The throne is never in abeyance.** Victoria reigns throughout.

**What follows for 1887:**
- Hawaii cannot refuse anything in this part: its business has been in the Territorial Council's hands since 1876. That is why E1–E3 do not mention it.
- **The delegation (K-10, APPROVED).** From 1876 the Territorial Council also chooses Hawaii's delegation to the Confederal Council, as it has the legislature's other business. Hawaii's votes in the Council are the Crown's from then on.
- In 1887 Hawaii's ratification comes from the Territorial Council, as CR-021 has it, **with the Queen's assent**. Victoria assents to the Constitution that makes her Regent. Hawaiian loyalists reject both the Council's vote and her assent (CR-072 P23).
- The cure is the Act of Settlement of 1907 (CR-040), which dissolves the Council and restores the legislature (canon, CR-027 B5).
- **The Queen's Guard** (canon CR-027 B4, kept in 1873) is the one force in the Realm that answers to a member's head of state. It is not caught by the Militia Act, because it is the Queen's household and not a member militia. In 1887 it becomes the guard of Thalassia, which stands on the Hawaiian crown's land. Why this exception is safe is Part D, D6 (K-4).

## E7. The prototype: Alaska — E-11 APPROVED

The Northern Commission (CR-070) governs Alaska from 1867 by compact and commission, with no legislature. It is the one part of the Confederation that already looks like a county of 1887. It stays under the Commission and outside the 124 (Part I, I-7, APPROVED; CR-070 open decision 9). Baja's commission (1867–87) and the Mountain Districts' (outline A-1) are the other cases.

The outline's broader prototype (C3: lands taken in 1862–65 governed as counties from the start) is dropped: canon makes New Mexico and the interior members ("the new state", §10; "the interior states", CR-021).

## E9. Two men of honour

Two historical men give the preparation its honourable faces, each in his own character: Sherman, the professional soldier who despises politics and will not take power; Ralston, the builder who loves his city more than its sovereignty.

### E9.1 Sherman — E-12, E-13, E-14 APPROVED

**Canon already has him** in California from the 1850s, siding with constitutional government in 1856, a Pacifican citizen before the war, in office after his refusal of 1862 (CR-068 G4), backing constitutional military allegiance at the Truce, refusing political power in June 1884 and dying in February 1891 (§6, §22).

**1882: the examining board.**
- *Hist.* In 1881 Sherman, as commanding general, founded the army's School of Application for Infantry and Cavalry at Fort Leavenworth, and he pressed professional education throughout his career.
- *Here.* As the army's senior officer he chairs the board that examines every commission under the Militia Act (E1): the militias', the regulars' and the Auxiliary's. He makes no exceptions, the founder's son included; Silas fails the mathematics, and Elias refuses to intervene (canon CR-022).
- He also keeps the commissions out of the Party's hands. The Party may have the boards; it may not have the army (CR-072 B). Commissions are examined, not given on a ticket.

**His mercy, and its cost.**
- True to the general who cared for his soldiers, he asks that the failed officers not be discharged but kept together under a command of their own, the inland command (not to be confused with the county reserve, which the Commissioner commands, CR-021 B3). Elias agrees. It is the first time he misapplies the lesson of Kagoshima (Part D, D6, K-14).
- That command is the inland command at Sacramento. In 1887 the Truce writes it into organic law as Silas's for life (canon). The body Kagoshima warned against (Part D, D6) is gathered by an honourable act.
- It also answers a point in canon: Silas is capped below general rank (CR-022), yet holds the inland command. A command of reserves can be held below general rank.

**June 1884: the refusal of the succession.**
- *Hist.* At the Republican convention in June 1884 Sherman refused the nomination: if nominated he would not run, and if elected he would not serve.
- *Here.* Elias is failing and Victoria is increasingly governing (canon §21). Men of the old army, and some Prosperity men who do not trust the Queen's influence or the Japanese naval party, want a soldier to hold the state after the founder. They offer it to Sherman. He gives the same answer.
- With the one respected soldier refusing, the old army is left with Silas, and the succession goes to a negotiated truce rather than to a general. At the Truce Sherman backs constitutional allegiance over personal rule (canon). His refusal is the patriotic act of this sequence.

### E9.2 Ralston — CR-073; E-15, E-16 APPROVED

CR-073 (approved decisions R1–R25) gives Ralston his arc: no failure in 1875; the Reserve Bank's discount in August 1875; Spring Valley bought by the Republic; the San Francisco houses brought to Broderick from that winter; the first chair of the Patrimony from 1880, with the Bank of California's direction passed to Alvord; retirement at 68 in 1894. His honour lies in accepting the state's terms when he could have organised private money against it (CR-073 D), and in printing every loss.

**What Part E takes from CR-073:**
- **E2:** August 1875 is the members' lesson in what the Confederation's credit is worth.
- **E4 (E-16):** Judah's survey works under the Patrimony's charter of 1880, so Ralston signs the appointment. He sees the map's second use and keeps the secret.
- **1886–87 (E-15):** as chairman of the Patrimony, Ralston brings the San Francisco houses to accept the Constitution. His argument is canon's rule that "the city that holds the money never holds the government" (CR-021 B6): San Francisco becomes a Crown city and keeps the mint, the Reserve Bank, the syndicates and the champions' headquarters, and gives up governing. A builder who wants to build, not to rule (CR-073 D2), can sell that to the houses. This is the act that empties the Committee party's last ground (CR-072 C9), and it is used in Part H.
- **Sharon** is broken in January 1875 (CR-073 G4) and is not a figure in this part.

**What the houses' money bought in 1875, and what it did not (K-8, APPROVED).** From the winter of 1875 the large houses subscribe to the Party (CR-073 E2; CR-072 C9). Their money buys the Party's tickets outside San Francisco and its majorities in the confederal chambers, which is where the houses' charters, concessions and syndicates are decided. It does not buy California. Their clerks, the smaller merchants and shopkeepers, the *Bulletin* and the *Call* and the riparian landowners of the valley stay with the Committee party, which holds California's legislature on the city's and the riparian counties' votes until November 1886. The partners pay the Party and keep quiet at home. What E-15 adds in 1886–87 is their public word: the houses break with the Committee party inside California and back the Constitution.

**Ralston's guards in 1886–87 (K-8, APPROVED).** CR-073 E3 gives the chairman published accounts, recusal from his own bank's syndicates and a Fund separate from the Reserve Bank and the Treasury. E-15 keeps them:
- the Patrimony spends nothing on the campaign of 1886 or on the ratification; its accounts show no such outlay, and the houses pay for their own (Part F, F3);
- Ralston argues; he does not stand, speak on the hustings or sit on a Party committee;
- the Committee party's last editors say the Fund's chairman is the Party's banker (CR-073 G2), and the printed accounts are his answer, as in 1880.

CR-073 goes into canon before this CR (K-1), so this CR carries E-15 and the guards into CR-073's registry entry and E3 (Part F–G, section S).
