# Part F — The triggers (APPROVED)

The preparation of Part E makes abolition possible. The triggers make it urgent, and make it 1887.

## F1. The main trigger: the founder's life — APPROVED

Elias is dying, and there is no legitimate heir (§21–§22). The states are the arena in which a successor could be beaten: the eight member legislatures (outline A1), any of which can be bought, courted or raised against a regent. Part D's lesson L12: a reform finished before its makers die outlives them. Kido, Ōkubo and Saigō were all dead by May 1878, and the prefectures stood.

## F3. The precipitating trigger: water — APPROVED

**Hist.** *Lux v. Haggin*, 69 Cal. 255 (April 1886): California's Supreme Court upheld riparian rights against upstream diversion for irrigation (Miller and Lux against Haggin and Tevis); the legislature answered with the Wright Act of 1887. In this timeline the decision of the Confederal Court on appeal is dated October 1886 (F-3).

**Who is still there to sue (K-6, APPROVED).** CR-073 holds back the two historical land-and-water empires, and the case needs a plaintiff large enough to fight it. Miller & Lux's lands along the San Joaquin are riparian and private, bought in the 1850s and 1860s from rancho grantees and private holders. None of CR-073's levers reaches them: the concessions, the Land Fund and the register govern the public domain and Native land, and the Republic's water law governs its own grants and works (K-5). What is held back is their spread onto the public domain and their purchase of the canal, so they remain the largest riparian owner in the valley and can still go to law. Haggin and Tevis, who historically built on public land, never get the Kern: its water goes to a district chartered on a confederal grant. The case of this timeline is therefore **_Lux v. Kern River District_** (working name), a riparian owner against a confederal district.

**Here (October 1886).**
- Under the Articles water law is a residual power of the members: California's courts apply California's doctrine, riparian rights first, to every private claim on its rivers. The Republic's own law governs its grants and the works and districts it charters under the Enabling Law (K-5). A grant pays for works; it does not change whose law governs the private rights on the river.
- California's court holds that a district built on a **confederal water grant** cannot override a riparian right in California.
- **The appeal.** The Confederal Court, limited to the common competences, holds that **a confederal grant does not extinguish a private riparian right held under a member's law**. The Enabling Law and the grants made under it stand; the rivers' private law stays California's. Every district in the state now holds its water only until the next riparian suit, and the founder's own court has told him the grants cannot take the rivers from those who hold them.
- **The campaign.** In November 1886 the Party runs in every irrigation county on the decision. With the San Francisco houses that Ralston has brought over (E-15), spending their own money and not the Patrimony's (K-8), and with the Central Valley farmers, it wins California's legislature, narrowly. The Committee party keeps San Francisco and the riparian counties.
- **The answer.** In 1887 the residual powers pass to the Realm, and public land, water and minerals to the Crown (CR-021 B2). The districts' water stands under the Realm's law (G2).
- **CT1** with CR-073 ("state water law"): see section S.

# Part G — The act

## G1. The instrument and its sequence — APPROVED (G-1, G-2, G-11)

### G1.1 The problem with drafts 1 and 2

- **Draft 1** (in force at Elias's death): the states stay sovereign until a deathbed, and the Realm's birth waits on it.
- **Draft 2** (in force on 1 August while Elias lives, with a transitional article giving the Providentor the Crown's powers): the chambers, the members and the Truce's parties would be handing a dying man in decline **larger powers than he has ever held**, and every one of them would know that they did not know what he might do with them. No faction would vote for that, and there is no precedent for a founder taking new powers in his last months.

### G1.2 What the precedents show

Founders who wanted to see their succession work handed it over while they lived:
- **Charles V** abdicated at Brussels in October 1555, ill and leaning on William of Orange, and handed the Netherlands to Philip in person before the estates (Hist.).
- **Japan's custom of retirement** (*inkyo* in the household; the retired emperor in the state) put the heir in office while the elder still lived. Kiyoshi's Satsuma kin know it: Shimazu Hisamitsu governed Satsuma as the daimyo's father (Hist.). A Meiji parallel for Part D.
- **The counter-precedent** is Franco's Law of Succession (1947): a kingdom with the founder as head of state for life, the successor left open, the state waiting on one man for twenty-eight years. That is what the Truce exists to avoid.

### G1.3 The sequence: the founder hands over in person

**The core.** The Constitution enters into force on a fixed day, **1 August 1887**, and on that day the **regency begins**. The Providentor's office is not carried into the Realm: it ends on the day of entry into force, with no successor to it ("Elias's life office ends with him"). Victoria, named in the regency article, takes the oath as Regent of the Realm at Thalassia, in Elias's presence.

**Why it removes the uncertainty.**
1. **Everyone knows who will hold the powers and how.** She is named in the text (canon), she has governed in practice since 1883 (§21), and the factions negotiated the Truce with her.
2. **No new powers go to a dying man.** The Providentor lays powers down; he takes none up.
3. **The Truce is sworn before the founder.** Silas, Kiyoshi and Soberanes take their offices from the Regent's hand, in Elias's sight, and sit for the first time in the **Regency Council** (canon §22; CR-021 A). The first weeks of the succession are lived through while the founder can still be seen to want it: L12 carried to its end.
4. **The Enabling Law's delegation lapses that day** ("when the regency begins in 1887", §12).
5. **The members' window closes on a known date.** Ratifications cannot be withdrawn once given (stated in the act).

**What Elias keeps (G-11, APPROVED).** No office, no powers and **no seat**, so that no one can appeal from the Regent to him. The preamble names him founder; he keeps the style *Providentor* for life as a title, as the retired emperor kept his. What he says on his deathbed binds no one in law (R, item 13).

**Why he agrees.** He is declining and knows it; Victoria already does the work; and he has seen Meiji's founders die before their settlement was tested (Part D). He wants to watch his successor govern.

**What it gives the story.** The moment on 1 August is Victoria's: the Realm begins with her oath, not with his death. His death in August becomes the test of what he made: the news passes through the telegraph he built, and nothing happens. In October, with the founder dead, Silas's veterans hold the summit at Donner. Silas did not move while his father lived.

**The scene (for the Chronicler).** The handover on 1 August at Thalassia: the founder, too weak to stand long; the Regent's oath; the Truce's three men taking their commissions. The Chronicler may give the day and the room; he need not give Elias's words.

**Whose consent the law needs.** Two-thirds of both chambers and **both founding states** (canon). The charters of 1866 are entrenched and amendable only with each member's consent (CR-059 V10, CT8; CR-060 A5), but each carries an article of supersession: it yields to a constitution adopted by this same procedure, which must carry its protected provisions as guarantees or end them with the power they rest on (K-15, APPROVED; section T). No member's own ratification is required. California's ratification is legally required, which is why California's President has something to bargain with (G6), and why the act comes closest to failing in California's senate in May 1887 (H1, K-9). The interior legislatures are bought because the Party wants every member's name on the instrument. **Deseret's consent is not legally needed** (CR-021 B4); its acceptance on the eve of 1 August is the Church's choice and turns a conquest into an Aufhebung (CR-072 C7). Taylor's resistance is where the Aufhebung nearly fails, not the act. **The act does not wait on Salt Lake (K-15).** The Constitution enters into force on 1 August whatever Deseret's legislature does, and the personal guarantee of religious marriage is in its text either way (CR-072 P16). What the Church decides is only whether it comes in as the Party's Deseret caucus or stays outside as a resentful bloc. Taylor's death on 25 July and Cannon's telegram (G-4) stand as approved: luck the Realm enjoys, not luck it needs.

## G5. How it fits the Truce of Thalassia — APPROVED

- The Truce (spring 1887) comes before the votes and is their price: each faction gets a domain in organic law, and none opposes the Constitution.
- *Silas:* the inland command for life, headquartered in California's old capitol. *Kiyoshi:* the naval sphere at Pearl Harbor. *Soberanes:* the Polytechnic's charter, and with it the examinations that staff the 124 counties (E3.2). *Victoria:* the regency article and Thalassia.

## G6. California's last President — APPROVED (G-10, G-10a)

### G6.1 Romualdo Pacheco (Hist., verified)

Born in Santa Barbara in 1831; schooled in Honolulu at the Oahu Charity School, 1838–43; seaman, judge, state treasurer, brigadier general of the California militia (1863; the Native Cavalry was a separate battalion of federal volunteers); Governor of California, February–December 1875; later a congressman, chairing the committee on private land claims; he spoke for Chinese exclusion in 1882. In canon (CR-029 A12, APPROVED) he goes to Manila in 1876 as Governor-General for a long term.

**In this timeline.** He comes home from Manila about **1885**, at fifty-four, his term done, and is chosen **President of California in November 1886** on the Party's ticket. Canon names no President of California after Vallejo and does not say how the office is filled (R, item 6), so the method is left to integration.

### G6.2 How Pacheco is brought in

He is not bought, and he does not simply obey. He bargains, because California's ratification is legally required (canon) and he holds the founding state's signature. Four things move him, in rising order of weight.

1. **Manila.** For nine years he has governed a dependency by commission under the Providentor's office (CR-029 A10): a Governor-General, a council, examined officers, towns with their own funds and no priest over their budgets (CR-029 A15). He has seen the county model work. He owes his career to the office, and he does not believe the Commissioners are tyrants, because he was one.
2. **The Californios' arithmetic, which is Soberanes's argument (E3.2).** Since the gold rush the Californios have been a minority in their own state's legislature. Sessions bring bills to narrow Spanish in the records, to question a grant, to touch community property. Under the Articles California keeps publishing its laws in both languages (CR-021 C2), but only because the Californios win that fight each time; in 1880–81 a bill for English-only records is defeated, with del Valle leading the fight (G6.4). Inside the state they win slowly and could lose any session. Inside the Realm they are a **historic land whose guarantees no majority can touch**. Soberanes, a Californio of Monterey, makes the case to Pacheco as one Californio to another: the state is where the Californios could lose, and the Crown is where they are safe.
3. **Victoria.** The Regent-designate receives him at Thalassia in the winter of 1886–87, on the island where he went to school as a boy. It is her negotiation, not Elias's, and one of the moments of 1883–87 in which she governs before she is Regent (§21).
4. **His price, which becomes the Constitution's text.** What Pacheco takes home from Thalassia is the **historic-land clause for California**: Spanish entrenched, community property kept, the Mexican grants confirmed as organic law beyond the reach of the Chamber (CR-021 B2, C4; CR-033 A21a). Canon has the clause; G6 gives it an author. For himself he asks an appointed seat in the Senate and precedence at Monterey (G4).

**The friction (G-10a, APPROVED: eyes open).** His anti-Chinese politics are the Sacramento faction's (CR-029). The Realm takes immigration to the centre (CR-021 B2), so the last President of California signs away the one power through which California could have passed exclusion. He trades exclusion for the Californios' guarantees and knows it. He remains a man who chose.

### G6.3 Reginaldo del Valle (Hist.; corrected after the review of 17 November)

- Born in Los Angeles on 15 December 1854; son of Ygnacio del Valle, who came from Jalisco in 1825 and was alcalde of Los Angeles in 1850; the family at Rancho Camulos from 1861.
- Fluent in English, French and Spanish. Santa Clara (1873); the bar in 1877.
- **Assembly, 1880–83.** A Democrat. He fought, and lost, a bill requiring state offices to keep their records in English only instead of in Spanish and English. He carried the bill for the State Normal School at Los Angeles (later UCLA). He lost the speakership by three votes in 1881.
- **Senate, 1883–87.** President pro tempore at twenty-eight, the youngest ever. The *Los Angeles Times* (1885) called him the upper house's "parliamentarian par excellence".
- **Later.** He chaired the Democratic state conventions (1888, 1894) and lost races for Congress (1884) and lieutenant governor (1890). Later he spent many years on Los Angeles's Public Service Commission (dates to verify) and helped end the Owens Valley water wars after the aqueduct was dynamited. A supporter of mission restoration (the Landmarks Club itself was founded by Charles Lummis in 1895). Died in 1938.

### G6.4 Del Valle's part

1. **He is the Republic's own son.** Born in 1854, eight years after the *República de California* (canon §3), he was never a Mexican or an American citizen. His vote ends the state he was born in.
2. **Water.** He is historically the Californio who made peace in the water wars. With the water decision of 1886 as the trigger (F3), he is the natural voice of the irrigation districts in the south.
3. **Spanish.** Historically he fought an English-only bill and lost. In Pacifica he fights it and wins (CR-021 C2 keeps bilingual publication), which is why the Californios trust him with the vote that ends their state.
4. **The chamber.** A ratification carried "narrowly" needs a parliamentarian, and he was the best in California's upper house in exactly these years.
5. **Schools.** The Normal School ties him to the examinations, and so to Soberanes's estate.
6. **A convert.** Historically a Democrat, he comes to the Party from the opposition, as CR-072 P20 has it absorb its rivals.
7. **He lives to 1938.** Pacheco dies in 1899. Del Valle can carry the Californio thread into Julian's reign and sit in the Realm's Senate in the fight of 1910–1911 (CR-021 B5); he reaches sixty-eight in December 1922 and retires under CR-038, so in 1927 he watches from the Court's bar or the board of a Los Angeles county, not the Senate.

### G6.5 Both

- **Pacheco is President** and makes the bargain at Thalassia (G6.2).
- **Del Valle carries the ratification** through California's senate in May 1887, narrowly, as its president pro tempore or floor leader (Hist.: he sat in the state senate until 1887).
- In the Realm, del Valle sits for a Los Angeles county from 1888, or among the appointed quarter.

California's exit has two faces: the old Californio who bargains for guarantees, and the young one, born in the Republic, who votes the state away.

## T. Amendment carried to CR-059, CR-060 and CR-061: the article of supersession (K-15, APPROVED)

The charters of 1866 are integrated canon (`main`, commit 28b7263). > **The article of supersession.** This charter may be amended only with the consent of the member's legislature. It yields to a constitution of the Confederation adopted by the procedure that entrenched it, two-thirds of both confederal chambers and ratification by both founding states, provided that constitution either carries the provisions this charter protects as guarantees of the whole, or ends them because the power on which they rest passes from the members to the centre.

**T2. CR-059 S8 already foresees the first case: the exclusion article ends "with the county policy or at the latest the Constitution of the Realm in 1887".
