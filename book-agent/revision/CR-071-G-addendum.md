## K-19. Why Regent and not Queen: the crown offered and declined — APPROVED

### G1.3a The offer (new subsection, after G1.3's "What it gives the story")

**The answer: she is offered the crown and declines it.** The regency is her choice, not something done to her.

- **When (PROPOSED).** December 1886, before the bill is laid before the chambers in January 1887. Soberanes brings her the draft, and its first article on the Crown makes her **Queen of the Realm**.
- **Who puts it there (APPROVED, 18:11).** Elias. The founder wants to crown his wife before he dies. A crown for Victoria passes, through her, to Julian, so the founder would be choosing between his sons without saying so.
- **What she does.** She strikes the article and has Soberanes draft the regency article in its place, with the clause "until an Act of Settlement establishes the Crown". It is the text canon has, and now it has an author, as G6 gives the historic-land clause one.

**Her reasons, in the order they weigh with her.**
1. **The dynasty is not agreed.** A queen regnant's heir is her heir: Julian, a hānai son whose claim the loyalists dispute (CR-027). A crown in 1887 would shut out Kiyoshi, Elias's second son, without a hearing, and force the question of the founder's sons while he is dying. A regency holds the Crown open until the Realm can settle it. That is why Silas and Kiyoshi can swear to the Truce in the spring (G5), and why the question waits for 1907 (CR-040).
2. **The Truce would not carry a crown.** The members' men accepted a life Providentor, not a king. A Hawaiian crown over the Realm, on the day the states end, would read in Sacramento, Salem and Salt Lake as Honolulu absorbing Monterey. A regency is a holding office, and everyone can vote for it.
3. **Her own scruple.** The Realm she would reign over still carries the illegal ordinances of 1874–76 and the ignored judgment of 1876 (CR-027). A second crown taken from the state that stretched her lease would look like the lease's price. She will not join her crown without asking her people (CR-029, redated by CR-040), and in 1887 she has not asked them.
4. **The precedents are on the regency's side.** Spain's Constitution of 1869 declared a monarchy and made Serrano Regent while the Cortes looked for a king (Hist., 1869–70; known in 1887). Kaʻahumanu governed as Kuhina Nui, and Victoria herself held the crown's powers for a day in November 1863 (CR-027). Power held for a crown not yet settled is the more Hawaiian form, not a demotion.

**What it gives the story.** "Regent while Queen" becomes something people say about her, admiring or sneering. She builds the throne and never sits on it: in November 1907 she lays down the regency and her Hawaiian crown, and Julian accedes to both (CR-040). On 1 August the founder who offered his wife a crown watches her swear to a lesser office she wrote herself.

**The scene (for the Chronicler).** The draft on her desk at Thalassia and the struck article. The Chronicler may give the act; he need not give her words, or Elias's.

## R. Reconciliation with Part I (draft 11)

### R1. What Part I already settles

- **The map (I-8, I-26, I-46, I4a).** Twelve large homelands stand outside the 124, about 102,700 square miles in all, on the lines of the agreements that made them: the Diné country (21,280 sq mi), the Utes' Slope and Parks (32,234), the Nez Perce country (10,810), the Apache country of the White Mountains (8,069), the Tohono O'odham's desert (5,149), Colville (4,916), the Chihenne and Bedonkohe country (3,886), the Uinta valley (3,704), the Chiricahua's (3,329), Fort Hall (2,805), Yakama (2,157) and Klamath (1,653), with the Hopi country inside the Diné's (2,742). Everything else is an enclave inside a county, beyond its board's reach (`population/outputs/homelands_1887.md`).
- **The Senate, as draft 11 had it (I-9, I-11, I-32, I-64; superseded by I-70 and I-71, see R3).** **Seven homeland seats, by region**, each chosen by the councils of that region's compact nations in their own way: (1) California and Baja; (2) the Columbia and the Sound with the plateau peoples; (3) the Basin; (4) the Rockies' Utes; (5) the Diné; (6) the Pueblos, the Hopi, the Apache and Arizona's river and desert peoples; (7) Alaska's nations. Enclaves are represented as well as the large homelands.
- **Voting (I-9).** A member of a nation who lives in a county registers and votes there, and only there.
- **Leased ground (I-34, I-47, I-68).** The Utes' mining districts and roads in San Juan and Gunnison are county ground; Lewiston and the Clearwater camps, Globe and Clifton, Bisbee, Santa Rita and Roc Creek are leased ground inside a homeland, in no county.

Revision 2's section C1 (the list of qualifying homelands, drawn by hand from canon and historical acreages) is **withdrawn**: Part I's I4a is the list, measured on Royce's lines.

### R2. Where today's decisions fit without conflict

- **K-22 (leased ground) fits.** It applies to the leaseholds Part I leaves in no county (Lewiston, the Clearwater camps, Globe, Clifton, Bisbee, Santa Rita, Roc Creek): for schools, roads, police and the Chamber's roll their residents are attached to the neighbouring county, which taxes the improvements and not the land. The Utes' districts in San Juan and Gunnison are county ground already (I-68) and are untouched.
- **K-24 (citizenship by registration) fits.** It completes I-9: a member of a nation becomes a citizen by entering himself on the Superintendency's register, with no English test, on the precedent of the citizenship choice east of Glorieta (CR-061 P5). Canon has members of treaty nations free to hold citizenship (CR-059 open decision 27, decided 12 November). Still open: where a citizen who lives inside a large homeland, not in a county, is on the Chamber's roll. Recommendation: the neighbouring county's, as under K-22.
- **The appointed quarter.** Canon's base is the whole Senate (§21: "up to a quarter of the Senate, with twelve Polytechnic seats and eight aliʻi seats"), so the appointees are at most a third of the other seats. The figures under the settled seat rule are in R3.

### R3. How the two conflicts were settled (author, 18:50 and 18:56)

**Seats.** The author kept the size rule against I-64's regions, "especially since tribes that live close to each other may not be friendly": I-64 had to make the Pueblos and the Apache share a senator and pull the Hopi out of the Diné's group. Settled as Part I, I-70 and I-71:
- **One senator for each large homeland**, chosen by its own council in its own way (K-20; I-70).
- **Leagues by choice for the enclaves** (I-71). Enclaves may join partners of their own choosing; when a league's combined land reaches a million acres it chooses a senator. A nation is in one league at a time and may leave at the end of a term; large homelands have their own seats and join none. Every member of a nation who lives in a county also votes there (I-9).
- **Alaska's seat** stays (I-32).
- K-23 (one per compact) is withdrawn.

**Threshold.** One million acres, about 1,563 square miles, for the map and the Senate alike (K-21; I-72). It makes Yakama, Klamath and Fort Hall large by the rule itself, gives the **Hopi** (2,742 sq mi) a senator of their own, and moves three enclaves out of their counties and leaves a fourth inside (Part I, I4b; I-73): **Flathead** (1,813, out of Missoula), **Temecula** (1,930, out of San Bernardino, San Diego and Santa Ana), **Santa Ysabel** (1,620, out of San Diego) and the **Siletz coast** (1,806 before the Yaquina deed, out of Tualatin, Calapooia and Lane). Measured on 17 November, evening: without the Yaquina tract bought by deed (CR-059 S5; about 200,000 acres), the Siletz coast is about 1,490 sq mi and **stays an enclave**; the map is redrawn on that (Part I, I4b), and San Diego's Salton desert goes to San Bernardino. Hualapai (1,504) stays an enclave.

**The Senate of 1888 (PROPOSED counts).** 123 county seats, 12 Polytechnic, 8 aliʻi, 16 homeland seats and Alaska's (the Siletz coast stays an enclave after the Yaquina deed; Part I, I4b); no league assumed. The appointees are at most a third of the rest: **53**, in a Senate of at most **213**.

## D. K-25 — The nations' ladder: buying land back and earning powers — APPROVED (author, 18:50)

**The aim (author, 18:29).** A way for the nations to acquire more land, and an incentive to keep, adapt and develop their powers into modern times in new and surprising ways.

**The design.** The counties already have a ladder: chartered, ordinary and administered, earned by meeting the Office's standard for five years (CR-021 B3; §25). The homelands get one too, with the same logic and different rungs. Autonomy is earned, in land as in powers.

### D1. Land back

1. **First refusal on their own ceded land.** The Land Fund holds the land each nation ceded by deed (CR-033 A5, A22). Whatever is still unsold, the nation may buy back **at the price the Fund paid for it**, the annuity price, which is far below the market. Land the Fund has already sold to settlers the nation may buy at market when it is offered.
2. **Purchases join the homeland by supplementary compact.** I-46 holds: a homeland's line is the line of an agreement, so the addition is a new agreement, signed by the nation and the Realm and entered in the register. The land leaves the public domain and, if it lay in a county, the county's map. The county is paid the lost land tax for ten years out of the Fund's margin, so the board has no grievance to take to its senator.
3. **Growth changes standing.** An enclave whose homeland grows past a million acres leaves its county, becomes a large homeland and chooses its own senator (I-72, I-70). Hualapai (1,504 sq mi) is nearest; the Siletz coast, if the Yaquina deed takes it under, is next.
4. **The leak still runs** (CR-033 A23). Long leases at low rents and assigned annuities keep moving land out; the buy-back moves it in. The Office's tables publish both columns for every nation, and they tell different stories nation by nation.

### D2. Powers earned

A nation whose published accounts and services meet the Office's standard for five years becomes a **chartered nation**. It may then:
1. **Be its own trustee.** It leases its own ground and collects its own rents, and keeps the fee the Realm has kept on every lease since 1851 (CR-033 A22). This is the largest money in the ladder, and it goes to the nations that do the work.
2. **Keep a nation's patrimony.** Royalties go into a fund on the Patrimony's rules (CR-057): the capital is kept, only the yield is spent, and the fund may buy land (D1) and shares. In canon these royalties already exist: Santa Rita's copper (the first Apache royalty, I-66), the Utes' San Juan lease and Roc Creek's uranium (I-68, CR-049), the oil, helium and uranium of the Diné country (I4a), the kilowatt-hour royalties of the Columbia's dams (CR-051, CR-055) and the finder's shares in Alaska (CR-070).
3. **Charter companies and borrow at home** under the Realm's company law and, as a chartered county does, with the Treasury's consent.
4. **Hold its own court of first instance and police**, with appeal to the Court of the Realm at Monterey.
5. **Have its language certified on the Crown's list** when its schools teach it to the Polytechnic's standard. Every citizen of the nation who passes in it then casts a **tongue vote** (CR-021 C3). A nation that keeps its language adds to its weight in the Chamber.

### D3. What it can give the story (PROPOSED, for later CRs)

- **The Diné become landlords of oil, helium and uranium**, and by 1932 their patrimony is one of the Realm's larger endowments, invested in the San Juan basin's own refinery.
- **The Ute council holds the uranium royalty** from the Works' lease at Roc Creek. It is paid for a metal whose use it is not told, which sits close to the atom's secret (CR-050, CR-051).
- **The Columbia's peoples are paid by the kilowatt-hour**, and a plateau nation becomes a shareholder in a power company before most Pacifican towns own their own lights.
- **Language as votes.** The first Native language certified (Diné bizaad or Hawaiian's neighbour on the list) gives a bilingual homeland more votes per head than a monolingual county. County men protest, and they cannot answer the argument that the Realm pays for every language it can examine.
- **Inside the nations.** The ladder divides councils: leasing for royalties against refusing the plough and the mine (Smohalla's teaching, CR-051), buying land back against spending on schools. Not every nation climbs, and some are administered.

### D4. Precedents

- **Swiss corporations** (Hist.): the Oberallmeindkorporation Schwyz and Bern's Burgergemeinde hold land, forests and enterprises as bodies older than the cantons, outside ordinary municipal government. The nearest model for a homeland that owns and invests.
- **New Zealand's Māori land incorporations** (Hist.; from the 1890s, date to verify): owners of a block incorporate to farm and invest it as one.
- **Our timeline's Indian Reorganization Act of 1934** (Hist.): land taken back into trust, tribal constitutions and corporations. Pacifica does in the 1890s what the Union began in 1934.

### D5. As approved

K-25 (D1 and D2) in principle: first refusal at the Fund's price, additions by supplementary compact with ten years' tax paid to the county, and the chartered nation with its five rungs. The figures (the Fund's prices, the tax years, the standard's measures) are MODEL for the CR-071 settlement workbook (I-67). D3's story lines stay PROPOSED for the CRs that reach those years.
