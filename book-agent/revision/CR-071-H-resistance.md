The state comes closest to force at Donner in October 1887, where the reform's own armed losers stand, and is spared at Salt Lake by Taylor's death. Its first use of force under the Realm is in Honolulu in July 1889, by the Queen's Guard (H2, H4).

### H1.1 California's senate, May 1887: where it nearly fails

- **The arithmetic.** The Party carried the legislature narrowly in November 1886 (F3). California's senate has forty members (MODEL; in our timeline the constitution of 1879 fixed forty); the Party's majority there is two or three (MODEL). The riparian counties, the Committee party's San Francisco senators and the exclusionists of the Sacramento valley vote together against.
- **The riparian amendment.** The opposition does not move to reject. It moves to ratify **on condition** that California's water law be written into the historic-land clause beside Spanish and community property. It is the cleverest move of the campaign: it splits the irrigation men, who want ratification, from the Party, and every Californio who fears for the guarantees can vote for more of them.
- **Why it would kill the act.** A conditional ratification is no ratification: the act admits none (G1.3, "ratifications cannot be withdrawn once given"; PROPOSED: nor qualified), so a conditional vote leaves the founding state's consent unrecorded. And in substance it would make California a veto player over every river it shares, which is the Kagoshima the historic lands were designed against (Part D, D6).
- **Del Valle's evening.** He speaks against the amendment as the man who made peace in the water wars (G6.4): a water law the Realm cannot touch is a water law the districts cannot use. The amendment fails by one vote. The ratification then passes by three. Pacheco, as President, signs it that night.
- **The near miss is public.** The *Bulletin* prints the division list. It is the list the riparian counties remember in 1910.

### H1.2 The riparian owners: what happens to the rivers (new decision H-2)

The Realm takes water as a residual power in 1887 (CR-021 B2). What it does with the private rights *Lux v. Kern River District* upheld is not yet settled anywhere.
- **PROPOSED:** an organic law of 1888 confirms every riparian **use** in being, measured and registered by the Polytechnic's surveyors, as a vested right of the Realm with its quantity; riparian **claims** not in use lapse after five years. The districts' confederal grants stand as appropriations. Compensation is paid for any confirmed use the Realm later takes for a district.
- **Hist. support:** California's own path was slower and the same in direction: the Wright Act of 1887, the compromise between Miller & Lux and Haggin of 1888, and the constitutional amendment of 1928 that limited riparian rights to reasonable use.
- **The cost.** Henry Miller, whose partner Charles Lux dies in March 1887 (Hist.), keeps his water and loses his monopoly of the river. Miller & Lux stay the largest riparian owner in the valley and the most persistent litigants before the Court of the Realm. They are among the riparian counties' senators' backers in 1910–1911 (H3).

### H1.3 Oregon and Deady

- **Matthew Deady** (Hist.: 1824–1893; federal district judge in Oregon from 1859, compiler of Oregon's codes of 1862–64) is in canon the compacts' court of 1861, the judge who ordered the relief steamer released in the flood of December 1861 (§11, CR-059).
- **PROPOSED:** in 1887 he is the most respected lawyer in the Northwest, and the exclusionists ask him to say that the charter cannot be superseded. He says the opposite, from the article of 1866 (section T): the charter yields, on the condition that what it protects is carried over or ends with its power. He then holds the Constitution to it. The exclusion article ends because residence passes to the Realm; the Native article and the floor for Chinese residents are carried as guarantees of the whole, and he says in print that they now bind California too.
- **His reward and his cost.** He takes a seat on the Court of the Realm at Monterey in 1887, at sixty-three, and retires under CR-038 in 1892, the year before his death (Hist.). His reading of the article of supersession, that a carried-over guarantee is entrenched in the Realm as it was in the member, is the Court's first constitutional holding, and it binds the regency.
- **The Douglas Democrats** who backed the charter in 1866 are leaderless by 1887: Nesmith died in 1885 and Lane in 1881 (Hist.). There is no Oregon figure of their weight on the other side.

### H1.4 Columbia's name

Columbia's settlers asked for the name at Monticello in 1852 and Congress gave it away (canon §12, CR-059). L8 (carried out by H-5 and Part I, I-6) names no county after a member. **PROPOSED:** the rule holds, and Columbia loses its name as a polity after twenty years. The Olympia men's petition to keep it for a county is refused at Thalassia with the reason L8 gives. The name stays on the river, on the Polytechnic's later Columbia works, in the counties' own speech and, from 1887, on the Court of the Realm's circuit at Olympia, whose capitol is its courthouse (K-16). It is the smallest loss in this table and the one the Northwest's papers mention every August.

### H1.5 Washoe and the Bonanza men

- **Hist.** James Fair, one of the four Bonanza partners, sat for Nevada in the United States Senate from 1881 to 1887. The Comstock's output collapsed after 1880, and Nevada's population fell from about 62,000 in 1880 to about 47,000 in 1890.
- **PROPOSED:** Washoe's legislature, which chooses its Council delegation (CR-073 R24), sends Fair to the Confederal Council in 1881. He was chosen as the Bonanza firm's man when the Party wanted the Comstock's money; in April 1887 he breaks with the Party and takes three of Washoe's nine with him against the act, for the mines' tenure, and loses (Washoe divides five to four for; the Council 66 to 24; outline A3, W-1); his seat ends on 1 August with the Council itself. The legislature, the Party's since 1874 (CR-073 G4), ratifies.
- **The price.** Existing claims keep their titles (K-17) and pay at the old rate of the tax on net proceeds until worked out; only new claims pay the Realm's royalty. Washoe's five counties (G3) are the clearest case of one county, one senator outrunning the people: by 1890 they have fewer residents than one San Francisco ward (MODEL; to check against the population model).

### H1.6 New Mexico: Chaves

- **José Francisco Chaves** (Hist.: 1833–1904; New Mexico's delegate in Congress, 1865–67) is in canon the man who, with Francisco Perea, stayed with the territorial government in 1862 and published the protest at Las Vegas (§10, CR-061). After 1867 he lives in a member he did not choose.
- **PROPOSED:** in 1886 he petitions Thalassia that the 124 not divide the Rio Grande's Spanish-speaking majority between counties drawn from the outside. The survey has already drawn the Rio Grande counties round the Hispano districts (E4), and the petition is answered by the second map, not by a concession. Spanish becomes the county language along the river (CR-021 C2), the grants and their commons are confirmed as organic law (CR-033 A21a), and CR-037 E10 lets those counties be governed mostly by Hispanos. Chaves stands for a Rio Grande board in 1888 and wins.
- **The patrones** lose the legislature that was their instrument. Their peons and captives were freed by the ordinance of 1865, with compensation; they have nothing left to bargain with in 1887.

### H1.7 Jacksonville (the old county seat)

- **Hist.** Jacksonville was the seat of Jackson County, Oregon, from 1853. The railway passed it by in 1883 and Medford grew at the station; Jacksonville lost the seat to Medford by vote in 1927.
- **PROPOSED:** under the survey's rule (one railway station or port as seat, E4) the seat of the new county goes to Medford in 1887, forty years early. Jacksonville keeps the county court of first instance (E-9). Jackson County is also where the returns commission voided a stuffed box on Pacifica's own complaint in 1866 (§12), so the town has a memory of being on the losing side of Monterey's honesty. The quarrel is settled in the Party's county committee; the courthouse stays a courthouse.
- **The railway.** CR-059 W9 builds the Sacramento line north to the Siskiyous by 1867, on a contract that pays Jackson County men in gold; that it continues through the Bear Creek valley past Jacksonville, as the historical line did in 1883, is a story assumption to confirm.

## H4. The Honolulu rising of 1889: the Regent's first test (H-11, APPROVED)

**Hist.** Robert William Wilcox (1855–1903), born on Maui to a Hawaiian mother and an American father, was sent by Kalākaua's government to the military academy at Turin in 1881 and recalled in 1887. On 30 July 1889, with about eighty to a hundred and fifty men, most of them Hawaiian, he occupied the grounds of ʻIolani Palace to force a return to the constitution of 1864, which the Bayonet Constitution of 1887 had replaced. Government rifles, and explosives thrown into the bungalow where the last of them held out, ended it in a day; seven of the rising were killed. A Hawaiian jury acquitted Wilcox of treason in 1890. (Figures to verify.)

**Here (H-11 APPROVED; details inside it PROPOSED).**
- **The grievance.** In May 1887 the appointed Territorial Council ratified, with the Queen's assent, the Constitution that made her Regent (E6). The loyalists reject both. In 1887–88 they courted Noe and looked to Albert Kūnuiākea (§23); Noe dies in the autumn of 1888, and Albert is no leader. The younger men find their own.
- **The man.** Wilcox goes to Turin in 1881 as historically, on Kalākaua's private arrangement, which the Queen permits and Monterey does not pay for (canon: Kalākaua is her envoy, §17). He comes home in 1887 to find the Council ratifying the kingdom into the Realm.
- **The day.** On 30 July 1889, the eve of Lā Hoʻihoʻi Ea, the day of the restoration of 1843 and of the Confederation of 1856 (§6), Wilcox's men occupy the grounds of Aliʻiōlani Hale, where the Territorial Council sits, and demand that the Council be dissolved and the kingdom's legislature restored. They rise in the Queen's name, against her Council, as Lunalilo's followers rose in her name in February 1874 (§17).
- **Her choice.** The Queen is at Thalassia, twelve miles over the Pali. Kiyoshi's naval party at Pearl Harbor offers marines, and the Commissioner for Honolulu county offers the reserve. She refuses both. In 1874 confederal marines landed in Honolulu, and in her view the deaths were Monterey's (§17 leaves each side blaming the other); in 1889 Japanese marines firing on Hawaiians would poison the compact with Tokyo, and the Realm's reserve would make it the Realm's quarrel. She sends the Queen's Guard over the Pali under its own officers, with Kalākaua answerable for its loyalty, as he has been since the mutiny of 1873 (§17). Her terms go ahead of it: lay down arms by morning, and the grievance goes to the kingdom's Supreme Court.
- **The fighting.** Wilcox refuses. The Guard takes the grounds in the afternoon. Seven of the rising are killed, as historically. Hawaiians have fired on Hawaiians in the Queen's name, and the Queen gave the order. In 1874 she kept the Guard in its barracks so that a rising in her name would not become a war; in 1889 she sends it out for the same reason.
- **The aftermath.** She rides into Honolulu that night. Wilcox is tried for treason before the kingdom's Supreme Court, the court whose judgment of 1876 the Council ignored (E6), and a Hawaiian jury acquits him in 1890, as historically. The Regent accepts the verdict. She also says what the Council is for: it carries Hawaii's business until the Crown is established, and the Act that establishes the Crown will end it. The promise is kept in 1907 (CR-040).
- **What it tests.** Whether the Regent is a Hawaiian queen or the Realm's officer: she answers both at once, with her own guard and her own court. The loyalists sing of the seven; the Realm's press notes that no marine landed. **Wilcox's later life (H-14, APPROVED):** he dies in 1903, as historically, four years before the plebiscite of 1907, and the campaign against the union passes to others, possibly his widow Theresa Owana Laʻanui Wilcox, whom he married in 1896 and who was politically active after his death (Hist., from memory, to verify).
- **The Guard's commander (H-13, APPROVED):** Samuel Nowlein, historically captain of the royal Household Guard in the 1890s, commands the Queen's Guard in 1889. Major William Moehonua took the command in 1873 (§17) and historically died in 1877; when Nowlein succeeds him is OPEN.

## H5. Against Meiji: why the friction is lower, and where it went (H-12, APPROVED)

**The abolition itself was bloodless in both.** On 29 August 1871 the guard of eight thousand stood ready and no one fought (Part D, D1). On 1 August 1887 the Constitution entered into force and no one fought. The violence in Japan came three to six years later: Saga in 1874, the Shinpūren, Akizuki and Hagi risings in 1876, Satsuma in 1877 (Hist.). It came from the class the reforms ruined, after the commutation of the stipends into bonds and the ban on swords in 1876, and conscription from 1873. Pacifica's after-years have Donner (one death, Caleb's), the telegraphist burned in his hut at the summit in January 1888 (canon, §22; unnamed) and Honolulu (seven). The comparison is of the aftermaths, and five things explain the difference.

| | Meiji Japan, 1871–77 | Pacifica, 1879–89 |
|---|---|---|
| **What was taken** | A class's income, rank and profession: about two million samurai and their households | Offices and legislatures. No voter loses property, income or rank; the officeholders get seats, pensions or ward seats (G2, G4, E-9) |
| **Money** | A centre in deficit, paying in bonds that lost value | A rich centre paying in grants, assumed debts and pensions (E2; §21's grants) |
| **Attachment** | Domains centuries old | Most members twenty years old; the three with deep identity, California, Hawaii and Deseret, get guarantees (G2; CR-072 C7) |
| **Channels** | No legal arena for the losers until the People's Rights movement, which grew after 1877 as the alternative to the sword | Elections, courts and newspapers from the start. The losers fight with them and lose with them (H1) |
| **Time** | Six years from the guard to Shiroyama | Sixteen years of hollowing before the act (D7) |

**Where the friction went.** Pacifica does not escape Kagoshima; it concentrates it. Every armed resentment the reform creates sits in one command, the inland command, and the Truce writes it into organic law for life (D6). Japan paid in blood once and was done: after 1877 the opposition went into the Diet. Pacifica pays less in 1887 and keeps paying: Donner, then the county oligarchies' bloc in the Senate in 1910–1911 and the refusal of 1927 (H3). The Chronicler does not draw the comparison with Satsuma (Part D, decision 10); this section is the CR's reasoning, not text for the page.

## H6. The settler majority: who speaks for him, and his price (H-15, H-16; directions APPROVED)

**The gap (review of 18 November 2026).** H1 buys every organised interest: Californios, the Church, Hispanos, the nations, judges, the member offices, the San Francisco houses. Nobody pays the Anglo-American farmer, miner and workingman of Oregon, Idaho, Montana, Washoe, Columbia and the Sacramento valley. Yet he loses his state, gets a Hawaiian Regent and a Commissioner he did not elect, and in 1888 the Franchise Law gives his examined or multilingual neighbour more votes than him. The constitution also ends the members' power to exclude. This man is the period's most volatile mass politics: in our timeline, the Workingmen of 1877–79 and the anti-Chinese movement of 1885–86. Two historical parallels:
- the abolition of the Mexican states in 1835 brought the risings of Texas, Zacatecas and Yucatán;
- in July 1887 the Bayonet Constitution showed the reflex of American settlers toward native rule.

### H6.1 Silas as the settler interior's guarantor (H-15, direction APPROVED)

The interior's Anglo-American majority gets an armed guarantee that native rule will not be turned on it: its man holds the inland command for life (G5; §22).   - His price includes something for his constituency beyond his own command.
  - The interior's settlers see the inland command as theirs.
  - Silas is the reason the exclusionists of the Willamette and the Sacramento valley do not become a rising.

### H6.2 A labour article as the settlers' price (H-16, direction APPROVED)

- **The idea.** This is the workingman's counterpart of the historic-land clause (G6.2) and the Church's personal guarantee: a labour article in the Constitution of 1887. Its aim is to meet the grievance that drove the Workingmen in our timeline, which was unfree and undercutting labour, not the presence of the Chinese as such.
- **PROPOSED elements, to choose from:**
  - the same wage for the same work, enforced by the county wage boards (CR-038's benchmarked pay);
  - no indenture, credit-ticket or gang-boss labour, extending Hawaii's abolition of penal contracts of 1866 (CR-038) and the rule of directly paid crews (CR-031-R1) to all the Realm;
  - entry by examination and sponsorship, not by contract;
  - possibly the guest-worker levy brought forward from 1890 to fund the county schools.
- **Canon it builds on.** In §25 (l. 914), "In 1888 selective skilled and sponsored-family immigration replaces the open door … unskilled Union arrivals are refused." Canon already closes the door to unskilled labour a year after the constitution; H-16 can tie that act to the ratification as the price it is.
- **OPEN:**
  - Whether the article is in the constitution or is an organic law promised with it.
  - Its exact terms.
  - Whether the 1888 immigration act is that promise.

- **H-17 (direction APPROVED): the incident.** Canon's Rock Springs (1885) and Hells Canyon (1887) (§15, l. 703) are replaced by a Pacifican incident: an attack on Asian farmers in one of the new irrigation districts in the mid-1880s, which is prosecuted. The reasons:
  - our timeline's drivers of those massacres are mostly absent in Pacifica;
  - Hells Canyon's setting does not exist, because the Wallowa is the Nez Perce's country (I-47);
  - land is where settler and Asian families really compete.

  Its knock-on: l. 931 ("Union critics answer with the Chinese massacres") changes with it. **PROPOSED, to settle:** the district, the year, what is done (sheds and crops burned, families driven off, deaths or none), the trial and the sentence. **This is a canon tension:** l. 703 is amended (CT7 below).
- **H-18 (direction APPROVED): the grievance and the remedy.** Pacifican nativism is a grievance of status and ethos, not of wages. A proud settler people rejects the grind as alien and does not want it overvalued, or too closely coupled with power. The author holds it legitimate: Pacifica must substantially make good on it. Canon's institutionalized agon is the avenue: "Give ability its trial and achievement its honour", and among the qualified, achievement outranks examination rank (CR-049, §5 l. 214, §8 l. 295). The remedy is not a quota (CR-037 forbids one); it widens what counts. **PROPOSED mechanisms, for the author:** H6.4.

### H6.4 Making good through the agon (items 1–4 APPROVED, 18 November 2026, 05:51; item 5 PROPOSED)

1. **The examination as a floor, not a ladder, for power.** The civil examination stays the gate to office (CR-037). Above it, postings to county Commissioner and to the Realm's offices go by the record of works, as fellowships have since 1854, not by marks. In 1887 the rule written for the Polytechnic becomes the rule for the service.
2. **Hours out of the contest.** H-16's labour article adds a maximum working day: eight hours on the Realm's works and in its chartered undertakings, and a weekly day of rest everywhere. No one wins a wage job by working longer. This was the Workingmen's and the Knights of Labor's first demand; California's unenforced eight-hour law of 1868 is the historical seed. Family farms and shops stay outside it, which is why the irrigation districts remain the sharp edge (H-17).
3. **Arenas where the settler's virtues win honour.** These are open to every Pacifican, so the honour is earned, not reserved:
   - the militia's commission, which already carries the Franchise Law's third vote;
   - the counties' contest for the excellence of their works (the league tables);
   - deeds before witnesses: the survey parties, the Northern Commission, the mountain divisions, fire and flood;
   - public festivals of the counties' marksmen and riders on the model of the Swiss federal shooting festival, an agon that bound a confederation of citizen-soldiers.
4. **Silas as the type.** He is the brave soldier capped by an examination (he fails the mathematics in 1882). The inland command is the refuge of the men the grind turned away (E-13, H-15). The agon is the Realm's answer to the next Silas; whether it comes too late for this one is the story's.
5. **The limit, kept honest.** Asian families who out-work their neighbours on their own land cannot be legislated against without breaking CR-037. The agon answers the status grievance; it does not end the competition for land. That residue is the nativists of 1906 and the county oligarchies of 1910–11.

### H6.5 The honest avenue for the unexamined (H-19; direction APPROVED)

> I approve 1-4, but I think an honest avenue should be made for those who can't pass or refuse to be examined for whatever reason to obtain not just status but also property and the means to win over a smart wife. Just as the officers who fail get the inland command to honor their achievements on the battlefield, so the state should not obstruct real rewards commensurate to virtue accruing to all citizens. Technical schools where profitable trades are learned are a must and almost go without saying, but what Pacifica should do over and above that is to encourage and provide training and legal avenues for brave young men to go abroad and make their fortunes, perhaps in the form of a Pacific Privateer force. (Author, 18 November 2026, 05:51)

**H-19 (direction APPROVED; shape APPROVED 18 November 2026, 06:01; own CR).** Every citizen gets an honest road to status, property and marriage, examined or not:
- **Technical schools for the profitable trades** (APPROVED; "almost go without saying").
- **Over and above them**, state training and legal avenues for brave young men to go abroad and make their fortunes. **Form PROPOSED**: the author's "perhaps … a Pacific Privateer force".

**PROPOSED: the form** (for the author; drafted 18 November 2026).

1. **Privateering in the strict sense is a war power only.** A letter of marque licenses a private ship against an enemy's commerce, in a declared war, under a prize court; in peace the same act is piracy. The Declaration of Paris (April 1856) abolished it among its signers; the United States refused to sign. **OPEN:** whether the Republic accedes. If it stays outside, letters of marque are a real tool in canon's wars: against Russian shipping in 1878 (§14), in 1898–99 and 1904–05, with prize courts at San Francisco and Honolulu and the prize shares as the fortune. In the forty years of peace between them, they are no avenue at all.
2. **The peacetime avenue: licensed service and enterprise abroad.** Three historical models, combined:
   - **The Swiss capitulations.** For three centuries the cantons signed treaties with foreign crowns that supplied whole regiments. The treaties fixed pay, kept the men's own officers and courts, and brought wealth and honour home. That was the honest road abroad for the poor mountain cantons' brave sons, until the federal ban of 1859.
   - **Frederick Townsend Ward's Ever-Victorious Army.** A Salem sailor turned adventurer drilled a Chinese force for the Qing in 1860–62, a natural Pacifican prototype a year before the war.
   - **Land for service.** The United States' bounty-land warrants for its veterans (1847–55), and the Pioneer Column's promise of farms and gold claims (1890), without its conquest.
3. **What the Realm provides (PROPOSED elements):**
   - **A school of the companies.** Admission is by trial of body and nerve, not by paper: riding, shooting, seamanship, surveying, field medicine and a working tongue. Rank inside goes by deeds before witnesses (H6.4, 3).
   - **Capitulations with partners.** Companies serve abroad under treaty with their own officers, pay and justice: guard, gendarmerie or the Customs' preventive service for Korea, China, Siam and the Philippines' crown; railway guards in Manchuria and Siberia. Canon already has railway guards and a Pacifican-led Customs Inspectorate from 1885.
   - **Licensed ventures.** Mining, sealing, pearling, plantation and trading ventures abroad, each under a Realm licence and bond and only on a concession the host government grants, with the Realm's consuls and courts behind them. The Unsan gold concession in Korea (1895) is a ready case.
   - **Rewards at home.** A Land Fund warrant for years served, honoured like the inland command's; a company commission counts for the Franchise Law's third vote (**a canon tension**: CR-021's "commission"); the roll of deeds; and the county festivals.
4. **What it may never be (canon's limits):**
   - **No filibustering.** In 1853–54 the Republic cut off Walker's supplies and tried his men (§5). The legal road is the answer to that one, not a return to it.
   - **No service in the Americas.** Pacifica is "the giant its neighbours need not fear" (CR-058), and Mexico remembers Walker.
   - **No land taken except by compact.** Paid labour only, and no campaign of extermination (protected intent).
   - **No covert arms** for the rump Confederacy or anyone else (CR-048 A2).
5. **Where it sits.** Its roots run from 1854 (after Walker) and 1860 (Ward). Its statute is the settler's promise of 1887–88, beside the labour article (H-16), and its life runs past 1932. **Recommendation:** its own CR for the institution, with CR-071 carrying only the promise of 1887 and the statute's name.

**OPEN:**
- the name ("Privateers" is exact only in war);
- accession to the Declaration of Paris;
- the partners and the first capitulation;
- the warrant's size;
- whether the 1887 promise is in the Constitution or an organic law;
- the separate CR.
