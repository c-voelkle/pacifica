## I1. The drawing rules — APPROVED (I-6)

As in E4, with what this part adds:

1. **Watershed first** (Powell, 1878).
2. **One railway station or port as seat**, within a day's travel of every part of the county.
3. **Population within a band** around the average (about 47,000 in 1887), widened in the empty interior, where the day's travel governs.
4. **Language respected** where a county can be drawn round a Spanish- or Hawaiian-speaking district.
5. **The historic lands keep their outline.** California's outer boundary survives as county lines; Hawaii's is the sea. Elsewhere no county reproduces a member, none is named for one, and no county's lines enclose a member whole.
6. **Homelands are drawn round, not through** (I-8).

A consequence: outside California and Hawaii the counties cross the old member lines, so the counts "by old unit" in I3 are accounting, not boundaries.

## I4. The homelands — I-8 and I-9 APPROVED; the rest PROPOSED

- **Two sizes.** The large homelands are units of their own on the map, outside any county: the Diné country (the test: larger than any county, and across the old New Mexico and Arizona lines), the Ute country of the Western Slope and the Parks, and others to be listed region by region. The small ones (the Californian homelands, the Pueblo grants) lie inside a county's lines; the county's board, taxes and courts stop at their boundary unless the nation contracts for a service.
- **The relation** runs to the Realm under each compact; disputes go to the Court of the Realm at Monterey. County powers, boundaries and grades are organic law, amendable without local consent (§25); a homeland's are not.
- **They do not count toward the 124.**
- **Voting (I-9).** A member of a nation who lives in a county registers and votes there like anyone else, and votes only where he is registered.
- **The Senate (I-9, I-11, I-32, APPROVED).** **Seven homeland seats** (the seventh for Alaska's nations, chosen PROPOSED at the Northern Commission's yearly council). The first six: **six homeland seats**, beside the eight aliʻi seats, each chosen by the councils of the compact nations of one region, in their own way: California; the Columbia and the Sound; the Basin (Shoshone, Paiute, Washoe and the Utes of Deseret); the Rockies' Utes; the Diné; the Pueblos and the Apache.

### New Mexico and the Diné country (draft 1, PROPOSED)

**The Diné country.** The homeland of the treaty of 1865 (§15) is a unit of its own on the map, outside every county (I-8). Its southern line runs north of the thirty-fifth-parallel railway, so the line, Gallup, its coal and the other settlements along it stay in the counties (I-18, APPROVED). PROPOSED for the rest: the San Juan on the north; on the east the Chaco country to Mount Taylor; on the west across the Little Colorado to the San Francisco Peaks. That is on the order of 30,000 square miles, larger than any county of the 124, and it lies across the old New Mexico and Arizona lines, so no county line is drawn through it. About 20,000 people in 1887 (MODEL, to be sourced: no Long Walk in this timeline). It chooses the Diné seat in the Senate (I-11). The Hopi mesas lie inside it as a homeland of their own (terms below).

**The four mountains (APPROVED, I-19).** The four are the corners of a kite of about 28,000 square miles that fits the Diné's own country badly: it takes in the Chama valley, Conejos and the Utes' and Jicarilla's ground, and leaves out Kayenta and Navajo Mountain. So the homeland is the country the Diné hold, drawn out to the two mountains it can reach:
- **Mount Taylor and the San Francisco Peaks:** the summits and the northern slopes lie inside the homeland; the southern feet, where the railway, Cubero, Cebolleta and Flagstaff are, stay in the counties (I-18). Both are sacred to other nations as well (Acoma, Laguna and Zuni; the Hopi and the Havasupai), and the compact writes their access in.
- **Hesperus and Blanca Peak** stand in the Utes' country and above the San Luis Valley. A mountain article withdraws their upper slopes from grant, lease and Land Fund sale and keeps passage open for ceremony; the ground stays where it is.
- The oil of the San Juan basin, the helium and the uranium of the Carrizo and Monument Valley country lie north of the railway, inside the homeland, to be leased through the Realm with the rent paid to the nation (CR-033; a later CR).
