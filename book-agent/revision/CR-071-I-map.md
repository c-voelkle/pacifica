## I1. The drawing rules — APPROVED (I-6)

As in E4, with what this part adds:

1. **Watershed first** (Powell, 1878).
2. **One railway station or port as seat**, within a day's travel of every part of the county.
3. **Population within a band** around the average (about 47,000 in 1887), widened in the empty interior, where the day's travel governs.
4. **Language respected** where a county can be drawn round a Spanish- or Hawaiian-speaking district.
5. **The historic lands keep their outline.** California's outer boundary survives as county lines; Hawaii's is the sea. Elsewhere no county reproduces a member, none is named for one, and no county's lines enclose a member whole.
6. **Homelands are drawn round, not through** (I-8).

A consequence: outside California and Hawaii the counties cross the old member lines, so the counts "by old unit" in I3 are accounting, not boundaries.

## I4. The homelands — I-8, I-9, I-11, I-26, I-32 and I-46 to I-60 APPROVED; details PROPOSED

- **Two sizes.** The large homelands are units of their own on the map, outside any county: the Diné country (the test: larger than any county, and across the old New Mexico and Arizona lines), the Ute country of the Western Slope and the Parks, and others to be listed region by region. The small ones (the Californian homelands, the Pueblo grants) lie inside a county's lines; the county's board, taxes and courts stop at their boundary unless the nation contracts for a service.
- **The relation** runs to the Realm under each compact; disputes go to the Court of the Realm at Monterey. County powers, boundaries and grades are organic law, amendable without local consent (§25); a homeland's are not.
- **They do not count toward the 124.**
- **Voting (I-9).** A member of a nation who lives in a county registers and votes there like anyone else, and votes only where he is registered.
- **The Senate (I-9, I-11, I-32, APPROVED).** **Seven homeland seats** (the seventh for Alaska's nations, chosen PROPOSED at the Northern Commission's yearly council). The first six: **six homeland seats**, beside the eight aliʻi seats, each chosen by the councils of the compact nations of one region, in their own way: California; the Columbia and the Sound; the Basin (Shoshone, Paiute, Washoe and the Utes of Deseret); the Rockies' Utes; the Diné; the Pueblos and the Apache.
- **Who joins which seat (I-64, APPROVED).** (1) California and Baja; (2) the Columbia and the Sound, with the plateau peoples (Flathead, Bitterroot Salish, Nez Perce, Coeur d'Alene, Klamath); (3) the Basin; (4) the Rockies' Utes; (5) the Diné; (6) the Pueblos, the Hopi, the Apache and Arizona's river and desert peoples. The Hopi sit with the Pueblos, not with the Diné.

## I4a. The homelands' lines (draft 2, PROPOSED; redrawn from Royce on the author's request of 16 November, 17:35)

**Source.** The author supplied Royce, *Indian Land Cessions in the United States* (Bureau of American Ethnology, 18th Annual Report, 1899; the 1971 reprint). Its schedule gives each treaty's or order's boundary in words. The lines below are placed from those words: parallels, meridians and distances exactly, rivers from a river map, crests and creeks within a few miles. They are treaty lines, not surveys. Read in the schedule this session: the Colville order of 1872, the Moqui (Hopi) order of 1882, the Yakama reserve of 1855, the Ute treaty of 1868, the Klamath reserve of 1864, the Fort Hall order of 1867, the White Mountain order of 1871 and the Chiricahua order of 1872. The Nez Perce reserve of 1855 and the Uinta order of 1861 were read later the same night (17 November) in the schedule and checked against the plates for Idaho (CXXIII, nos. 441–442) and Utah (no. 431); both lines were corrected (below).

**The rule (APPROVED, I-46).** A homeland's line is the line of the agreement that made it. Pacifica keeps its own treaties and assumes the Union's ratified ones (CR-059 N-B; CR-060 N1). Where our timeline drew the line only after 1861, it is used as a first size and the agreement is Pacifica's own, to be dated. A cut our timeline made later is not made (the Nez Perce, 1863), except where it took out a mining district or a settled valley, which here is county ground or leased ground anyway (I-34).

**Counties follow the homelands.** Each county is its historical counties less the homelands. Strips under about twelve kilometres wide go to the homeland beside them; a piece cut off from its county goes to the neighbour it borders (`population/outputs/homelands_1887.md` lists every such change). No county is divided by a homeland.

### New Mexico and the Diné country — APPROVED (I-15 to I-20)

**The Diné country.** The homeland of the treaty of 1865 (§12) is a unit of its own on the map, outside every county (I-8). Its southern line runs north of the thirty-fifth-parallel railway, so the line, Gallup, its coal and the other settlements along it stay in the counties (I-18, APPROVED). **The treaty's own words (I-63, APPROVED):** a treaty of 1865 cannot name a railway built in 1880–83 or a town of 1881. Barboncito will not have degrees; the line is described by landmarks (the Puerco of the West, named springs, Ojo del Oso), and the southern line runs north of Beale's wagon road. An article reserves the strip along the road for "a road of wagons or of iron", with compensation; the railway runs where the treaty left room for it. As drawn (I4a): the San Juan on the north; on the east the Chaco country to Mount Taylor; on the west across the Little Colorado to the San Francisco Peaks. That is about 24,000 square miles with the Hopi country (I4a), larger than any county of the 124, and it lies across the old New Mexico and Arizona lines, so no county line is drawn through it. About 20,000 people in 1887 (MODEL). The sources: an incomplete count gave 7,300 in 1867 and fewer than 9,000 in 1869, after the Long Walk; the census of 1890, on a faulty system, gave 17,204 enumerated and estimated, of whom about 11,000 were enumerated in Arizona, 5,109 in New Mexico and 993 in Utah or roaming (the parts do not sum to the total, which includes estimates; the Arizona figure is 11,012 or 11,042 in different transcriptions) (Special Agent Marmon's report of 1891; summarised in Johnston, *An Analysis of Sources of Information on the Population of the Navaho*, BAE Bulletin 197, 1966). With no Long Walk and no Bosque Redondo here, 20,000 by 1887 is at the high end but defensible. It chooses the Diné seat in the Senate (I-11). The Hopi mesas lie inside it as a homeland of their own (terms below).

**The four mountains (APPROVED, I-19).** The four are the corners of a kite of about 28,000 square miles that fits the Diné's own country badly: it takes in the Chama valley, Conejos and the Utes' and Jicarilla's ground, and leaves out Kayenta and Navajo Mountain. So the homeland is the country the Diné hold, drawn out to the two mountains it can reach:
- **Mount Taylor and the San Francisco Peaks:** the summits and the northern slopes lie inside the homeland; the southern feet, where the railway, Cubero, Cebolleta and Flagstaff are, stay in the counties (I-18). Both are sacred to other nations as well (Acoma, Laguna and Zuni; the Hopi and the Havasupai), and the compact writes their access in.
- **Hesperus and Blanca Peak.** Hesperus stands in the Utes' country (I-68, approved 17 Nov, 09:36); Blanca Peak on county ground above the San Luis Valley. A mountain article withdraws their upper slopes from grant, lease and Land Fund sale and keeps passage open for ceremony; the ground stays where it is.
- The oil of the San Juan basin, the helium and the uranium of the Carrizo and Monument Valley country lie north of the railway, inside the homeland, to be leased through the Realm with the rent paid to the nation (CR-033; a later CR).

**The Hopi (HP1 to HP7 APPROVED, I-20).** Hist.: each village governed itself under its own kikmongwi; seven Hopi leaders went to Santa Fe in 1850 to ask the agent Calhoun for help against Navajo raids; the Hopi never signed a treaty, and the reservation of 1882 (about 2.47 million acres, a rectangle of longitude and latitude) gave them use, not ownership, left out Moenkopi and was later surrounded by the Navajo reservation.
