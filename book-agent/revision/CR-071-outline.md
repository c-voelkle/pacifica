## 0. What canon already fixes

- **1856:** Confederation of California and Hawaii; residual powers with the members (§6; CR-021 D).
- **1861–65:** occupation, compacts and association: Oregon's civil compact (1861), the Rio Grande compact (1863), Deseret's Compact of Association (1863), Washoe retaken (1864) (CR-059–061).
- **1866–67:** charters of accession voted in 1866 by Oregon, Washington Territory (which names itself Columbia), Idaho, Washoe, western Montana, New Mexico (with Arizona county) and Deseret; all become members on the exchange of ratifications in 1867. Each charter is entrenched and amendable only with its own member's consent (CR-059 V10, CT8; CR-060 A5), and yields to a constitution adopted by the Articles' procedure (the article of supersession, K-15, APPROVED; F–G section T). Alaska comes in 1867 as a territory under the Northern Commission (CR-070).
- **1865:** Enabling Law; Elias is Providentor for life.
- **1865–87:** confederal grants for water, railways and land go straight to county boards. "By 1887 the states are hollow" (CR-021 B5).
- **1871:** Japan abolishes the domains for prefectures, "which Elias watched closely" (CR-021 A). 1872: the Iwakura Mission lands at San Francisco (§14). 1877: Pacifica backs Tokyo against Saigō (§15).
- **1887:** Imperial Constitution. The states are abolished for **124 counties** with rotated Commissioners. California and Hawaii survive as historic lands. Deseret is split across several counties. Ratified by two-thirds of both chambers and both founding states, "with the interior states' legislatures bought with land and railway grants" (CR-021 D).
- **After 1887:** three grades of county, league tables, receivership, one senator per county chosen by its board (CR-021 B3; §25).

So the end state is fixed. This CR supplies the **causes, the method, the opponents and the map**.

## C. The method before 1871: hollowing (1865–1871)

- C1. **The grant machinery (1865–1871). APPROVED (author, 17 November 2026, 10:23: "These work for me").** Canon: from 1865 Elias uses the Enabling Law to deal with county boards over the members' heads, through water grants, railway subsidies and land sales (§25; CR-021 B5). The machinery:
  - **Who applies.** A county board (or, from the 1870s, an irrigation district or a railway company the board backs) petitions the confederal Superintendency of Mines and Public Revenue (canon, 1852) for a grant or Development Fund credit for named works. The member's legislature is not a party and need not be told.
  - **Who audits.** The Superintendency's auditors, examined and posted from outside the county. The accounts are published each year beside the next county's: the first comparison tables, two decades before the Office of Public Measures (1888).
  - **What the board signs away.** (1) Its works' accounts, open to confederal audit; (2) the works' route and purpose, which no later board or member law may change without confederal consent; (3) any member tax on the works or their revenue; (4) the forum: disputes go to the Confederal Court, not the member's courts. In return the board gets money its member could never give it, and credit at the centre's rate.
  - **What it does.** By 1871 most interior boards spend more confederal money on works than member money (PROPOSED, no figure). Supervisors learn to look to Monterey; the member's legislature learns that the works in its own counties are not its own. Nothing is taken from the members; everything new goes round them.
- C2. **The first quarrel: Oregon's Land Act (1869–1870). APPROVED (as C1; the case from I-65).** The Chinese floor of Oregon's charter (CR-059 X5) has opened Oregon to Chinese Pacificans, and it provokes the first anti-Chinese movement inside Pacifica in southern Oregon and Idaho (CR-059 S4). In 1869 the Breckinridge men of Jackson and Josephine carry a Land Act through Oregon's legislature: no Land Fund sale in Oregon to any person whom the 1857 constitution would have barred from holding land, and a member tax on assisted settlers' instalments.
  - **The case.** A Chinese Pacifican from California, holding a Land Fund contract for a farm in the Rogue valley, refuses the tax (his name OPEN). The Confederal Court strikes the Act in 1870 on three grounds: the Land Fund's title is confederal and no member may attach a condition of race to it; the charter's floor (X5) protects a citizen of another member; and no member tax lies on a confederal grant (C1).
  - **What Oregon keeps.** Its residence power over non-citizens and its exclusion article (CR-059 X8) are untouched, and Elias says so in public: the Court has held only what the charter says. Assisted settlers who are not yet citizens are routed to Columbia and Idaho until they naturalise. The residence power ends in 1887 (G-6).
  - **What it teaches.** The legislature lost to a farmer it could not tax, before a court it could not appeal, on a contract it had never seen. It is the first time a member learns that its own counties' business is no longer its own. The Chronicler records the case in its year; the reader connects it at 1887.
- C4. **(Settled.)** Alaska's Northern Commission (1867) as the prototype: a territory governed by compact and commission, with no legislature at all (Part E, E7). Baja's commission of 1867–87 is a second case (Part I, I-39, I-54), the purchase approved by the author on 17 November (Part I, I-61).
- C5. **The settlement programme (1867–1887). APPROVED in principle (Part I, I-4); its contents and figures PROPOSED.** The hollowing has a second purpose: the states are emptied of power while the interior is filled with people, so that by 1887 the acceded units hold about twice what they held in our timeline (1.17 million more people, MODEL; Part I, I2) and no member can claim its people owe it their land. The money and the land are confederal, so the settlers' first loyalty is to the Land Fund office that sold them their farm, the district that waters it and the confederal office that paid their passage, not to the member's legislature.
  - **Land.** The members' land enters the Land Fund from 1867 (L15). It is bought from the nations whose country it is, by compact (CR-033), surveyed, and sold in 160-acre farms on five years' instalments at the Land Fund's price (the higher of the $1.25 floor and a share of census value; the floor binds to about 1880); about 105,000 farms by 1887 (MODEL). The homelands are never in it (I-8).
  - **Passage.** Emigration offices at Hamburg, Liverpool, Christiania, Chicago and Canton, and at Yokohama under the Japan treaty (canon; historically Japan forbade emigration until 1885, apart from the party of 1868), 1867–1887. About a third of the arrivals have their passage paid or advanced, repaid with the land instalments where they farm. The offices place no one by origin (CR-037): they offer what there is (irrigated farms, railway-crew land, mining and smelter work), and arrivals choose. Where Asian arrivals take the irrigated farms and the railway towns, they may become the majority in a county (I-5): the Snake plain, Salt River and Yuma, Washoe's mining and railway towns.
  - **Water.** Public irrigation districts on Deseret's model: the confederal Treasury lends to a district for its dam and canals, and the district repays from water rates. About 0.9 million acres watered by 1887 (MODEL).
  - **Railways.** Companies chartered by the confederal chambers with Land Fund grants, ten sections to the mile: the Raton line (1878–79), the thirty-fifth parallel (1880–83) and the Snake line on the dates of our timeline's Oregon Short Line and its connections (1881–84) (I-53). Land, not cash. No grant comes from a homeland: the Snake line crosses Fort Hall at the Portneuf and passes the Umatilla reserve, and each crossing is a right-of-way compact with the nation.
  - **Anchors.** Smelters, coal and timber, on private capital with confederal loans, give the mining and forest counties their railway town (I1 rule 2).
  - **Diversion.** A share of the Union's plains settlers is recruited to the Palouse and the Sound; they pay their own way.
  - **Cost** (`population/settlement_1887.py` → `population/outputs/settlement_1887.md`, MODEL, dollars of about 1880): about $26 million out over 1867–87; about $25 million back by the end of 1887 and $7.5 million later, most of it land receipts.  The land is a change of timing, not new acres: L15 sells our timeline's census acreage as it grew, mostly after 1887, and the programme sells about 17 million of those acres earlier. **In the workbook (I-67):** assisted passage is already there (People T × `assist_cost`, about $109m in 1867–87); the build adds the irrigation loans and their repayments and the interior's farms sold early and missing later (Events E243–E246, `finance/CR-071-settlement-workbook.md`). Net: about $18 million over 1867–1932, moving tax burden from the 1880s to the 1890s–1900s; Story checks 0, Stress 50 (was 51).
  - **For the author.** (1) Part I's earlier line had "the trunk railways about a decade early"; I-53 keeps the three named lines on our timeline's dates, so that phrase is withdrawn. If an earlier line is wanted anywhere, it is the Sacramento–Siskiyou line of 1865–67, which the workbook already carries. (2) **Who and against whom (I-65, APPROVED, 17 November).** John Wesley Powell heads the land and water office; an emigration agent such as Hans Mattson runs the passage offices; Oregon's legislature passes a Land Act barring Land Fund sales by race and taxing assisted settlers, and loses in the Confederal Court, the "first quarrel" of C2 (its residence power untouched until 1887); Fort Hall's council leases the Portneuf right of way for a rent instead of selling it, the precedent for every crossing; and at Rock Springs, in Green River county, a riot against Chinese miners in 1885 is stopped by the sheriff and its killers tried, as at Boise in 1866. Showing refusals and bargains keeps seventeen million acres bought in twenty years from reading as dispossession by compact. ## E. Preparation (1872–1886)

Drafted in Part E. What has to be true before the vote can be called:

## J. Aftermath (1887–1900) — APPROVED (author, 17 November 2026, 10:23: "These work for me"); names OPEN

- J1. **The first Commissioners arrive (1888): one good posting, one bad.** The first rotation comes from the examined men of 1879–87 (B4), each posted outside his home county (CR-021 B3).
  - **The good posting: a Spanish county of the Rio Grande** (Taos or Gallinas). The Commissioner is a Californio of the examined service who must and can work in Spanish (the county language, I-30). He keeps the old alcaldes' habits of hearing complaints in the plaza, publishes the county's accounts in both languages, and in his third year the county's school passes rise above the best of New Mexico's old counties. The county's own men had expected an Anglo from Sacramento.
  - **The bad posting: Hilo county** (I-41; not Oʻahu, whose Commissioner offers the Queen the reserve in July 1889, H4). The Commissioner has passed the Hawaiian examination (I-44) but treats it as a qualification, not a language; he speaks English to the Queen's people and records their petitions through an interpreter. He replaces an island governor of the Queen (Part I, the Hawaii section, "What the counties replace") and is the face of the loyalists' charge (CR-072 P23) in the months before the Honolulu rising of 1889 (Part H, H-11). Victoria recalls him early in 1889, before the rising, and it happens anyway.
  - Names OPEN; historical people are preferred where an examined Californio of the right age can be found.
- J2. **The first receivership and the first charter.**
  - **Receivership, July 1892: Coeur d'Alene.** Historically the Coeur d'Alene mine owners' lockout and the miners' union's war of 1892 brought martial law (Hist.). Here the Regent declares a disorder in the county (CR-021 B3: a disorder declared by the Sovereign, faster than the three-year test), suspends the board, which the owners hold, and a commission governs. It enforces the paid-labour code against both sides (CR-031-R1; CR-038), tries the men who blew up the Frisco mill and the mine guards who fired into the crowd, and hands the county back in 1895, the year of the trades congress (CR-038). The first receivership is of an owners' board, not a workers' county.
  - **The first charter, 1893: Salt Lake.** Five years at the Office's four standards from the Public Measures law of 1888 make 1893 the earliest year. The first county to qualify is Salt Lake: high examination passes, low infant mortality, order. The people most suspicious of the centre top its tables. The charter waits a year on the inspectors' test that no county may be captured by a congregation (CR-037), and the Church's men on the board resign their Church offices to keep it. Salt Lake is chartered in 1894.
- J3. **What ordinary people notice.** The sheriff stays (elected by the county, as before). The school examiner is new, examined and from elsewhere. The tax bill has one line less: the member's levy is gone, and the county's line is longer. The courthouse keeps its building and changes its seal. In Oregon nothing is announced: the exclusion law lapses with the member (G-6), and the first Black family to buy a Willamette farm in 1888 does so without anyone saying why it can. In Olympia the capitol becomes the Columbia circuit's courthouse (K-16). In Carson the Comstock's owners learn that their tax cap has gone with Washoe (K-17).
- J4. **What the Chronicler's own time thinks of it.** By the Chronicler's own day the 124 counties are simply the map. The old members survive as the names of circuits, regions and songs: Columbia at Olympia, Washoe in a miners' ballad, Deseret in the Saints' own speech. His contemporaries call 1887 the bloodless reform and set it beside Meiji Japan's abolition of the domains, and the Chronicler lets them. What he notes, without comment, is the cost the reader has seen: with the members gone there was no place below the Crown where an opposition could hold power, so county oligarchies fought through their senators (the Senate of 1910–11 and the refusal of 1927; CR-021 B5) and the counties' only shield against the centre was the Senate they chose.
