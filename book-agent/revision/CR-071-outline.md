## 0. What canon already fixes

- **1856:** Confederation of California and Hawaii; residual powers with the members (§6; CR-021 D).
- **1861–65:** occupation, compacts and association: Oregon's civil compact (1861), the Rio Grande compact (1863), Deseret's Compact of Association (1863), Washoe retaken (1864) (CR-059–061).
- **1866–67:** charters of accession voted in 1866 by Oregon, Washington Territory (which names itself Columbia), Idaho, Washoe, western Montana, New Mexico (with Arizona county) and Deseret; all become members on the exchange of ratifications in 1867. Each charter is entrenched and amendable only with its own member's consent (CR-059 V10, CT8; CR-060 A5), and yields to a constitution adopted by the Articles' procedure (the article of supersession, K-15, APPROVED; F–G section T). Alaska comes in 1867 as a territory under the Northern Commission (CR-070).
- **1865:** Enabling Law; Elias is Providentor for life.
- **1865–87:** confederal grants for water, railways and land go straight to county boards. "By 1887 the states are hollow" (CR-021 B5).
- **1871:** Japan abolishes the domains for prefectures, "which Elias watched closely" (CR-021 A). 1872: the Iwakura Mission lands at San Francisco (§14). 1877: Pacifica backs Tokyo against Saigō (§15).
- **1887:** Imperial Constitution. The states are abolished for **124 counties** with rotated Commissioners. California and Hawaii survive as historic lands. Deseret is split across several counties. Ratified by two-thirds of both chambers and both founding states, "with the interior states' legislatures bought with land and railway grants" (CR-021 D).
- **After 1887:** three grades of county, league tables, receivership, one senator per county chosen by its board (CR-021 B3; §25).

So the end state is fixed. This CR supplies the **causes, the method, the opponents and the map**.

  That gives **nine members and eight member legislatures** in the 1880s. Arizona is not a member: it is Arizona county of New Mexico. The Western Slope and the Parks are a Ute homeland with no settlers to vote (CR-060); South Park and the upper Arkansas join with the interior in 1867 (CR-060, population note). Alaska is a territory, not a member.
- A2. What each compact reserved: Hawaii's crown and institutions; Deseret's marriage and church-property law; Oregon's exclusion law; Columbia's charter.
- A3. The Council after the accessions: who sits, with how many votes. (In 1864 it is 18 seats, California 9 and Hawaii 9.) **Canon (CR-059 CT8, CR-060 A5; author, 12 November 2026):** every new member has a Council delegation equal with the others, and Assembly seats by population. Washoe's legislature chooses its delegation (CR-073 R24). **PROPOSED (K-10):** Hawaii's delegation is chosen by the Territorial Council from 1876, as the Council has the legislature's business (E6). The two-thirds of April 1887 is then six of nine delegations: the Party holds Oregon, Columbia, Idaho, western Montana, Washoe and New Mexico, Hawaii's is the Crown's, and California's changes hands with its legislature in November 1886. Only Deseret's is against.

*Settled:* Arizona, New Mexico, Washoe and the interior were not governed as counties from the start; canon makes New Mexico and the interior members ("the new state", §10; "the interior states", CR-021). The prototype C3 is withdrawn (E-11).

## B. Causes: why the states had to go

Long-run causes, each to be tied to an event already in canon:
- B1. **The veto.** Washoe 1860 and the 5:4 California delegation of 1864 taught Elias that members can block him through his own constitution.
- B2. **Water and rails cross state lines.** The grants of 1865–87 already treat the county as the working unit.
- B3. **One market, one code.** State charters, state courts and state codes are a patchwork the champions cannot use. (Banking is not part of it: it has followed the currency to the centre since 1857, CR-057 G13.)
- B4. **The examination state needs postings.** A rotated, examined service has nowhere to serve while governors appoint.
- B5. **Succession.** A regency cannot hold eight member governments the way the founder could. The states must be gone before he is.
- B6. **Thalassia.** A capital a week from the continent needs its own officers in every county seat.
- B7. **The Union.** A member state is something Washington can court, recognise or restore (Port Townsend 1863–64, Salem 1866). A county is not.

*To decide:* which two or three causes the Chronicler names, and which he leaves for the reader.

## C. The method before 1871: hollowing (1865–1871)

- C1. The grant machinery: who applies, who audits, what the board signs away.
- C2. The first quarrel: a state legislature that tries to tax, veto or re-route a confederal grant, and loses.
- C3. ~~The prototype~~ **WITHDRAWN (E-11, APPROVED).** Canon makes the lands taken in 1861–65 members from 1867, not counties under commissioners.
- C4. Alaska's Northern Commission (1867) as the one prototype: a territory governed by compact and commission, with no legislature at all (Part E, E7).
- C5. **The settlement programme (1867–1887). APPROVED in principle (Part I, I-4); its contents and figures PROPOSED.** The hollowing has a second purpose: the states are emptied of power while the interior is filled with people, so that by 1887 the acceded units hold about twice what they held in our timeline (1.17 million more people, MODEL; Part I, I2) and no member can claim its people owe it their land. The money and the land come from the Realm, so the settlers' first loyalty is to the county board that sold them their farm and the confederal office that paid their passage, not to the member's legislature.
  - **Land.** The members' land enters the Land Fund from 1867 (L15). It is bought from the nations whose country it is, by compact (CR-033), surveyed, and sold in 160-acre farms on five years' instalments at the Fund's floor of $1.25 an acre; about 105,000 farms by 1887 (MODEL). The homelands are never in it (I-8).
  - **Passage.** Emigration offices at Hamburg, Liverpool, Christiania, Chicago, Yokohama and Canton (1867–1887). About a third of the arrivals have their passage paid or advanced, repaid with the land instalments where they farm. Asian settlers go where their coming disrupts least, and may be the majority in a county (I-5): the irrigation districts of the Snake plain, Salt River and Yuma, Washoe's mining and railway towns.
  - **Water.** Public irrigation districts on Deseret's model: the Realm lends to a district for its dam and canals, and the district repays from water rates. About 0.9 million acres watered by 1887 (MODEL).
  - **Railways.** Companies chartered by the Realm with Land Fund grants, ten sections to the mile: the Raton line (1878–79), the thirty-fifth parallel (1880–83) and the Salt Lake–Snake–Columbia line (1881–84), on our timeline's dates (I-53). Land, not cash.
  - **Anchors.** Smelters, coal and timber, on private capital with Realm loans, give the mining and forest counties their railway town (I1 rule 4).
  - **Diversion.** A share of the Union's plains settlers is recruited to the Palouse and the Sound; they pay their own way.
  - **Cost** (`population/settlement_1887.py` → `population/outputs/settlement_1887.md`, MODEL, dollars of about 1880): about $26 million out over 1867–87 and about $31 million back, most of it land receipts. New to the financial workbook: about $11.6 million over twenty-one years (assisted passage net of repayment $5.6m, the offices $3.1m, the irrigation loans' losses $2.8m), with $11.2 million lent to the districts and repaid over about twenty years. The land receipts are already in the workbook (L15) and are not added again. **Workbook step pending:** a Programme row for the new items and the districts' loans as a balance, to be built when the author has seen these figures.
  - **For the author.** Part I's earlier line had "the trunk railways about a decade early"; I-53 keeps the three named lines on our timeline's dates, so that phrase is withdrawn. If an earlier line is wanted anywhere, it is the Sacramento–Siskiyou line of 1865–67, which the workbook already carries.

## D. The Meiji lesson (1869–1878)

The influence runs both ways: Pacifica's advisers are in Japan from 1868, and what comes back is a proof of concept. Drafted in Part D.

| Japan (Hist.; checked in Part D) | What Elias could take from it |
|---|---|
| 1869: the lords return their registers and stay on as governors | Change the title first, the office later |
| 1871: domains abolished by edict, with a guard force assembled beforehand; lords called to live in Tokyo | Have the force and the money in place before the announcement; move the men, not only the map |
| The centre assumes domain debts and stipends | Buy the creditors and the office-holders |
| Governors appointed from outside | The Commissioner who never serves at home (canon; CR-037 gives the Qing root, this adds the Meiji one) |
| 302 prefectures merged to 72, about 45 by 1888 (sources give 43 to 47) | Redraw twice: the first map is provisional |
| Peerage for the former lords | Appointed seats in the Senate ("up to a quarter appointed for life", canon), held to sixty-eight (CR-038) |
| 1876–77: stipends commuted, then the Satsuma Rebellion | The warning: the losers must not keep an army. Absorb the militias first. |

- D1. Who carries the lesson: the Iwakura Mission in 1872, Pacifica's army mission, Kuroda's Hokkaido office, Hamada Hikozō, Kiyoshi.
- D2. A scene: Elias with Ōkubo or Kido at San Francisco in 1872, each explaining to the other what he has just done or means to do.
- D3. Where Pacifica differs: Japan merged many units into few; Pacifica breaks few into many. Japan had an emperor to issue the edict; Elias has two chambers and member legislatures and must buy a vote.
- D4. The other model, the French departments of 1790 (canon), and John Wesley Powell's 1878 proposal to draw arid-land units by watershed.

## E. Preparation (1872–1886)

Drafted in Part E. What has to be true before the vote can be called:
- E1. **Force.** State militias, the Nauvoo Legion included, pass under the Defense Law (the Militia Act of 1882).
- E2. **Money.** The Confederation assumes member debts (the Assumption Act of 1879). Banks are not part of the bargain: they have been under the Reserve Bank since 1857.
- E3. **Men.** Examined officials are posted to member administrations; judges of the member courts are offered seats in the Court of the Realm's system.
- E4. **The survey.** A boundary commission draws the lines years ahead, by watershed, railway and language. Judah heads it.
- E5. **The old counties.** Elias's allies since 1865 are the roughly 200 American-style county boards. The 124 are fewer and larger, so some of his allies lose their courthouse. How he keeps them.
- E6. **Hawaii.** Its position from 1874 to 1887 (CT1, resolved).

## I. The map: the 124 counties

- I3. **The named list**, with seats, parts, people and county languages: Part I, I3a, and `population/outputs/map_1887.md`. People are MODEL (5.84 million in the model, 5.79 million in the 124).
- I4. **Native homelands** (I-8, I-9, I-26, I-46 to I-52, I-56 to I-60): compact lands, not counties. Large homelands (over about 3,000 square miles or across two watersheds) are units of their own outside the 124; smaller ones are enclaves inside a county's lines and outside its board's reach. No county is divided by a homeland. The lines follow Royce where our timeline has a text (I4a). The Diné homeland is the largest; the Hopi country lies inside it (HP1 to HP7). The Apache line of 1871 is the Apache country's from 1865 (I-52).
- I5. **Alaska** (I-7, APPROVED): the Northern Commission continues; Alaska is outside the 124 (CT3).
- I6. **Changes after 1887:** mergers, splits, new Crown cities (OPEN-021-12), the Hawaiian restoration of 1907 (CR-040). OPEN.
