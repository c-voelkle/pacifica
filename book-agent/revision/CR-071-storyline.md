## B. New text, in storyline order

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…President Vallejo hesitates before confronting a powerful body of merchants and armed volunteers." (K-18)

> <!-- CR-071: the capital at Sacramento --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The legislature moves to Sacramento in December 1849, to the gateway of the registered gravels, and sits there through the flood of January 1850 with the squatters' quarrel under its windows. Monterey keeps the courts, the academy and the republic's memory as its first capital.

### §12, after the paragraph beginning "**[PROPOSED — CR-060]** In the Rockies the line follows what each side can hold" (A-1, I-68)

> <!-- CR-071: the Utes' alliance and the Mountain Districts --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The Utes have earned their terms. Ute guides led the column up the San Luis Valley in 1863, and through the winters of 1863–65 the Ute bands held the passes against the Union's Colorado volunteers. The mountain districts that vote themselves over never become a member: South Park and the upper Arkansas enter as districts under a confederal commission at Fairplay, each with its own miners' code. **[OPEN]** Whether the massacre at Sand Creek happens.

### §12, the paragraph beginning "**[APPROVED — CR-015]** In 1865 a treaty recognizes the Navajo homeland." — add after "…No Navajo walks to the Pecos." (I-63, HP1–HP7, I-57)

> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Barboncito will not have degrees: the treaty draws the homeland by landmarks, the Puerco of the West, named springs and Ojo del Oso, and its southern line runs north of Beale's wagon road, with an article that reserves the strip along it for a road of wagons or of iron, with compensation. A mountain article keeps the summits of Mount Taylor and the San Francisco Peaks inside the line. The Hopi villages make their own agreement at Santa Fe in 1865, each for itself, and their mesas enter the register as a homeland inside the Diné country, with Moenkopi apart; the line between them is an article of the Navajo treaty too, signed by both, and no agent, school or mission goes onto the mesas unless a village asks. **[PROPOSED — CR-071]** The villages' leaders and Oraibi's place in it.

### §12, same paragraph, at its end, after "…Mangas dies in his own country in the winter of 1866–67 and is buried by his people." (I-52, I-66)

> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The peace of 1865 gives each people its own country: the White Mountain country to its bands, the Chiricahua mountains and valleys to the Sonora line to Cochise's people, and to the Chihenne and Bedonkohe the country from the Mimbres to the Gila's headwaters and Ojo Caliente. No settler has to be moved off any of it.

### §12, the paragraph beginning "**[PROPOSED — CR-060]** By these agreements and by votes the interior consents" — replace "**[OPEN]** The Ute leaders and terms, and Washakie's agreement." with (I-68)

> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Ouray, who grew up at Taos speaking Spanish, comes to the table with Carson; the Slope is Colorado west of the divide, with North and Middle Park. **[OPEN]** The other Ute leaders and terms, and Washakie's agreement.

### §12, after the paragraph beginning "**[PROPOSED — CR-061]** Until the exchange the armistice line at Glorieta holds" (A-3)

> <!-- CR-071: San Miguel and Mora adhere --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The land east of Glorieta passes by the treaty, and for a year any resident may declare for the Union and keep his property. In October 1867 San Miguel and Mora elect conventions on the old franchise to vote on the compact and the charter. Las Vegas adheres by a wide margin; Mora, fed for five years by Fort Union's forage contracts, adheres narrowly once the Confederation keeps the fort and takes over the contracts. Manuel Chaves speaks against at Las Vegas and stays.

### §13, the paragraph beginning "**[PROPOSED]** Demobilization brings land offers" — after "…Mexico retains Sonora." (I-39, I-54, I-56)

> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Baja is governed from La Paz by a commission on the Northern Commission's model, three commissioners with Spanish as a working language, until 1887. In the 1870s it makes compacts with the Kumeyaay, Paipai, Kiliwa and Cucapá for homelands in their sierras and the delta. **[OPEN]** The commissioners' names.

### §13, after the paragraph ending "…Prices, garrisons and firms await audit." (C1, C2, C5, I-53, I-65, B2)

> <!-- CR-071: the grant machinery, the settlement programme and the first quarrel --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** From 1865 the new works go round the members. A county board petitions the Superintendency for a water grant, a railway subsidy or Development Fund credit; it signs away the works' accounts to confederal audit, their route to confederal consent, any member tax on them and its own forum to the Confederal Court, and it gets money no member could give it. The accounts are published each year beside the next county's. By 1871 most interior boards spend more confederal money than member money, and their supervisors look to Monterey. Nothing is taken from the members; everything new goes round them.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The interior is filled while the members are emptied. John Wesley Powell heads the land and water office, and his irrigation districts are drawn by watershed; the passage offices bring settlers from Europe and Asia to farms sold by the Land Fund on instalments. The trunk lines are chartered by the confederal chambers on Land Fund grants: the Raton line reaches Las Vegas in 1879, the thirty-fifth-parallel line crosses to the Colorado in 1880–83, and the Salt Lake–Snake–Columbia line is built in 1881–84. The settlers owe their farm to the Land Fund, their water to the district and their passage to a confederal office, not to the member's legislature. **[PROPOSED — CR-071]** The programme's scale; Hans Mattson at the passage offices.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The first quarrel is Oregon's. The Chinese floor of its charter has brought Chinese Pacificans into the Rogue valley, and in 1869 the Breckinridge men of Jackson and Josephine carry a Land Act through the legislature: no Land Fund sale to anyone the old constitution would have barred, and a member tax on assisted settlers' instalments. In 1870 the Confederal Court strikes it down: the Land Fund's title is confederal, the floor protects a citizen of another member, and no member tax lies on a confederal grant. Oregon keeps its power over residence until 1887. Fort Hall's council sets the other precedent: it refuses to sell the railway its right of way down the Portneuf and leases it for a rent the railway pays, as every later crossing of a homeland does. **[OPEN]** The Rogue valley farmer's name.

### §14, after the paragraph beginning "The 1870–1871 Japanese alliance is equal" (Part D, D2, L10, decision 6)

> <!-- CR-071: the abolition of the domains, watched --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Kido and Itō have read the Articles of 1856 through Hikozō at Nagasaki and concluded that Japan cannot afford a confederation whose members keep their own militia and a veto. Part of the alliance's credit lets Tokyo promise to take the domains' debts and paper money, and Elias, who has the accounts, watches the abolition of the domains in August 1871 as an underwriter, not a spectator: the registers returned first, the guard assembled before the announcement, the lords brought to the capital and given rank for office, the new prefectures named for rivers and mountains. **[PROPOSED — CR-071]** Part of Japan's loan of 1873 for the samurai stipends is placed in San Francisco, with the Bank of California as agent.

### §14, after the paragraph beginning "<!-- CR-070: the Northern Commission" … "**[APPROVED — CR-070]** Alaska is a territory of the Confederation" (I-68, I-69)

> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** In 1873 Ouray negotiates the San Juan lease, which gives the miners their districts and roads and keeps the valleys Ute; the leased districts of the San Juan and Gunnison join the Mountain Districts' commission, which moves to Leadville in 1879. In 1885 William Healey Dall, the naturalist of the telegraph line, succeeds Davidson at the head of the Northern Commission at forty.

### §19, after the paragraph beginning "**[APPROVED — CR-022]** The examinations reach the officer corps in mid-career" (Parts D and E; E-1 to E-17; B4)

> <!-- CR-071: the memorandum, the assumption, the Collar, the Militia Act and the survey --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** In the winter of 1878–79, back from Vladivostok, Soberanes writes Elias a memorandum on what Japan has shown: money first, then the men, then the armed men, then the map, then the vote. Victoria reads it. The lean years of 1875–77 have put the members' railway and road bonds into arrears while the Reserve Bank carried the coast's largest private bank through August 1875, and the Confederation now offers to take the members' debts.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The Assumption Act of 1879 goes to each member legislature with *An Act for the Common Execution of the Laws*, to be taken together or refused together. A member that takes them has its debt assumed and its paper guaranteed, its treasury audited by the Polytechnic and its treasurer and auditor examined and posted from outside; its governor, still elected, accepts a confederal commission as Commissioner of the Confederation and answers to the Providentor for the common law. Each receives his commission from Elias in person at Monterey. The Committee party's papers call it the Collar, and the *Deseret News* takes up the name. Oregon, Columbia, Idaho, western Montana, Washoe and New Mexico take it in 1879–80; California, which does not need rescuing, and Deseret, whose Church will not open its books to Carquinez's auditors, refuse. Through the 1870s and 1880s the members adopt California's codes one after another on Party majorities.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The Militia Act of 1882 makes every member militia a reserve of the common army, its officers commissioned by the Confederation and its arms in confederal arsenals. Sherman chairs the board that examines every commission, the regulars' and the Auxiliary's with the militias', and keeps the commissions out of the Party's hands. At his request, with Elias's consent, the officers who fail are kept together as a command of their own; from 1882 to 1885 the Auxiliary's veteran battalions pass to the interior garrisons and come under it, and Silas commands on the Sierra line.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** In 1880 the Patrimony's Development Fund commissions a survey of water districts, stations and land values, headed by Theodore Judah. Its second use is known to Elias, Soberanes and Judah; Ralston, who signs the appointment, works it out and says nothing. In 1884 the census of 1880 is printed again by the survey's districts, with a provisional map of about 130 districts in 1884, and every confederal table from then on is printed by district. The second map, the 124 in 1886, is argued over seat by seat inside the Party's county committees; where two towns want the seat, the railway station decides and the loser receives the court, the school or the hall.

### §21, the first paragraph — after "…establish her record before she becomes regent." (F1–F3, K-5, K-6, F-2)

> <!-- CR-071: the triggers --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Two events of 1885–86 make the founder's failing life urgent. In 1885 California's legislature resolves against a war with Britain over Port Hamilton; the resolution has no force and is printed in London. In the water decision of October 1886 the Confederal Court holds, on appeal from Henry Miller's riparian suit against a confederal district on the Kern, that a confederal grant does not extinguish a private water right held under a member's law. Every district in California now holds its water until the next suit. The Party runs on the decision in every irrigation county and wins California's legislature narrowly in November, and Romualdo Pacheco, home from Manila, is chosen President of California.

### §21, the second paragraph — replace "A regency article names Victoria, Queen of Hawaii, Regent of the Realm until an Act of Settlement establishes the Crown, and Julian Heir of the Realm. Elias's life office ends with him." with (K-19; R1 item 2)

> A regency article names Victoria, Queen of Hawaii, Regent of the Realm until an Act of Settlement establishes the Crown, and Julian Heir of the Realm. **[APPROVED — CR-071]** In December 1886 Elias's draft makes Victoria Queen of the Realm. She strikes the article and has Soberanes draft the regency in its place: a crown for her would pass through her to Julian and decide between the founder's sons while he is dying; the members' men took a life Providentor, not a king; the Realm still carries the ordinances that stretched her lease; and power held for a crown not yet settled is the older Hawaiian form. Elias's life office has no successor; he lays it down when the regency begins.

### §21, the third paragraph — after "…Hawaiian loyalists reject it." (G6, H-1, K-9, E-15, G-3, G-4, K-15)

> <!-- CR-071: the ratifications --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Pacheco settles California's terms with the Regent-designate at Thalassia in the winter of 1886–87, on the island where he went to school. He takes home the historic-land clause, Spanish entrenched, community property kept and the Mexican grants as organic law, and signs away the power through which California could have excluded the Chinese, knowing it. The bill goes to the confederal chambers in January 1887 and passes by two-thirds in April. In May Reginaldo del Valle, born in the Republic, carries it through California's senate, where a riparian amendment fails by one vote and the ratification passes by three; Hawaii ratifies through the Territorial Council with the Queen's assent. Ralston brings the San Francisco houses to give their word for it; the Patrimony spends nothing. The interior legislatures ratify on their grants in May and June. Every charter of 1866 carries Elias's article of supersession, so the Constitution enters into force on 1 August 1887 whatever Salt Lake does. John Taylor will not accept it and dies on 25 July; George Q. Cannon brings the Church in, and Deseret's acceptance is its legislature's last act. Cannon's telegram reaches Thalassia two days before the handover, and Elias reads it himself.

### §21, the fifth paragraph — replace "County boards choose one senator each." as R1 item 3, and after "…they answer to the ruler, not the Chamber." add (G2, G4, K-11, I-7, I-8, I-70 to I-72, K-22, K-24)

> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The members' executives in office that day, and Pacheco, receive appointed seats held to sixty-eight; the member legislatures are dissolved, and their men may stand for the county boards, elected for two terms by wards that follow the old county lines. California's capitol at Sacramento becomes the inland command's headquarters, Salem's a county hall and Olympia's the courthouse of the Court of the Realm's Columbia circuit. Alaska stays a territory under the Northern Commission, outside the 124, and Baja's two counties replace its commission. The homelands are not counties: a homeland of a million acres or more stands outside the 124, and a smaller one lies inside a county's lines beyond its board's reach; their compacts run to the Realm and change only with the nation's consent. A member of a nation becomes a citizen by entering himself on the register, and votes where he lives; those who live on leased ground in no county are on the roll of the county beside it.

### §21, the last paragraph — R1 item 4, and before "In August 1887 Elias dies." insert (G1.3, R-1, G4, B-1, B-2)

> <!-- CR-071: the handover --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** On 1 August 1887 the regency begins at Thalassia. The Providentorship ends with the regency on 1 August 1887, and the states end with it. Elias, too weak to stand long, watches Victoria take the oath; her first act as Regent is to sign the commissions of the 124 Commissioners from the list he approved, and Silas, Kiyoshi and Soberanes take their commissions from her hand and sit for the first time in the Regency Council. He keeps the style of Providentor and no seat. The founder's lesson of 1860 and of the vote of twelve to six was that a constitution of members could stop him; the lesson of 1887 is that it could stop anyone after him, and the examined men he had trained since 1879 had nowhere to serve while the members held the offices. **[PROPOSED — CR-071]** The Chronicler gives the day and the room, not Elias's words.

### §23, after the paragraph beginning "She dies in the autumn of 1888" (H-11, H-13, H-14)

> <!-- CR-071: the Honolulu rising of 1889 --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** On 30 July 1889, the eve of Lā Hoʻihoʻi Ea, Robert Wilcox, home from the military academy at Turin, occupies the grounds of Aliʻiōlani Hale with a hundred-odd men and demands that the Territorial Council be dissolved and the kingdom's legislature restored. They rise in the Queen's name against her Council. Kiyoshi's naval party offers marines and the Commissioner for Honolulu the reserve; the Regent refuses both and sends the Queen's Guard over the Pali under Samuel Nowlein, with Kalākaua answerable for its loyalty, and her terms ahead of it. Wilcox refuses them. The Guard takes the grounds that afternoon and seven of the rising are killed. She rides into Honolulu that night. The kingdom's Supreme Court tries Wilcox for treason and a Hawaiian jury acquits him in 1890; the Regent accepts the verdict and says that the Act that establishes the Crown will end the Council. The loyalists sing of the seven; the Realm's press notes that no marine landed. Wilcox dies in 1903, before the union is put to the Hawaiian people.

### §24, after the first paragraph ending "…rather than as guardian obliged to surrender office on the boy's majority." (G-8, R-2, K-12)

> <!-- CR-071: the interim --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** Until the first Senate and Chamber sit in 1888, the Confederal Council and Assembly sit on as the Realm's chambers under the Regent, and the old county boards carry on in their wards. The Council consents to the Chancellor the Regent appoints, and the Senate confirms him at its first sitting. The interim chambers' first act, early in 1888, is the Franchise Law; the first county boards are elected on it in the spring and choose the first Senate. **[OPEN]** The first Chancellor.

### §25, after the paragraph beginning "Victoria and Soberanes consolidate through examinations, finance and published comparisons." (J1, J3)

> <!-- CR-071: the first Commissioners --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The first Commissioners arrive in 1888 from the examined men of 1879–87, each outside his home county. On the Rio Grande a Californio Commissioner hears complaints in the plaza as the alcaldes did and publishes the accounts in both languages, and in his third year the county's school passes are the best in the old New Mexico. At Hilo a Commissioner who has passed the Hawaiian examination speaks English to the Queen's people and takes their petitions through an interpreter; Victoria recalls him early in 1889. What ordinary people notice is less: the sheriff stays, the school examiner is a stranger, the courthouse changes its seal, and the tax bill loses the member's line. In Oregon the exclusion law has lapsed with the member, and the first Black family to buy a Willamette farm in 1888 does so without anyone saying why it can. **[OPEN]** The Commissioners' names.

### §25, after the paragraph beginning "County autonomy is earned." (J2, K-25, I-70, I-71)

> <!-- CR-071: the first receivership, the first charter and the nations' ladder --> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The first receivership is of an owners' board. In July 1892 the Regent declares a disorder in Coeur d'Alene, where the mine owners' lockout has met the miners' union with dynamite and rifles; the board is suspended, a commission enforces the labour code against both sides, tries the men who blew up the Frisco mill and the guards who fired into the crowd, and hands the county back in 1895. Salt Lake is the first county to qualify for a charter, in 1893: the people most suspicious of the centre top its tables. The charter waits a year on the inspectors' test that no congregation may capture a county, and the Church's men on the board resign their Church offices to keep it. Salt Lake is chartered in 1894.
>
> **[APPROVED — CR-071]** The nations have a ladder of their own. A nation may buy back its ceded land still in the Land Fund at the price the Fund paid for it, and its purchases join the homeland by a supplementary compact, the county being paid its lost land tax for ten years; a homeland that grows past a million acres leaves its county. A nation whose accounts and services meet the Office's standard for five years becomes chartered: it is its own trustee and keeps the lease fee the Realm has always taken, holds its royalties in a patrimony of its own, may charter companies and borrow at home with the Treasury's consent, keeps a court of first instance and its own police, and may have its language certified, so that its citizens who pass in it cast a tongue vote. Smaller homelands that want a voice in the Senate join leagues with partners of their own choosing. The leak of land through long leases still runs, and the Office prints both columns for every nation. **[PROPOSED — CR-071]** The year of the ladder's law; which nations climb first.
