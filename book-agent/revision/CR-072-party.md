The model the author named is the party described in the second half of Curtis Yarvin's essay "The Midterm Postmortem" (*Gray Mirror*): a real organisation rather than a label; members disciplined like soldiers; candidates chosen and funded centrally; an intelligence organ and a party line carried by the press; turnout as the source of power; a legislature that passes the executive's bills; a cost to be paid for, not a business selling favours. This CR adapts it to a nineteenth-century Pacific state; it does not quote it.

## What this CR does

1. **It names the instrument that canon already implies.** From 1865 Elias "deals directly with county boards over the states' heads" through grants (CR-021 B5). The Prosperity Party is the organised form of those dealings: the boards that take the grants also take the ticket.
2. **It gives Pacifica the cohesion Japan's oligarchs had by birth** (CR-071 D9). Satsuma and Chōshū could act by rescript. Elias must carry two chambers and several member legislatures, and the Party is how he does it.
3. **It divides the work of the state four ways.** The Crown decides; the estates hold the posts and the numbers; the Party holds the boards, the ballots and the Senate; the counties deliver. No part is in conflict with the others by design (P3). The friction comes from the factions the Party does not serve.
4. **It delivers 1887.** The two-thirds of both chambers, the founding states' consent and the purchase of the interior legislatures (CR-021 D) are the Party's work.
5. **It absorbs its rivals.** Deseret's Church machine is cancelled as a power of its own, kept as the Party's Deseret caucus, and raised into the Party's model for the arid counties (C7); Deseret's marriage law becomes a guarantee of the Realm. San Francisco's Committee party is neutralised (C9). By about 1888 the Party has friends and tame opponents, not rivals (P20). The Hawaiian loyalists follow in 1907 (P23).
6. **It outlives Elias.** Under Victoria it carries the Office's league tables into every county board, which is why she seldom needs receivership (CR-021 B5). Under Julian it hardens into the county oligarchies that fight him through the Senate: the quarrels of a dominant party are fought inside it.

## B. The division of the state

| Part | Holds | Chosen by | Canon |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Crown** (Elias; from 1887 the Regent) | Decision; appointments; ordinances | — | CR-021 A |
| **The estates** (the Polytechnic; from 1888 the Office of Public Measures) | The posts: Commissioner, treasurer, auditor, examiner, chief of police; the examinations; the numbers | Examination | CR-021 B3, CR-037 E10 |
| **The Prosperity Party** | The county boards; the tickets; the Senate bloc; the wire and the press; turnout | Its own cadre | **This CR** |
| **The counties** | Delivery: schools, roads, sanitation, sheriffs, the land tax | Their boards and the Commissioner | CR-021 B2–B3 |

**How the estates use the Party (P3).** The posts are examined and the Party cannot pass a candidate (CR-037). But the boards are elected, the boards pass the budgets and choose the senators, and an examined Commissioner with a hostile board can only suspend its acts for thirty days. The estates need majorities on the boards. The Party supplies them:
- **The programme is the Office's table.** Where a county misses a standard (infant mortality, examination passes, revenue per head, order), the Party's slate runs on fixing it, and the Commissioner's budget arrives with a board ready to pass it. *Things work* is a claim the tables can check.
- **The candidates are the estates' graduates.** Teachers, engineers, assayers, surgeons and surveyors trained by the Polytechnic and the colleges stand for the boards. The cadre is the examined (P13).
- **The reward is a charter.** A county that reaches the standard for five years becomes chartered and governs itself within bands (CR-021 B3). The Party's county men want the charter; the estates set the test. Ambition runs the same way for both.
- **The Senate.** The boards choose the senators, so a Party majority among the boards is a Party bloc in the Senate, from 1887 the body where the counties are heard.

## C. History

### C0. The organisers — P10, P11, P12, P24 APPROVED

Elias pairs a machine man, who knows men, with an engineer, who knows lines.

**David C. Broderick (b. 1820), the machine.**
- *Hist.* Born in Washington to Irish parents and raised in New York, a Tammany fireman, he came to San Francisco in the gold rush and built the Democratic machine there. Its offices carried no salary but fees, which could run to $50,000 a year; Broderick gave the office to the aspirant who would keep half and hand him the other half for the organisation. He led the free-soil Democrats against Gwin's southern wing. The Committee of Vigilance of 1856 was aimed at his machine, hanged Casey, one of his men, and broke its hold on the city. He was elected to the United States Senate in 1856 and killed in a duel by David Terry, the former chief justice, in September 1859.
- *In this timeline.* In 1856 Elias breaks the second Committee of Vigilance (canon §6). The machine the vigilantes meant to destroy survives because of Elias, and Broderick owes him that. There is no Senate seat in Washington to fight over and no Lecompton quarrel, so the feud with Terry never comes to the field (P24; Terry's later life OPEN). Broderick is 46 in 1866.
- *What he brings:* wards and captains, the printed ticket, turnout, and the discipline to make a bought vote stay bought.
- *What he has to unlearn:* the sale of offices. His system ran on fees; Elias's offices are examined. Broderick builds a machine paid for from above, a cost and not a business (E), and he never quite believes it can work.
- *Stepping aside (P11):* about 1880 he gives up the direction of the Party, which lets the examined cadre take it over (C3). He is the last of the old machine; the Carquinez men are the new one. He stays in San Francisco as the Party's elder. In 1888 he reads its first published accounts (P6): a machine paid for from above, the thing he never quite believed could work, set out county by county for anyone to read. He dies that winter, in 1888–89, at 68. The Carquinez men run his funeral like an operation, the last the old machine holds, and Ralston brings the city's banks (P26).

**Theodore Judah (b. 1826), the lines.**
- *Hist.* Rensselaer-trained, he came west in 1854 to build the Sacramento Valley Railroad. The Pacific Railroad Convention of 1859 at San Francisco sent him to Washington to lobby, and he worked the committees there. In 1860–61, with Daniel Strong, the druggist of Dutch Flat, he found the line over Donner Pass and raised the first money for it town by town along the route, signing up Huntington, Crocker, Stanford, Hopkins and Bailey in Sacramento after San Francisco turned him down. He fell out with them over their Dutch Flat wagon road, sailed for New York in October 1863 to raise money to buy them out, caught yellow fever crossing Panama and died in New York on 2 November 1863.
- *In this timeline.* Judah is in canon's engineering community, "included in principle without separately settled undertakings" (§5). Pacifica is at war with the Union in 1863; there is no going to New York for capital, so there is no Panama crossing and he lives (P24). His quarrel with Huntington and Crocker, both in canon, goes to Elias instead, and the Sacramento line of 1865–67 is built on confederal money. Judah dies of yellow fever at Colón in 1890, at 64, heading the first survey of the Panama works after the French enterprise is bought in 1889. The crossing that killed him historically in 1863 kills him a generation later, with the counties drawn (P26).
- *What he brings:* the convention and the lobby; a map of every town, board and subscriber along a line; and the habit of treating the county as the unit of a network. The Party's wire runs along his telegraph and rail lines, and its county committees follow his stations.
- *His later part (P12):* he heads the boundary survey that draws the 124 counties (CR-071 E4), by watershed, railway and station. The engineer who organised the counties along one line draws them for the whole Realm.
- *Why he matters to P3:* he is an engineer among machine men, and the first bridge between the Party and the estates. Soberanes, when he takes the Party up after 1878, inherits Judah's half of it.

### C1. Founding (1866) — P1, P4, P8 APPROVED

- **When.** 1866, the first elections after the Enabling Law of 1865. The war's emergency government is over; Elias, now Providentor for life, needs a permanent majority in the Confederal Assembly and in the member legislatures, where the residual powers still lie.
- **The name.** The Prosperity Party: the war is over and the grants are flowing, and the name says what the founder offers. In Hawaii, and among its Japanese and Chinese members, it is the Hui (P7).
- **The core.** Broderick's machine; Judah's towns along the Sacramento line; the county boards that have taken confederal grants for water, railways and land since 1865; and the men the war made: quartermasters, telegraphers, Californio officers, the Mormon road men of the Compact.
- **The instrument is personal.** The Party answers to Elias, not to a convention. It has no platform of its own beyond his.
- **Its rival in San Francisco (P19).** The vigilantes' party, which historically ruled the city for a decade after 1856 under the name People's Party (Hist.), survives in this timeline as the party of San Francisco money, weakened but not destroyed. In the text it is **the Committee party**, so that "People's Party" can mean only Deseret's (C7). It is the machine Broderick knows best. Its end is in C9.

### C2. Growth (1866–1879) — P14 APPROVED

- **The wire.** A Party wire service over the confederal telegraph, and a chain of member newspapers in English, Spanish and Hawaiian, most of them owned by Party men.
- **The member elections.** The Party contests every member legislature and wins most; it does not win them all. Washoe (1860) and California's 5:4 delegation in 1864 (CR-068) are the memory of what one lost legislature costs. Deseret's is the one it never wins (C7).
- **Step one (CR-071 L1, APPROVED).** The confederal commission for member governors in the late 1870s passes through Party majorities in the member legislatures.
- **The Meiji winter (CR-071 D4, 1872).** Ōkubo and Kido, who govern by rescript, see a man who governs by ticket. Kido asks how it is done (P14).

### C3. The estates take it up (1879–1887) — P3, P13 APPROVED

- **Soberanes returns from Vladivostok (1878)** as the officer who brought the squadron home (§16) and comes to lead the Polytechnic. He sees what the Party is and what it is not yet: the founder's machine for winning votes, with no idea of what to do with the counties it wins.
- **His memorandum (CR-071 decision 9)** proposes the use: the Party should carry the estates' standards into the boards. From the early 1880s, with Broderick stepped aside, the Party's county slates are drawn from the examined.
- **The cadre (P13).** The Polytechnic's graduates become the Party's cadre in the 1880s. The name comes later: canon gives it to the barracks crowd of Vallejo College in the 1890s (CR-038), the rising officers who take a year at Monterey from 1889. Vallejo College is where the cadre is formed and where it gets its name. CR-037 E11's clique of the examined is this cadre.
- **Why Soberanes champions the counties.** The member states are the one arena the Party can lose, and the states' patronage posts are outside the examination. Abolishing them gives the posts to the estates and the boards to the Party. His Californio reason stands beside it: in California's own legislature the Californios are outvoted, and as a historic land California keeps Spanish, the Court at Monterey and no San Francisco machine over Monterey.
- **The campaign for 1887.** The Party carries the two-thirds in both chambers, the founding states' votes and the interior legislatures. The land and railway grants that buy those legislatures (canon) are distributed through it, and it makes the bought votes stay bought until ratification.

### C4. The Truce of Thalassia (spring 1887)

- The Party is not a fourth faction at the table. It belongs to the Crown and the estates, and at the Truce that means **Victoria and Soberanes**.
- **Silas's inland command** stands outside it. The old army's fighting officers failed the examinations (CR-022); a party whose cadre is the examined is their enemy's party. The veterans at Donner Pass in October 1887 are, among other things, the men the Party has no place for.
- **Kiyoshi's naval party** is a faction, not a party in this sense (CT4).
- **Deseret** comes in the same year (C7).

### C5. The regency's pillar (1887–1907) — P2, P5, P6 APPROVED

- **The Franchise Law of 1888** gives a second vote for the civil examination and a third for a degree, commission or diploma, plus tongue votes (canon §25). The plural franchise weights the electorate toward the Party's own cadre.
- **The ballot (P5).** The Party goes on printing its own ticket under the regency. The secret, state-printed ballot comes only under Julian, in 1911 (P25, C6).
- **The accounts (P6).** From 1888 the Party publishes its accounts with the Office's other numbers: who paid, what was spent, in which county.
- **League tables and the boards.** Victoria governs by comparison (CR-021 B5). The Office publishes, the Five's press makes the tables famous, and the Party's slates run on them in every board election. That is why shame and ambition work faster than receivership.
- **The press seat.** Kalākaua's project of the image passes to the press seat of the Five in 1891 (canon §19). PROPOSED: the Party's wire is one of the things it inherits.
- **Victoria's honesty.** The essay's party line is presented as always true. Pacifica's is built on numbers that are true, weak spots included (canon §25; the rump-state decision of 31 October 2026). The Party chooses which true numbers to make loud.
- **The House** (C8) counts what the Party runs on.

### C6. Under Julian (1907–1932): the outline only

- Julian governs by command, receivership and ordinance (CR-021 B5). The boards are suspended in many counties in the war years, and the Party's county men lose what they built.
- **The county oligarchies** that fight Julian through the Senate in 1910–1911 and in 1927 are the Party's county organisations grown old, rich and hereditary. The examined cadre has become a clique after all, which is what CR-037 E11 foresaw. With no opposition outside the Party (P20), the fight is inside it.
- **The ballot of 1911 (P25).** Julian answers the oligarchies in the Senate fight of 1910–1911 by taking the ticket from them. From 1911 the state prints the ballot and the House counts it, and a candidate goes on it by a public test: signatures, or a board's standing in the league tables. The county men lose the means to make a bought vote stay bought; the examined lose almost nothing. The Union's reformers wanted the secret ballot against the parties. Here the Crown uses it against a clique inside its own party, and the Party remains the only one that can win (P20; CT6 stands). The oligarchies must find other means, which is part of why they come back in 1927.
- Details are for a later CR.

### C7. Deseret: the Aufhebung — P16, P17, P18, P21 APPROVED

The three senses of *aufheben*: Deseret's machine is **cancelled** as a power of its own, **preserved** inside the Party, and **raised** into something the whole Realm uses. Its marriage law goes the same way: cancelled as the law of a member state, preserved for every Mormon wherever he lives, and raised into a guarantee for every church.

**Before: two machines (1866–1886).**
- In Deseret the Prosperity Party is at first the party of the gentiles: the miners of Bingham and the Wasatch camps, the railway men at Ogden, the Gentile merchants of Salt Lake. Historically these men formed Utah's Liberal Party in 1870.
- The Church answers as it did historically, with its own **People's Party**, built on the wards and stakes, run by the bishops, voting as one. It holds Deseret's legislature throughout. This is the one member legislature the Prosperity Party never wins (C2), and the best-organised machine in the West: every household visited, every vote counted, every ward a precinct.
- Elias does not try to break it. He has watched what breaking a church costs (Mountain Meadows, the trial of Lee in 1863, canon §10), and he wants what it can do.

**The occasion (1885–1887).**
- Brigham Young dies in 1877 (Hist.). John Taylor leads the Church from 1880 (Hist.); with no Union raid in this timeline, he leads it in the open, from Salt Lake, not in hiding.
- The bill of 1887 splits Deseret across several counties (canon). The Church fears more than the loss of its state: Deseret's accession protected member-state marriage and church-property law (canon §10), and once there is no member state, a single civil code of the Realm (CR-021 B2) would leave that protection with nothing to stand on.
- Deseret is therefore the hardest opponent in CR-071 Part H, and the one the Party has to win rather than beat.

**Why the Realm keeps the protection.**
- **It is canon's own principle.** Religious freedom is promised to all, and the toleration of the Mormons is that principle applied, not a special case (canon decision). Deseret's accession "does not impose one marriage system on every state" (§10). A Realm that took the protection away the moment the state was gone would show the Mormons that the toleration was only the price of their militia.
- **It is what makes the Aufhebung real.** A machine that is told it is preserved while its law is abolished has been beaten, not raised. The Church comes into the Party because the one thing it cannot trade is guaranteed better under the Realm than it was under the member state.
- **It is the founder's kind of bargain.** The Union fights the Mormons over marriage for thirty years (Hist.: the Morrill Act of 1862, Edmunds 1882, Edmunds–Tucker 1887). Pacifica takes the Church's loyalty at no cost to itself and lets Washington pay the price of the quarrel.

**The guarantee (P16).** The Constitution of 1887 carries the protection over as a **personal** guarantee rather than a territorial one:
- A marriage made in the form of a religious community registered with the Realm is valid in every county, whatever the form, and church property is held as corporate property under the Realm's law.
- It is not a Mormon privilege. It covers every registered church and congregation: Catholic, Protestant, Jewish, Buddhist, Shinto, and the Hawaiian forms the Society protects. Deseret's law becomes the first case of a Realm-wide rule.
- **The Realm's floors apply to every marriage, of every form,** as confederal law already does for the member states (§10: no state may ban a marriage on grounds of race or origin):
  - no bar on grounds of race or origin;
  - the free consent of each party, given before a registrar;
  - the ages of marriage and the age-gap rule (P17, P21, below);
  - registration with the Office, so every marriage is counted;
  - the right of any spouse to leave, with a share of property and support for children, in the Realm's courts.
- Canon's marriage-floor case of the 1870s (§15, a Mormon–Shoshone or Mormon–Chinese union before the confederal court) is where these floors are first tested. The guarantee of 1887 writes the court's answer into the Constitution.

**The ages of marriage (P17, P21).** For the record only; the mechanics stay off the page.
- **With parental consent:** 14 for a man, 12 for a woman. These are the ages of the Spanish and Mexican law Pacifica inherited, the same as the common law and as Utah's (Hist.).
- **Without parental consent:** 21 for a man, 18 for a woman.
- **Pacifica's addition, the age-gap rule:** a spouse may not be older than twice the other spouse's age, less seven years.
  - Worked example (author): a bride of 12 may marry a groom of at most 17, and needs her parents' consent until she is 18.
  - Read the other way, the younger spouse must be at least half the elder spouse's age plus seven. A man of 30 may marry no one under 22; a man of 50, no one under 32.
- **Scope (P21):** the rule binds every marriage of every form, like the other floors. It is not aimed at one church, though it binds hardest on older men taking young plural wives.

**The bargain.**

| Cancelled | Preserved | Raised |
|---|---|---|
| The member state of Deseret; its legislature; the People's Party as a separate party | The Church's machine, as the **Deseret caucus** of the Prosperity Party: its wards are the Party's precincts, its slates are the Party's slates in the Deseret counties, its senators sit as a bloc | The ward, the irrigation company and the cooperative store become the Party's model for every arid county; Deseret's university, which measures how much water an acre needs (canon §15), trains the interior's cadre |
| Deseret's marriage law as the law of a member state | The same law, as a personal guarantee: valid for every Mormon in every county; church property held as corporate property | Freedom of religious marriage, under the Realm's floors, for every registered church |
| The Church's hold on posts | The boards: the CR-037 limit on any one congregation applies to senior **posts**, which the Office fills from outside; elected board seats are the Party's to fill | Mormon examined graduates enter the cadre like anyone else, and the Church's men stand for boards across the interior wherever the Party needs irrigation men |

- **What the Church gives up.** Its state, its legislature, its own party and its hold on Deseret's posts. Nothing of its doctrine.
- **Who carries it (P18).** George Q. Cannon, Taylor's counsellor and historically the Church's most practised politician, negotiates with Soberanes and Victoria. Taylor resists: he does not trust a guarantee from a Realm that is abolishing his state. Taylor dies in July 1887, as historically, and his death is what lets Cannon bring the Church in. This is where the Aufhebung comes closest to failing. The act itself does not need Deseret's consent (CR-021 B4; CR-071 G-3); it comes closest to failing in California's senate in May 1887, where the vote is legally required and narrow (CR-071 G1.3, H1, K-9).
- **What the canon line then means.** §22's "the Church steering a coherent Deseret Senate bloc" stands, and now says how: the bloc is the Party's Deseret caucus, and the Church steers it from inside the Party (CT5).
- **The costs.**
  - *Abroad:* the Union's revanchist press has its easiest charge against Pacifica: a Realm that licenses polygamy. Pacifica does not answer it.
  - *At home:* Protestant and Catholic clergy in California object, and so do some women of the House (C8). The House's answer is the register: from 1888 the Office counts every plural household, its children's schooling and health, and every departure under the right to leave. The figures are published like every other.
  - *Later:* a preserved machine is still a machine. In Julian's reign the Deseret caucus is the most disciplined of the county oligarchies (C6).

### C8. The Kaʻahumanu Society and the House — P15 APPROVED

**What canon already has.** The split exists, and it is CR-038's: at Vallejo College from 1889 "the engineers and managers take over Monterey's old Presidio barracks with their rowing and drinking clubs, and many of the women of the hospitals, schools and Office gather in the house they name after Victoria's Kaʻahumanu Society. A woman surveyor rows with the barracks crew; Polytechnic men sit at the House's lectures on sanitation." The barracks crowd becomes the Carquinez men. Women have sat the Republic's examinations since the 1850s and the colleges have admitted those who pass since 1862 (CR-035). *(Corrected at integration: canon does not say that women cannot vote; the 1888 Franchise Law gives "each adult citizen" a vote. The storyline rests the House's power on the numbers it counts, not on its members' lack of a vote.)*

**Two halves of one machine, not two factions.**
- **The Carquinez men are the Party's.** They are elected and they elect: boards, tickets, the Senate.
- **The women of the House are the estates'.** They cannot vote or stand, but they can be examined and posted. They are the Office's inspectors and statisticians, the county examiners, the surgeons and nurses of the county halls, the schoolmistresses whose pass rates are one of the four standards. They count the dead, the sick and the pupils.
- **So the Party runs on the House's numbers.** Every slate that promises to fix a county's infant mortality is promising to move a figure that a woman of the House measured, and that another woman of the House will come back unannounced to check (CR-038). *Things work* is the Party's line; whether things work is the House's finding.
- **The Society itself is older than both.** Victoria's Kaʻahumanu Society began counting its own sick and dead in 1866 (CR-035), the year the Party was founded. It is the root of the Office (through Noe's registers) and the House is its daughter. It is not in the Party and never joins it.
- **The rivalry.** Real, and useful to the state: the men want good tables, and the women will not give them good tables they have not earned. An inspector who finds a Party board's returns padded is doing exactly what the Regent wants. The rivalry is a check that Victoria built in: the half of the machine that can vote is audited by the half that cannot. Under P20 it is also the only real check: with no opposition party, the House is what tells the Regent the truth about the Party.
- **Victoria sits above both.** The Party is the founder's instrument she inherits (P2); the Society is her own. Through the one she carries the counties, through the other she knows whether they are telling the truth.
- **The thread for later.** The Altai falsification of 1921 (canon) is a crime against the Office's charter. Whether it is done over the House's objection, or with a House woman's complicity, is for the CR that writes it.

### C9. One party and its friends (by about 1888) — P20, P22, P23 APPROVED

**The tendency.** The Party never fights a rival to the death if it can take it in. Each competitor is offered a place inside or left a place outside where it can do no harm. By about 1888 Pacifica has no second party in the Union's sense: no organisation that could win a majority of the boards or the Senate and form a government.

**How each rival ends (P22):**

| Rival | Means | By |
|---|---|---|
| **Deseret's People's Party** | Absorbed as the Deseret caucus (C7) | 1887 |
| **San Francisco's Committee party** (P19) | Its money is bought before its men: from the 1870s the San Francisco houses subscribe to the Party (E), and the champions' charters and the Development Fund's credit run through Party boards. San Francisco becomes a Crown city in 1887 (canon). It keeps its board as a city-county (CR-021 B3), and the board chooses a senator, but the board governs no money: the mint, the Reserve Bank, the syndicates and the champions are the Realm's (CR-021 B6). The Party wins the board in 1888 with the houses' men on its ticket. What is left of the Committee party is a reform club of merchants and editors who publish the Office's tables against the Party's men and lose every election they contest. (San Francisco row corrected in draft 8, CR-071 K-7, APPROVED.) | 1887–88 |
| **The member parties of the states** (Oregon's, Columbia's and the interior's own machines) | They lose their arena: with the member legislatures gone in 1887 there is nothing to contest above the county, and the county boards' tickets are printed by the Party (P5) | 1887 |
| **Silas's veterans** | Not a party and never one: a faction of the old army with a command, not a ticket. Donner Pass (October 1887, canon) and the Truce bind them. They vote, and many vote the Party's ticket. | 1887 |
| **Labour and anti-Chinese movements** (historically California's Workingmen's Party of 1877) | The Party takes the wage boards and the county halls (CR-038) and leaves the agitators the street. Pacifica's guest-worker system and its protection of Chinese Pacificans (canon) leave the anti-Chinese movement no ticket to win. | late 1870s–1880s |

**The tame opposition.** Opponents are allowed to stand, print their own tickets and lose. They are useful: they make the elections real enough to count, they publish what the Party would rather not, and the Office's tables give them their only weapon. The Party lets them keep it. A loyal opposition that cannot win and cannot be silenced is part of the machine, as it was under the PAP.

**The exception (P23): the Hawaiian loyalists.** Those who reject the ordinances of 1874 and court Noe in 1887–88 (canon §23) are not a party contesting boards; they contest the legitimacy of the state. They are not tame in 1888. They are absorbed in 1907, when the Act of Settlement restores the Hawaiian crown and its institutions (canon §27): the Aufhebung again, a generation later. The details are for CR-071 Part H and the 1907 sections.

**The Union's friends inside Pacifica**, if any, are a matter for the security of the state, not for elections.

**What follows.** All real politics after 1888 is inside the Party: between the Carquinez men and the county oligarchies, between the caucuses (Deseret's, the Californio south, the Hawaiian counties, the Northwest), and between the Party and the House that audits it. Julian's Senate fights of 1910–1911 and 1927 are fights among Prosperity men (C6).

## E. Money (PROPOSED)

- The Party costs money and sells nothing. It cannot sell offices: they are examined. This is the break with Broderick's system (C0).
- **Sources:** dues; the founder's own fortune while he lives; subscriptions from the champions and the San Francisco houses; in Deseret, from 1887, a share of what the Church's caucus raises.
- **Not from the treasury.** Public money in the Party would make it the state's creature, not the Crown's, and every grant a bribe in law as well as in fact.
- **Accounts:** published from 1888 (P6).
