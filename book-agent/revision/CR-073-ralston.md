## What canon says now

**What "the Republic" means before 1887 (CR-071 S1, K-5, APPROVED).** For 1856–1887, "the Republic" and "the state" in this CR mean the Confederation's common government at Monterey (from 1885 at Thalassia), not California. The Confederation holds the public domain and the minerals under it, the mint, the Reserve Bank and the State Assay Office, and the works it builds or buys under the Enabling Law, Spring Valley among them; its law governs its own grants and the districts it charters. The members' law governs private land and private water rights until 1887 (§6; CR-021 B2, B5).

- **CT-RALSTON** (`character_bible.md` line 251; registry line 2038): "His firm is not automatically a central bank or owner of public gold … A banking failure, rescue, monopoly or repetition of his historical fate is not approved by this role." His dramatic interest in the bible (line 253): "Expand profitable influence while managing a dependence on public confidence". CR-069 CT2 offered "keep the coast's gold at home at a cost he can count, and be trusted for it" as the amended wording but recommended adapting instead, so the bible's wording stands. ## The historical record (Hist., Lavender)

The Bank of California ran out of coin and closed at 2:35 pm on Thursday 26 August 1875 (pp. 375–376). On the 27th the directors found Ralston $9,565,907 in debt, $4,655,973 of it to the bank and nearly $2m to Sharon; the bank's liabilities exceeded its available assets by about $4.5m (pp. 376–377). They made him convey all his property to Sharon in trust and asked for his resignation, carried by Mills. That afternoon he swam out from the Neptune bath-house at North Beach and was brought ashore dead (pp. 377–378). A coroner's jury of his friends found congestion of the brain and drowning, no poison and no sign of suicide; his widow's $68,000 life insurance depended on it (pp. 379–381). The causes, in brief:
1. **The bank as his treasury.** In February 1873 the directors found $3.5m lent to three of his own firms, all in default, and his wife's foster father, John D. Fry, paid in $3.4m (p. 355). In the summer of 1875 he over-issued 13,180 shares of the bank's stock and raised $1,319,277 on them, borrowed $300,000 on Southern Pacific bonds left with the bank in trust, had $2m of the assay company's bullion, part of it belonging to others, struck into coin on his own account, and disguised the bank's falling reserves (pp. 371–374).
2. **Money tied up in things that could not be sold quickly:** the Palace, whose cost was mounting toward $5m (p. 364), New Montgomery Street, Belmont, and manufactures that lost to Eastern goods and to their own costs (pp. 355, 364).
3. **Ophir.** In the autumn of 1874 Ralston followed Sharon into Ophir. By November their buying had lifted it from $20 to $133, and they paid Lucky Baldwin $2.64m for 20,000 shares, and $1.7m more for Hayward's share of the Crown Point. When the market broke from 8 January 1875, Sharon had already sold short; Ralston held too long (pp. 366–368).
4. **Spring Valley.** The city's engineer, T. R. Snowden, recommended the Calaveras watershed as the new source in 1874. Ralston and Charles Felton bought its 2,250 acres for about $100,000, took 49,000 of the company's 80,000 shares on 9% certificates, sold Calaveras to the company in May 1875 for $1,012,000, and offered the whole, worth roughly $10m, to the city for $15m. The *Call* and the *Bulletin* attacked it and the supervisors refused; Ralston counted on the September election to reverse them (pp. 370–371).
5. **The coin.** The depression reached California in 1875; $30m in gold left for the East. The Nevada Bank's paid-in capital of $5m drew more coin from circulation, and on the eve of the run Flood and O'Brien refused the directors' request for help. The Oriental Bank of London did not answer (pp. 371, 374–375).

### A1. Why he need not falsify his books

His frauds were a bridge: the Palace and the mills had to be paid for years before they earned, and there was nowhere lawful to borrow against them. In 1872 and 1874 California's legislature rejected bills for a bank commission (Lavender, p. 375 n.). In Pacifica banking is under Reserve Bank regulation, and the Reserve Bank discounts good commercial paper at a penalty rate. Sound bets can be carried across the gap lawfully, so the over-issued shares and the bonds taken from the vault never need to exist. Some of his ventures still fail on their own costs, as Kimball and the tobacco company did (pp. 349, 364–365); the difference is that a failure is written off rather than hidden. Pacifica's institutions make a reckless builder solvent; they do not change his character (G1).

### A2. The diamond field, 1872 (R13)

**Hist. (Lavender, pp. 306–320).** Philip Arnold and John Slack brought rough stones to San Francisco early in 1872 and deposited a sack of them at the Bank of California. Ralston, William Lent, George Roberts and Asbury Harpending formed a company; the stones were appraised in New York, and in July Arnold bought rough diamonds in London. In June the mining engineer Henry Janin went with the party by the Union Pacific to Rawlins, Wyoming, and four days' ride southwest to mesas bordering the northern arm of what is now Dinosaur National Monument, in northwestern Colorado, where they dug salted stones and staked claims. Clarence King's federal survey of the railway belt heard the rumours, found the site, and after three days in the cold proved it salted. On 11 November 1872 Janin called the directors together; Arnold had taken about $550,000, and Ralston lost about a quarter of a million.

**Here.** The field lies west of the Continental Divide in northwestern Colorado, on the Western Slope, which is Pacifican from the war (CR-060, line 572). Rawlins is a Union town (CR-036 D13). Arnold's ground is the Republic's, and Pacifican mining law requires registered claims and an assay. The exposure comes at the bank, before any expedition: when Arnold and Slack deposit their sack in the spring of 1872 and ask for money, the State Assay Office examines the stones and finds what Tiffany's appraisers missed. There is no expedition and no company. **Story assumptions:** the method of detection (lapidary marks on rough stones bought in London, or the mix of stones from different continents that King's men later read in the ground); and how Arnold would have reached the field, since canon has no Union railway to Rawlins on record. Ralston keeps his quarter of a million, and Harpending and Roberts are spared the grand jury (p. 320).

## B. What could still sink him

- **B1. The rule.** The Reserve Bank lends against good security and does not take mining shares (PROPOSED: CR-057's rule written into the charter of 1857).
- **B2. The caveat to R5: Ophir.** Historically the one ruin that did not come from his building was Ophir: in the autumn of 1874 he followed Sharon in, paid Baldwin $2.64m for 20,000 shares, and held too long when the market broke in January 1875 (Lavender, pp. 366–368). Sharon's motive was the Nevada Senate seat of 1875, which cost him perhaps $800,000 (p. 366). **Here the motive survives (R23):** Washoe is a member state from 1867 with an equal Council delegation and Assembly seats (CR-060 A5), and its legislature, elected in November 1874, fills Washoe's places, so Sharon has a seat to buy. What changes is the other side of the bargain:
  - The Prosperity Party contests every member legislature (CR-072 C2). Sharon runs outside it, with the Committee party's money, against the Party's ticket, as historically he ran against Sutro's Dolly Vardens (p. 366).
  - B1 means he cannot carry his Ophir pool on the Reserve Bank's credit, and Ralston cannot follow him on it.
  - **Ralston's first choice (R25).** In the autumn of 1874 Sharon asks his partner to come in on Ophir, as Ralston historically did "eagerly" (p. 366). Here Ralston declines. It is his first visible choice of the state's terms over his partner, nine months before August 1875, and the reason he is solvent when the coin runs short.
- **B3. The coin squeeze of August 1875** still comes: the depression, the gold drain and the Nevada Bank's $5m of paid-in capital (Lavender, p. 371). It is survivable because the Reserve Bank lends (E1).

Two historical powers are weaker here. The Big Four's grip on California's shipping is held back by auctioned concessions and the state's trunk rights of way. The land-and-water empires of Miller & Lux and Haggin and Tevis are held back by the Land Fund as sole buyer of Native land, the register as title and the Republic's water law over the districts it charters. Miller & Lux's riparian lands, bought from rancho grantees and private holders, stay theirs under California's law, and they remain the largest riparian owner in the San Joaquin; Haggin and Tevis never get the Kern (CR-071 F3). *(CR-071 S3)*

**The consequence for Elias.** The danger is a bloc, not any one rival: the houses, London and the railway men acting together, with Ralston as their natural leader. He is the one man in San Francisco who could organise private money against the state.

## D. Why Ralston agrees

Elias never relies on the other man's gratitude or honesty (character principle). The plan has to work even if Ralston is neither grateful nor honest.

**D1. The gates.** Nearly every large bet he makes passes through something the state holds:
- Spring Valley has one possible buyer.
- The canal needs the Republic's water grant and the Land Fund's buyers. *(CR-071 S4)*
- The Comstock mines pay the tax on their net proceeds (line 455), and the mills work under the Republic's mining law.
- The Hunter's Point dock's best customer is the navy.
- The agency profits are the Republic's loans; the niboo contract is the start of Japan's.
- Railway, port and telegraph concessions are auctioned by the state.

**D2. What only the state can give.** He wants to build, not to rule. The largest projects on the Pacific are the state's: the Japanese and Spanish loans, the Philippines, the canal company bought in 1889. And he wants what he had in 1862: to be trusted with the coast's money and to count what it costs (CR-069; CT-RALSTON's "dependence on public confidence").

**D3. He can be bypassed.** He cannot build the bloc (C), because every other member deals with the state directly:
- London lends to the Treasury itself.
- Mitsui and the Chinese houses deal with Monterey through their own governments.
- The railway men live on concessions.
- The Bonanza men would take his place as the Republic's agent tomorrow.

Ralston is replaceable as a middleman, and he knows it. That is Elias's hold on him.

**D4. The office.** The Development Fund lends only beside private capital that takes the first loss. Its chairman decides which private money works beside the state's: the houses, the railway men, London's issues, the Asian houses. The office costs Ralston his independence and puts him above every rival in the field. That is why he takes it, and why they hate him for it.

**E1. August 1875 (R9).** The depression, the gold drain and the Nevada Bank's capital leave the city short of coin, and the Bonanza men refuse help. The Reserve Bank discounts the Bank of California's good paper at a penalty rate, and the doors stay open. No run happens. The same month the Republic buys the Spring Valley Water Company at a published valuation of its physical property and takes the Calaveras watershed with it.
- **The valuation (corrected in draft 2).** Draft 1 spoke of a commission's survey of March 1875 and a value of about $6m. Lavender has neither: the city's engineer, T. R. Snowden, recommended Calaveras in 1874, and the company with Calaveras was worth roughly $10m (p. 371). The Republic values Calaveras at what Ralston and Felton paid for it, about $100,000, not at the $1,012,000 they charged their own company, so the published figure is about $9m (MODEL).
- **What Ralston gets.** At $15m Ralston and Felton would have cleared their share certificates with about $2.5m each (p. 371). At about $9m they pay for the shares with a modest profit. Ralston survives because his other ventures pay and he did not lose on Ophir, not because Spring Valley rescues him.
- **The state pays a fair price and never a monopoly price.** Ralston comes through the month because the state's institutions exist, and he knows it. The gratitude is real; the plan does not rest on it.
- **The election.** Historically Ralston counted on electing a board that would buy (p. 371), and the Dolly Vardens ran Andrew Hallidie for mayor against his candidate. Hallidie is in canon's engineering community (§8). Here the Republic, not the city's election, decides the purchase. **Approved (R19):** Hallidie, who knows the cable car's power and the water's, sits on the valuation as the engineers' man; his part is a story assumption, not history.

**E2. The houses come in; the agency (R10, R16).**
- **The Japan business (1872–73).** The niboo contract of 30 January 1872 happens as historically. Japan's loan, which Ralston could not raise on the West Coast in 1872 (Lavender, p. 327), is raised in part in San Francisco in 1873 in a richer Pacifica (CR-071 D). The Bank of California leads the San Francisco part: as the Republic's agent where the Republic lends (CR-071 D has Elias co-finance the abolition of the domains), and as Japan's banker for the rest, on the 1867 precedent. **Ledger:** there is no entry for a Japanese loan of 1873 on `main`; it belongs with CR-071.
- **The Spanish loan (1874, CR-029; ledger `C-1874-ES-GOLD-LOAN`, 1874–1876).** The Bank of California is the Republic's agent.
- **The dock (R16).** **Hist.** Ralston's Hunter's Point dry dock, cut from the rock of the Potrero ridge from September 1866 and finished in 1868, 421 feet long, 120 wide and 22 deep, at a projected $1.2m, held all but one or two of the largest ships afloat, and there was then "no place other than Hunter's Point on either side of the Pacific" where Pacific Mail's fleet could be serviced (Lavender, pp. 240–241). **Here.** After the Manila action of August 1873 the damaged squadron is at Honolulu that September "because neutral Japan would sell them coal but not a dock" (storyline line 722, CR-027 as amended by CR-029). After the barracks mutiny it sails home and is docked at Hunter's Point that autumn, at the navy's cost. The admirals' case for Puʻuloa, settled by the convention of March 1874, cites it. **Story assumptions:** Hunter's Point's place in canon (it is not mentioned on `main`), and that the navy's Mare Island facilities (line 277) have no dock large enough for the squadron's ships in 1873.
- **The Party (from 1875).** From that winter the San Francisco houses subscribe to the Prosperity Party, and Ralston brings them to Broderick: the merchants make their peace with the machine their vigilance committee tried to break in 1856. Their money buys the Party's tickets outside San Francisco and its majorities in the confederal chambers. It does not buy California: the Committee party holds California's legislature on the city's and the riparian counties' votes until November 1886 (CR-071 E9.2, K-8). *(CR-071 S5)*

**E3. The Patrimony, 1880 (R6, R7, R12).** Elias makes Ralston the first chairman of the Patrimony under its charter of 1880, over Huntington (G5). On the Doheny pattern (CR-058-R1, CR-037), Ralston keeps his shares in the Bank of California and gives up its direction to William Alvord (historically mayor in 1872 and a director of the bank, Lavender p. 325; president of the bank from 1878 to his death in 1904). The guards:
- the published accounts, every loss written off in public (CR-057; CR-049: a falsified account is theft from the commonwealth);
- recusal from every syndicate his own bank leads (PROPOSED: a new rule in the spirit of CR-037; canon has no recusal rule);
- the Fund's separation from the Reserve Bank and the Treasury (CR-057 B, guard 6: three boards; the Bank never lends to the Fund);
- the Fund takes no part in elections or ratifications: it spends nothing on them, and its accounts show it. In 1886–87 Ralston argues the Constitution to the San Francisco houses; he does not campaign (CR-071 E9.2, K-8). *(CR-071 S6)*

**E4. The charters of 1887–88 (R8).** The regency recharters the Patrimony with the other estates and offices, which brings its chairman under CR-038's rule. The storyline's extension of the age to every "estate head and senior officer" (line 876) may already reach him, but §24 lists the Development Fund apart from the estates (line 849), so the recharter removes the doubt. A private banker could never be retired by law; a chartered officer can. His retirement in 1894 is the visible proof that the bargains happened: by then he holds an office, not an empire.

## F. The end of the arc (1889–1901)

- **1889 (R14).** The Patrimony under Ralston arranges the money for the regency's purchase of the French canal company (canon §19).
- **1894 (R4).** Ralston turns 68 and retires. He asks the Regent to set the rule aside for him, and she answers that the charters are not the Crown's to touch (G3; line 849).
- **1895 (R3).** Canon's rescue of the Union's gold reserve goes east through a syndicate led by Alvord's Bank of California, at canon's price (canal recognition, neutrality in an Asian war, tariff concessions). The man who kept the coast's gold from the Union through the war retired the year before; his bank now sends the gold east to save it.
- **1901 (R3, R15).** The San Francisco syndicate that buys Carnegie Steel for $480m (canon §25) is the Bank of California's, and the Pacifican mills it joins to Carnegie's began as Ralston's Pacific Rolling Mills (founded 1866; Hist., not in Lavender). Canon leaves the "rolling capacity" of Kelly and Holley's steel undertaking OPEN (line 303); PROPOSED: Pacific Rolling Mills is that capacity, with Ralston's money behind Kelly's process and Holley's plant (decision 19). The canon wording "a San Francisco syndicate" (line 985) stands; CR-020's "Pacifica Steel buys" is read in its sense.
- **His death, 1897 (R17).** He keeps swimming off North Beach into old age and dies there of a stroke in 1897, at seventy-one, two years after his bank sent the gold east and four before it buys Carnegie. The cause his friends' jury gave in 1875, congestion of the brain in the cold water (Lavender, p. 381), comes true twenty-two years later.

## G. Tensions for the page

- **G1. The temperament.** Ralston believes nothing is impossible; the Patrimony's printed accounts say exactly what everything costs. His fights as chairman are with his own appetite and with the Patrimony's auditors, and from 1888 with the Office of Public Measures, which prints its tables.
- **G2. The charge.** The Bonanza men, the railway men and the Committee party's last editors say the Fund follows the Bank of California's syndicates. His published accounts answer them, but only just. The *Bulletin* and the *Call*, which hounded him historically (Lavender, pp. 371, 381), are the obvious voices.
- **G3. Victoria, 1894.** The man whose bank carries her credit asks the Regent to set the age rule aside, and she declines: the Crown does not touch the charters (line 849). She could not grant it if she wished, and she does not wish to. A small scene.
- **G4. Sharon, the foil (R11, R23).** Sharon refuses the terms. In 1874 he buys Ophir to raise money for Washoe's legislature, as historically, and runs his men against the Prosperity Party's ticket. The Party carries Washoe in November 1874, as it carries most member legislatures (CR-072 C2); a bought vote stays bought only inside the Party's discipline. When the market breaks in January 1875, Sharon has neither the seat nor the credit: the Reserve Bank will not take his mining shares. The state does nothing to him; its rules and its Party do it. He shows what Ralston escaped. Approved (R24, R25). Lavender doubts Sharon could have known in January 1875 that there was no bonanza in the Ophir (p. 368).
- **G5. The rival for the chair (R12).** Collis Huntington. The railway men are the only power in the field with an organisation as large as Ralston's, and CR-072 (project only) has them quarrelling with the state over Judah; `main` has Judah only "included in principle" (line 291). Historically Huntington and Pullman kept Ralston's Kimball cars off the Central Pacific (Lavender, p. 349), so their rivalry has a history.
