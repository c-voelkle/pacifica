## A. The name (PROPOSED)

**A1. The service is the Pacific Companies; a man in it is a company man.** The word already stands in approved text (l. 817), and it is the right one because it means two things in 1888 English: a company of soldiers, and a company in trade. The Act covers both roads abroad, armed service under treaty and licensed enterprise under bond, and one word names both.

**A2. The units.** A **company** (about 120 men under a captain) is the unit of service. A **capitulation** is the treaty of service with a partner government under which one or more companies serve, the Swiss term kept because it is exact. A **venture** is a licensed enterprise abroad, and its holder a **licensee**.

**A3. "Privateer" stays a legal term, and becomes the nickname.** In law a privateer is the holder of a letter of marque in a declared war, and only 1878 has them (B4). But the prize money of 1878 makes the first fortunes, and in the interior's saloons and the Sacramento school "privateer" becomes the slang for any company man. The Companies Office deplores the word in its reports; the men keep it. The author's word survives where words survive, in the mouths of the men it describes.

*Alternatives:* "the Pacific Privateers" as the official name (inexact for forty peaceful years, and an invitation to every foreign ministry); "the Free Companies" (a medieval mercenary name, the opposite of what the Act intends); "the Capitulated Service" (exact and lifeless).

## B. The Declaration of Paris (PROPOSED)

**B1. 1856: three rules accepted, the fourth withheld.** The Declaration of 16 April 1856 (Hist.) abolishes privateering, protects enemy goods under a neutral flag and neutral goods under an enemy flag, and requires blockades to be effective. It binds only those who accede. The Confederation, founded that year, answers the invitation by a note from Monterey: it will observe the three rules on goods and blockade as its own practice, and it withholds the first. Its reasons are the United States' reasons in Marcy's reply of July 1856 (Hist.): a small navy facing larger ones, and a merchant and whaling fleet that is its reserve. Like Marcy it offers to give up privateering if the great navies exempt all private property at sea from capture, which Britain refuses (Hist.). It adds a sentence of its own: it will issue no letter against a partner's ships or a neutral's goods. Hawaii had already acceded, in March 1855, to the neutral principles of the convention of 1854 between the United States and Russia (Hist.), that free ships make free goods; the note carries the kingdom's undertaking forward for the Confederation.

**B2. 1861: Pacifica stays outside.** Seward offers in 1861 to accede to the Declaration in full, to make Confederate privateers pirates in Europe's eyes (Hist.). Pacifica's note of 1856 already gives Richmond's agents nothing: it issues no letters for anyone else's war, and its neutrality closes its ports to privateers of both sides. The Union's boarding of the Hawaiian packet (CR-068 K16) is fought over neutral rights, which Pacifica has accepted, not over privateering.

**B3. 1878: the one war with letters.** Russia acceded in 1856 (Hist.), but the Declaration binds it only toward other parties; Pacifica's letters are lawful, and Russia protests that they are not. Russia's answer is the Volunteer Fleet, founded by public subscription in April 1878 (Hist., against Britain): fast merchant steamers that can be armed as cruisers. Here part of its first money is raised against Pacifica's sealers, and after the peace its ships carry settlers and rails to the Russian Far East (Hist.). Each side's private fleet shapes the other's navy for twenty years.

**B4. No letters after 1878.** By 1898–99, when Pacifica holds the sea for Japan, the Realm's navy is large and its merchant marine is the target a privateer would hunt. It issues no letters, and its delegates sit at The Hague in 1899 as a power that keeps the laws of war.

**B6. Julian's letters (author's direction, 10:16; PROPOSED shape, amends B5 and D-2).** Julian issues letters of marque again. Where it fits:
- **Not for Siberia (F7).** Canon has no declared war in 1918 ("Nobody declares war on Russia"; the declaration names the Council of People's Commissars a conspiracy), and letters of marque exist only in a declared war, under a prize court. The Bolsheviks also have no Pacific shipping to take.
- **In 1914 against Germany.** Julian declares war on Germany on 23 August 1914 (canon §28). Germany's cruisers in the Pacific live on colliers and supply ships from neutral ports, and its merchantmen run for them. On 24 August Julian issues letters to the company steamers, sealers and whalers, and their prizes go to the courts at San Francisco and Honolulu as in 1878. Prizes are taken on the high seas only, never in neutral waters, and no neutral's cargo is taken (B1).
- **What this needs:** Pacifica must still be outside the Declaration in August 1914. Julian would never denounce a treaty to make war: his credit rests on never breaking a contract for politics (§28). So accession moves from 1907 to **the peace of 1919 at The Hague**, which Pacifica mediates (CR-043). In 1907 the Regency's delegates sign only Convention VII on armed merchant ships and keep the reservation of 1856.
- **The last privateers.** Pacifica's letters of 1914 are the last any power issues. At The Hague in 1919 the mediating crown accedes to the Declaration it kept out of for sixty-three years, and the prize money of 1914 pays for the last fortunes made by privateering.

**B5. 1907: accession (superseded by B6 if D-31 is approved).** At the Second Hague Conference the Regency's delegates announce Pacifica's accession to the Declaration in full. The same conference's Convention VII (Hist.) regulates the conversion of merchant ships into warships under naval commission, and that becomes the naval arm's new form: company men and licensed sealers and steamers are enrolled in the naval reserve. It is the last act of Victoria's foreign policy as regent, signed weeks before Julian accedes.

*Alternatives:* accede in 1856 (then 1878's letters are impossible and an approved date falls); accede in 1899 (possible, but it would make the 1898–99 war's abstention a legal duty rather than a choice); never (Pacifica then keeps a right it never uses, among the few holdouts).

## C. The first generation under the licence, 1854–1887 (dates APPROVED; details PROPOSED)

**C1. 1854: the licence.** California's law answers Walker; from 1856 the Confederation's Chancellery issues the licences under its external power. A licence names the ship or party, the men, the purpose and the host's concession, and requires a bond, forfeited for any warlike act beyond self-defence. Consuls may issue licences abroad and take the bond from a correspondent house. The first holders: sealers on Guadalupe and the Mexican islands under Mexico's permit, Okhotsk whalers, guano men on the equatorial islands (CR-029 A34–A35), traders at Hakodate and Nagasaki after the treaties of 1854 and 1858. **The first refusal (PROPOSED):** in 1857 Henry Crabb, a former California state senator, asks for a licence to take colonists into Sonora (Hist.: he sailed from San Francisco and was shot with his men at Caborca in April 1857). The Republic refuses; he sails from Los Angeles without one and dies at Caborca as he did historically, and the Republic makes no protest to Mexico.

**C2. 1860–63: Ward's company at Shanghai.** (Checked against Carr, *The Devil Soldier*, and Smith, *Mercenaries and Mandarins*, supplied by the author; corrections in Part L1.)
- **Ward (Hist., 1831–1862)** is a Salem sailor. He comes to San Francisco on his father's ship in 1850 and sails from there to Shanghai in 1851. In 1852 he lands at Tehuantepec, joins Walker's Sonora filibuster in 1853 and deserts it, and stays in Mexico, where he refuses President Álvarez's offers and fails in a scrap-metal business. Early in 1854 he rides to California on a mule and signs on at San Francisco as first mate of the clipper *Westward Ho!* for Hong Kong. He then holds a French lieutenancy in the Crimea and resigns after quarrelling with his superior, is first mate of the coastal steamer *Antelope* in China in 1857, is in New York working for his father early in 1859, and reaches Shanghai by San Francisco late in 1859 with his brother Harry (Carr, ch. II). He condemns filibusterism, takes the Union's side in its war, and in 1861–62 becomes a Chinese subject and a mandarin (Carr, ch. IV–VI).
- **In Pacifica (PROPOSED):** he rides into California in the winter of 1854, while the Republic is trying Walker's men and writing the venture licence. He is questioned as a deserter from Walker and released, and he signs on the *Westward Ho!* the same month. He stays an American and becomes a Chinese subject, as historically. **The licence is the company's, not Ward's:** it is held by the Pacifican officers who sail from San Francisco to serve under him, with Yang Fang's San Francisco correspondents as sureties.
- **Partners:** the Shanghai taotai Wu Xu and the banker Yang Fang (Taki), whose Takee bank pays the corps (Hist.).
- **The company inside Ward's force:** the Foreign Arms Corps is raised in June 1860 from the wharves; its first assault on Songjiang fails; by mid-July Ward has added about eighty Filipino "Manilamen" under Vincente Macanaya, and Songjiang falls at the cost of 62 killed and 100 wounded out of about 250; at the first Qingpu his jaw is shattered and he does not leave the field (Hist.). The Pacifican company is one company of this polyglot force: Pacifican officers and sergeants, its ranks Westerners and Manilamen and, from 1861, Chinese. In 1861 the Republic's war at home recalls some of its officers. It holds Shanghai. Ward is shot at Cixi on 21 September 1862 and dies the next day at Ningpo (Hist.).
- **After Ward:** Burgevine takes command and is dismissed after striking Yang Fang at the Takee bank on 4 January 1863 and taking about $40,000 (Hist.); Gordon takes command in late March 1863 (Hist.). The Pacifican company keeps its own captain (name OPEN: Carr finds Forester, the candidate of draft 1, disloyal to Ward's memory and probably not at Cixi, so he is dropped; L1) and serves under Gordon's orders by its articles.
- **Suzhou, December 1863.** Gao Yongkuan and seven Taiping leaders surrender the city on 4 December; on 6 December they are killed by the Qing authorities after Gordon has left (Hist.). Gordon withdraws to Kunshan, threatens Li, and after nearly two months returns to the field (Hist.). The Pacifican company does not return: its articles forbid any part in killing prisoners, and its captain takes it out of the service. The refusal becomes Article 9 of the Act of 1888 (D9), still called "the Suzhou article".

**C3. 1866–75: Japan.**
- **1866–68, Hakodate.** A survey and guard company, veterans of 1861–65, works the Kayanuma coal and the harbour survey for the shogunate's Hakodate magistracy (precedent: Pumpelly and Blake's survey of Ezo for the shogunate in 1862, Hist.). When Enomoto's fleet comes in October 1868 (Hist.), the company will not serve a government no one recognises; it withdraws to the foreign settlement, unlike the French officers who joined him (Brunet, Hist.). Kuroda takes Hakodate in June 1869 (Hist.) and finds the survey and its maps intact.
- **1869–75, the Development Commission.** Kuroda's Kaitakushi (1869–82, Hist.) hires the same men. Companies of survey and guard work the roads, coal and harbours with the Pacifican advisers (canon: "Pacifican advisers north"; the advisers' chief OPEN, historically Horace Capron, 1871–75). From 1874 their sergeants train Kuroda's settler soldiers, the *tondenhei* (Hist.), as Sitka's garrison settles in the same years (CR-070).
- **The army mission.** After France's defeat (canon) the companies lend the Pacifican army mission its sergeant-instructors under the alliance of 1870–71.
- **The end, 1875.** By 1875 Japanese sergeants train the tondenhei, the advisers' contracts end, and the companies come home. Their maps stay in Sapporo.

**C4. 1878: the letters.** In the war with Russia the Confederation issues letters of marque to armed sealers and whalers, about thirty vessels (MODEL). They take Russian supply ships in the Okhotsk and off Kamchatka, never a neutral's cargo (B1). Prize courts sit at San Francisco and Honolulu; Honolulu's court is a Hawaiian court, and Hawaiian crews take their shares there. The shares make the first fortunes, and the first interior farms are bought with them.

**C5. 1884–85: Keelung.** A company guards the Chinese–Pacifican coal company's mines and miners at Keelung while the squadron demonstrates (§16). Pacifica is neutral, and the company's orders are the property guard's: it holds the pitheads and the miners' town, and it withdraws from any line the French attack. Liu Mingchuan, defending Taiwan (Hist.), wants more and is refused; he remembers the refusal and the coal that kept coming.

**C6. 1885: the Customs.** Under Fong's Inspectorate, Pacifican company men serve in the preventive service: the revenue cruisers and the anti-smuggling launches of the Canton delta and the Formosa strait. Until 1888 they serve under licence; from 1888 under the first treaty of service with China (F1).

**C7. Also under licence, 1882 (PROPOSED, new):** the Pacifican Borneo company (CR-029 A42, chartered about 1881) raises its constabulary from company veterans (precedent: the British North Borneo company's armed constabulary of 1882, Hist., to verify).

**C8. By 1887 (MODEL):** about 3,500 men have served under licence; about 300 hold interior farms bought with pay and prize money; about 150 have died in service.

## D. The Companies Act of 1888 (PROPOSED terms)

Passed by the first chambers of the Realm in 1888, with the labour law and the Franchise Law, as the settlers' promise of 1887 (CR-071 H7.8). The venture licence of 1854 is absorbed into it. Its articles:

**D1. Two roads.** Service abroad under a capitulation (D3–D10) and ventures abroad under licence and bond (D11). Letters of marque in a declared war remain a power of the Crown under prize law (until 1907, B5).

**D2. The Companies Office.** A small office under the Foreign Secretary, with a board: the Foreign Secretary's delegate, the inland command's general, a judge of the Court of the Realm and the Land Fund's commissioner. It negotiates capitulations, issues licences, keeps the rolls and pays the warrants. The Office of Public Measures publishes its tables every year: men serving and where, dead and wounded, refusals, warrants redeemed, bonds forfeited.

**D3. Capitulations are treaties.** No company serves abroad except under a treaty of service with a recognised government, signed by the Crown's government and laid before the chambers. A treaty fixes pay, term, station and duties, keeps the company's own officers, courts and chaplains, and provides repatriation at the host's cost.

**D4. Who may serve.** Any citizen from eighteen to thirty at first engagement, of any origin or faith (CR-037), examined or not, and any resident who has declared for naturalisation. Native nations' citizens serve on the same terms. Five years' service counts as five years' residence, but the civil examination is still taken in English (CR-021 C3). Armed service is for men; the medical service takes women as nurses from 1888 (OPEN).

**D5. The school at Sacramento.** One year under the inland command's officers in California's old capitol, inspected by CR-038's inspectors. Admission is by trial of body and nerve, not by paper: riding, shooting, seamanship (at Benicia), surveying, field medicine and English for those who lack it. Every cadet learns a working tongue of the station he is bound for, and a man who passes the interpreter's examination in it casts a tongue vote (CR-021 C3). Rank inside goes by deeds before witnesses (H6.4, 3). Silas is its patron (H7.1).

**D6. Pay.** The host pays, through the Office, never through a broker (CR-031-R1), at the treaty's rate, which is never below the Realm's benchmark for the rank (CR-038). Half is paid abroad. The other half, the *home half*, is held at the Land Fund at interest and paid at discharge, so that a man comes home with capital as well as land.

**D7. The warrant.** See Part E.

**D8. The commission and the vote.** A company commission (lieutenant and above, after the school's officers' course and two years' service) is a commission under the Franchise Law and carries the third vote (canon tension T1).

**D9. The Suzhou article.** No company takes part in killing prisoners, in reprisals on the unarmed or in forced labour. A captain who is ordered to may withdraw his company on his own authority and must report it to the Office within a month; the Office confirms or reverses the withdrawal. A captain who obeys such an order is tried at home.

**D10. The limits.** A company never serves:
- in the Americas;
- in a war against the Realm or a partner;
- for a pretender or a rebellion against a recognised government;
- in a European power's colonial army;
- on land taken from its people except by compact.

The Act also forbids filibustering in any form, and arms supplied abroad except by the Realm's open contracts (CR-048 A2). A treaty that breaks these is void, and a company serving under it comes home.

**D11. Ventures.** A licence and bond for any armed or labour-employing venture abroad, only on a concession the host has granted, with the Realm's consular courts where treaties provide them. The labour article goes with the licence: no labour bound by debt, the same wage for the same work, wages paid in money. A breach forfeits the bond and the licence.

**D13. Acts of state are outside the Act.** The Act does not govern the Realm's own forces, the Atlantic Auxiliary or a mission the Crown's government sends abroad. Those are acts of state, decided by the government and answerable to the chambers. A company is never a disguise for one, and a mission is never raised as a company (Part J).

**D12. Honour at home.** The roll of deeds, read at the counties' festivals of marksmen and riders (H6.4, 3); no preference in the Realm's offices, which are filled by examination only (CR-037).

## E. The warrant (ten years for the full farm APPROVED; acres MODEL; riders PROPOSED)

> For E4: I'm thinking it makes more sense to bind men for ten years for the full reward. (Author, 18 November 2026, 09:04, on draft 1's first alternative)

**E1. Ten years for a farm (APPROVED).** Each five-year engagement earns half a farm: 40 irrigated acres in a district on the Land Fund's bought land, or 80 dry-farming acres, or an interior town lot with the difference in Development Fund credit. A second engagement completes the farm: 80 irrigated or 160 dry acres after ten years. The warrant is good only in the interior's settlement districts (CR-071 C5) and only on land the Realm has bought (CR-033 A19). An orchard of forty acres is already a living in the valley; a whole farm is a man's at thirty if he enlisted at twenty.

**E2. The farm ceiling holds.** The labour law sells no household more than a family farm, and ten years' warrants reach that ceiling exactly. Service beyond ten years earns the district's water shares paid up and a Development Fund loan for machines, or a town lot; never more land.

**E3. Riders (PROPOSED).**
- **The engagement is five years, the reward ten.** A man is bound by the incentive, not by a ten-year contract: he may leave after five with his half farm and his home half, and re-engage for the second.
- **Wounds and death.** A man discharged for wounds, or killed, earns the whole farm at once, his own or his widow's or parents'.
- **Against the speculator.** The United States' bounty warrants of 1847–55 were sold to speculators and gathered into large holdings (Hist.). Here a warrant cannot be sold until the holder has lived on the land for three years.
- **The nations.** A Native man's warrant is paid as land joined to his homeland by supplementary compact (CR-071 G, D1), held by his nation's custom (PROPOSED, nations' companies, to be drafted).

**E4. Set aside (draft 1):** 80 irrigated or 160 dry acres for each five years (the farm in five years, too fast to keep men a second term); a warrant by value, redeemable anywhere (a sum, not a farm, and it sends men to the coast rather than the interior).

## G. What it gives the story (PROPOSED)

- **A second type beside the examined.** The Polytechnic man rises by paper; the company man rises by deeds before witnesses, and comes home with eighty acres, capital, a tongue and a vote. Each class thinks the other has the easier road.
- **Silas's institution.** The inland command's officers train the companies, and Silas's interest becomes the order's (H7.1). The double loyalty of the company men, to the Party's lodges and to their old commander, is what 1906 tests.
- **The bridge.** The company men served beside Chinese, Japanese and Filipino comrades. In the irrigation districts they are the settlers least ready to riot, and in 1906 and 1910–11 they are on the other side from the nativists.
- **The Suzhou article** is the company's pride. A captain in Korea, or in Siberia's Red Terror, has to decide whether an order is one it covers.
- **The companies' rivalry** at the festivals, raised against raised and raised against open (I8).
- **The privateers' fortunes of 1878** give the interior its first rich families who are neither ranchers nor bankers.

## I. Raised companies: freedom of association (direction from the author, 09:04; terms PROPOSED)

**I1. Two kinds of company.** An **open company** is raised by the Companies Office and takes any man who passes the school's trials. A **raised company** is raised by a body of citizens under a charter from the Office: a nation, a district's settlers, a church's members, a trade, a lodge or any group that can fill a company. This is how Pacifica already raised its volunteers: Carson's Taos and Rio Arriba companies under their own captains (CR-066 E), Lot Smith's Mormon cavalry, the Nauvoo Legion at Bannack (CR-060); and historically the Mormon Battalion of 1846. CR-037 governs the Realm's institutions at home (counties, colleges, offices); a company is a voluntary association serving abroad, and the Realm does not ask who its members are (T5).

**I2. What a raised company decides.**
- its name, and whom it takes: it may take only its own people;
- its language of command, provided its liaison officer has passed the examination in English;
- its colours, carried beside the Realm's, and its regalia and dress beyond the field kit;
- its own camp before Sacramento, and its own chaplain or healer;
- its honour code, which may be stricter than the Act's and never laxer. Counting coup fits D5's rank by deeds before witnesses; trophies from the dead are forbidden.

**I3. What it may not do.**
- **Lower the floor:** the Suzhou article (D9), the limits (D10), the pay floor (D6).
- **Commission its own officers.** Every commission comes from the Sacramento officers' course and the Office, so no church, lodge or council can hand out third votes (D8).
- **Hold arms or act at home.** Between engagements its arms are in the Realm's arsenals, as under the Militia Act of 1882, and its colours are kept by the body that raised it. Order at home stays with those canon gives it to: the inland command, the militia reserve, the Queen's Guard and the nations' police ("each people is kept in order by its own").
- **Proselytise under arms.** No company carries a mission, preaches or protects one on the strength of its capitulation. China after the Taiping and Korea's wary court make this the rule a host most needs.
- **Hold a man.** No raised company may bar a man from the open companies or punish him for leaving; at the end of any engagement he may transfer.

**I4. Mixed stations.** The Office posts raised companies alongside open ones, and no station abroad is held by raised companies of one kind alone. The bridge of the first generation (CR-071: "they served beside Chinese and Japanese comrades") survives the right of association.

**I5. The nations: an asymmetry of polity, not of association.** A nation is a compact polity with land and a Senate seat, not an association (CR-071 I), so its company has three things no other raised company has:
- **Consent.** The nation's council consents to each posting.
- **Land.** Its men's warrants are paid as land joined to the homeland by supplementary compact (E3).
- **Its own ventures.** A chartered nation may also hold venture licences as a body (CR-071 G, D2.3), and its veterans may become its police (D2.4).

Its drill book is written in the nation's language, which helps that language toward certification on the Crown's list and its men toward tongue votes. Some nations will refuse to raise a company. The Hopi are the likeliest: there is no Hopi government to raise one, since each village signs for itself (HP1), and the villages keep the Realm's agents, schools and missions off the mesas (HP5).

**I6. Hawaii.** The Hawaiian crown may charter raised companies under an act of its own, as it does its districts (CR-057), for Hawaiian seamen in the sealing and whaling ventures and the naval reserve.

**I7. Candidates (PROPOSED; the first is the author's call).**
- **Nations:** Chiricahua (Apache Pass, 1862; the US Army's Apache scouts are the historical parallel), Nez Perce and Ute (horsemen), Tlingit and Haida (seamen), Shoshone (the Snake road's scouts under the Box Elder compact).
- **Deseret:** a Saints' company, chartered to its members, not to the Church; the Mormon Battalion is its memory.
- **Settlers:** companies of the irrigation districts and of the inland command's sons.
- **Others:** a Chinese-Pacifican company (the name "Six Companies" is taken in San Francisco by the merchant associations, Hist., so it needs another), a Japanese-Pacifican company, an Irish company of Roney's trades.

**I8. Rivalry as agon.** Companies compete at the counties' festivals of marksmen and riders (H6.4, 3), and the best company of the year carries its colours at the head of the Regent's review. Rivalry between raised companies is the agon the settlers asked for, with witnesses.

**I9. The risks, for the story.** A settler company from the Feather has no Chinese comrades, and it is the unit whose double loyalty to the Party's lodges and to Silas is tested in 1906. A Saints' company in Korea tempts missionaries to follow it. A nation's council splits over raising one. None of these is a reason to forbid them.

**I10. Scale (MODEL):** the first raised companies in 1889–95; by 1914 about a third of the companies are raised.

## K. The founding companies of 1846 (PROPOSED; the author's question of 09:54)

**K1. The test: a company of the founders.** The founders of 1846 are not a company. They are the convention that proclaims the Republic at Pentecost (Pico, Castro, Alvarado, a reluctant Vallejo, de la Guerra, Hartnell, Elias and others, §4), and three weeks later Vallejo is a prisoner of the Bear Flaggers at Sonoma. A company of all the founders would put grandees in the ranks, which canon never does. One battle does have founders in the ranks: at Olompali on 24 June 1846 "a mixed Californio and Indigenous force including Elias and Caleb defeats a force of Bear Flaggers at serious cost" (§4, canon). **Recommendation:** the founding company is the force of Olompali, with Elias and Caleb in it, and not the founders as a body.

**K2. What Olompali gives (Hist.).** The battle was fought at the adobe of Camilo Ynitia (1803–1856), headman of the Coast Miwok of Olompali and the only Native American on the northern frontier to hold and keep a Mexican land grant, Rancho Olompali (1843). Castro's men under Joaquín de la Torre came up from Monterey, about fifty, with some twenty irregulars. In canon they are the Republic's men, and they win.

**K3. Two founding companies at one battle.**
- **The open company: the Company of Olompali.** De la Torre's Californios, the irregulars and the Anglos riding with them, Elias and Caleb among them, raised by the Republic in its first month and open to any man who would fight for it.
- **The raised company: Ynitia's men.** The Coast Miwok of Olompali fight on their own ground under their own headman, beside the Republic's men and not in their ranks. This is the first raised company, a nation's, fifteen years before the Civil War, as the author asks.
- The Coast Miwok are not the Miwok of Elias's guide in 1847 (the Sierra Miwok of the Mokelumne and the Cosumnes tract); the two must not be merged.

**K4. They are ancestors, not precedents.** Both companies fight at home, for the Republic's founding, seven years before the venture licence; neither serves abroad, and neither is a mission (D13). The Act of 1888 claims them in its preamble and its roll:
- The roll of deeds opens with **the dead of Olompali**, by name, not with Elias (CR-049: lost companions are remembered by name). "At serious cost" (canon) becomes the companies' first entry.
- The Company of Olompali is never raised again; its colour is carried each year by the best open company at the Regent's review (I8). Ynitia's colour is kept by the Coast Miwok and carried by the best raised company.
- The preamble's argument is the settlers' (H6.4): the Republic was founded by men no one examined.

**K5. The line from 1846 to 1888.** Olompali (1846, the Republic's own companies) → the refusal of the volunteer expeditions (1851, CR-033: a militia that makes its own war is a private army) → Walker and the licence (1853–54) → Crabb refused (1857, C1) → Shanghai (1860, the first company abroad) → the Act (1888).

**K6. Elias at Olompali.** Canon gives him the lesson: "winning loyalty by persuasion does not remove the consequences of taking up arms" (§4). The Suzhou article (D9) is that lesson made law. The Chronicler need not say so.

**K7. Canon tension:** the approved sentence "The first company is raised at Shanghai in 1860" (§19, l. 817) becomes "The first company abroad is raised at Shanghai in 1860" (T6).

**L2. Set pieces.**

1. **The mule and the trial (winter 1854).** A Salem sailor rides into California on a single mule while the Republic tries Walker's men (Carr ch. II for the mule and the *Westward Ho!*). The court releases him because he deserted Walker before the blockade; the licence law is in that month's papers. He signs on for Hong Kong and does not look back. Six years later the licence is what lets Pacifican officers serve under him.
2. **Songjiang, July 1860.** The first assault on Songjiang fails. Ward goes back to the waterfront, finds Vincente Macanaya and about eighty Manilamen, buys guns, and takes the town at the cost of two-thirds of his men killed or wounded (Smith ch. 2; Carr ch. III). At Qingpu his jaw is shattered and he stays on the field.
3. **The British raid and the window (April–May 1861).** Admiral Hope's men raid Songjiang for the Royal Navy deserters Ward has hired. Here they find the Pacifican company's licence and roll in order (no deserters, every man sworn under the Republic's bond) and leave it, and take Ward. Wu Xu produces papers, probably forged, making Ward a Chinese subject. Ward is held in a cabin aboard a British warship. In the last week of May, at two in the morning, he jumps through the cabin window into Vincente's sampan while thirty of Yang Fang's sampans scatter across the harbour (Carr ch. IV). Meanwhile some eight hundred British troops march round the company's mud forts outside Songjiang and go back to their ships without firing (Carr ch. IV, on Forester's garrison).
4. **The Hua banner.** Ward gives the force a flag of its own, a large rectangle bearing the single character *Hua*, the first syllable of his Chinese name, as the Chinese volunteer armies carried their commanders' names (Smith ch. 5). It is the precedent for every raised company's colours (I2).
5. **The Sacramento men on the Huangpu.** The force's river steamers, the *Hyson* among them, are "usually managed by Americans, who handle river-boats of this class better than Englishmen do" (Smith ch. 5). Here they are Sacramento and delta steamboat men. The shallow *Kajow* is the only steamer that can pass Songjiang's water gate.
6. **The wedding (mid-March 1862).** Ward marries Yang Fang's daughter Changmei, aged twenty-one, in a Chinese ceremony, as Peking approves him as a Chinese subject (Carr ch. V–VI). The Pacifican officers at the wedding are the men who will refuse Suzhou.
7. **The wrong war (1862).** Ward writes to Burlingame that if "old Uncle Abe does not give them thunder this fall" he will be surprised, and pledges ten thousand to the Union cause; his brother Harry, sent to buy steamers in America for China, sells them to the Union Army (Carr ch. VI, p. 223–226). Here the Republic is at war with the Union, and the Pacifican company serves under a Union man. The consul's report to Monterey and the captain's answer (the licence binds the company's conduct, not its commander's opinions) are a scene of their own.
8. **Cixi, 21 September 1862.** The plain is burning and the people are swimming the river on logs. Ward is hit in the stomach while the ladders go up; Vincente is first over the wall. On the *Hardy*, making for Ningpo, Ward dictates his debts to Lieutenant Bogle: Wu Xu owes him 110,000 taels, Yang Fang 30,000, and his wife is to have 50,000 (Carr ch. VII). The debts are argued over for years afterwards.
9. **Suzhou and the consuls (December 1863).** On 16 December the consuls at Shanghai, for every power great and small, unanimously condemn the killings at Suzhou as treachery (Smith ch. 7). The Pacifican consul signs with them. Li's reasons should be given fairly: the throne's amnesty policy was real, he feared a feigned surrender like the one at Taicang that April, and the surrendered leaders came unshaved and asked for new terms; Peking answered that "foreigners do not understand the principles involved" (Smith ch. 7). Gordon goes back after two months; the Pacifican company sails for home.
10. **Hua, the White God.** Ward is buried at Songjiang and a memorial temple with annual sacrifices is built round his grave (Carr, prologue). In 1900 an old Shanghai merchant tells foreign soldiers that none of them was first into a sacred place, because Hua had come from across the sea to fight for China and been carried into a temple (Carr, prologue). Here a delegation of the companies dips its colours at the shrine every year, and the roll of deeds, though it begins with Olompali, carries Ward's name and Vincente's.
11. **Customs, not command.** Peking tolerated foreigners in the Maritime Customs because of the regularity of its revenue, but was too jealous to put its army and navy under foreign control (Smith ch. 9, from the Ili crisis). That is the reason the companies' capitulations in China are the Customs' preventive service and the Formosan revenue guard, not commands (F1, F3), and why Korea's is instruction (F2).
12. **The tongue.** In the Ever-Victorious Army commands were given in English, and few of the Chinese learned a foreign language, "just as few Western officers, including Ward and Gordon, learned Chinese" (Smith ch. 5 and 9). Ward's force fell apart when he died, because it rested on him personally (Smith ch. 5: no desertions until Cixi). The school's working tongue (D5) and the Act's institutions (D2–D3) answer both failures.
