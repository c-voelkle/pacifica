## 0. What canon already fixes (APPROVED in CR-071)

- **1854:** the venture licence. No one may make war abroad from the Republic's soil; a venture abroad needs the Republic's licence and bond (§6, l. 246).
- **1860–63:** the first company abroad, at Shanghai (amended by T6).
- **1866–75:** veterans of 1861–65 serve in Japan.
- **1878:** the Republic issues letters of marque in the war with Russia.
- **1884–85:** a company guards the Keelung coal.
- **From 1885:** Pacificans serve in the Customs' preventive service.
- **By 1887:** some thousands have served; hundreds have bought interior farms with what they brought home; they served beside Chinese and Japanese comrades (§19, l. 817).
- **The shape (H-19):** training and legal avenues abroad for the unexamined; technical schools at home; a promise in the settlement of 1887–88; its own CR.
- **The limits (H6.5, 4):** no filibustering, no service in the Americas, no land taken except by compact, no covert arms.

## A. The name (APPROVED, D-1)

**A1. The service is the Pacific Companies; a man in it is a company man.** The word means two things in 1888 English: a company of soldiers, and a company in trade. The Act covers both roads abroad, armed service under treaty and licensed enterprise under bond, and one word names both.

**A2. The units.** A **company** (about 120 men under a captain) is the unit of service. A **capitulation** is the treaty of service with a partner government under which one or more companies serve, the Swiss term kept because it is exact. A **venture** is a licensed enterprise abroad, and its holder a **licensee**.

**A3. "Privateer" stays a legal term, and becomes the nickname.** In law a privateer holds a letter of marque in a declared war: 1878 and 1914 have them (B3, B5). After the prize money of 1878, "privateer" becomes the slang of the interior's saloons and the Sacramento school for any company man. The Companies Office deplores the word in its reports, and the men keep it.

## B. The Declaration of Paris (APPROVED, D-2 as amended by D-31)

**B1. 1856: three rules accepted, the fourth withheld.** The Declaration of 16 April 1856 (Hist.) abolishes privateering, protects enemy goods under a neutral flag and neutral goods under an enemy flag, and requires blockades to be effective. It binds only those who accede, and it cannot be accepted in part. The Confederation, founded that year, answers the invitation by a note from Monterey: it will observe the rules on goods and blockade as its own practice, and it does not accede. Its reasons are the United States' reasons in Marcy's reply of July 1856 (Hist.): a small navy facing larger ones, and a merchant and whaling fleet that is its reserve. Like Marcy it offers to give up privateering if the great navies exempt all private property at sea from capture, which Britain refuses (Hist.). It adds that it will issue no letter against a partner's ships or a neutral's goods. Hawaii had acceded in March 1855 to the neutral principles of the convention of 1854 between the United States and Russia, that free ships make free goods (Hist.); the note carries the kingdom's undertaking forward for the Confederation.

**B2. 1861: Pacifica stays outside.** Seward offers in 1861 to accede in full, to make Confederate privateers pirates in Europe's eyes (Hist.). Pacifica's note of 1856 already gives Richmond's agents nothing: it issues no letters for anyone else's war, and its neutrality closes its ports to privateers of both sides.

**B3. 1878: the first war with letters.** Russia, an original signatory, is bound only toward other parties, so Pacifica's letters are lawful; Russia protests that they are not. Its answer is the Volunteer Fleet, founded by public subscription in April 1878 (Hist., against Britain): fast merchant steamers that can be armed as cruisers. Here part of its first money is raised against Pacifica's sealers, and after the peace its ships carry settlers and rails to the Russian Far East (Hist.).

**B4. No letters in 1898–99.** By then the Realm's navy is large and its merchant marine is the target a privateer would hunt. It issues no letters, and its delegates sit at The Hague in 1899 as a power that keeps the laws of war.

**B5. 1914: Julian's letters (APPROVED, D-31).**
- **Not for Siberia (F7).** Canon has no declared war in 1918 ("Nobody declares war on Russia"), and letters of marque exist only in a declared war, under a prize court.
- **Against Germany.** Julian declares war on Germany on 23 August 1914 (§28). Germany's cruisers in the Pacific live on colliers and supply ships from neutral ports. On 24 August he issues letters to the steamers of licensed ventures, the companies' tenders and the sealers and whalers. Their prizes go to the prize courts at San Francisco and Honolulu. Prizes are taken on the high seas only, never in neutral waters, and no neutral's cargo is taken (B1).

**B6. 1907 and 1919: why accession waits (APPROVED, D-31; reason revised by R-6).** The Declaration has no article of withdrawal: accession is for ever, and so Pacifica accedes only when it means to give up the power for good. At the Second Hague Conference in 1907 the Regency's delegates sign the conventions, Convention VII on armed merchant ships among them, and keep the reservation of 1856. No company or venture ship is enrolled as a naval auxiliary under Convention VII, so that a letter of marque remains possible (R-7). At The Hague in 1919, where Pacifica mediates the peace (CR-043), it accedes to the Declaration, after Julian's letters of 1914.

## C. The first generation under the licence, 1854–1887 (dates APPROVED; details PROPOSED unless marked)

**C1. 1854: the licence.**
- **Who issues it:** California's government from 1854; the Confederation's Chancellery from 1856; from 1865, when the Chancellorship lapses under the Providentorship, the Providentor's cabinet through its office for external affairs; from 1888 the Companies Office (D2). Consuls may issue licences abroad and take the bond from a correspondent house.
- **What it requires:** a licence names the ship or party, the men, the purpose and the host's concession, and requires a bond, forfeited for any warlike act beyond self-defence.
- **The first holders:** sealers on Guadalupe and the Mexican islands under Mexico's permit, Okhotsk whalers, guano men on the equatorial islands (CR-029 A34–A35), traders at Hakodate and Nagasaki after the treaties of 1854 and 1858.
- **The first refusal (APPROVED, D-30; shape revised by R-18).** In 1857 Henry Crabb, a former California state senator, asks for a licence to take colonists into Sonora (Hist.: he sailed from San Francisco and was shot with his men at Caborca in April 1857). The Republic refuses. His ship is stopped at the wharf, and he slips out overland across the Colorado with the men who will follow him. His backers in San Francisco are prosecuted under the law of 1854 for fitting out an expedition; Crabb dies at Caborca as he did historically, and the Republic makes no protest to Mexico.

**C2. 1860–63: Ward's company at Shanghai.** (Checked against Carr, *The Devil Soldier*, and Smith, *Mercenaries and Mandarins*, supplied by the author; Part L.)
- **Ward (Hist., 1831–1862)** is a Salem sailor. He comes to San Francisco on his father's ship in 1850 and sails from there to Shanghai in 1851. In 1852 he lands at Tehuantepec, joins Walker's Sonora filibuster in 1853 and deserts it, and stays in Mexico, where he refuses General Álvarez's offers and fails in a scrap-metal business. Early in 1854 he rides to California on a mule and signs on at San Francisco as first mate of the clipper *Westward Ho!* for Hong Kong. He holds a French lieutenancy in the Crimea and resigns after quarrelling with his superior, is first mate of the coastal steamer *Antelope* in China in 1857, works for his father in New York early in 1859, and reaches Shanghai by San Francisco late in 1859 with his brother Harry (Carr ch. II).
- **In Pacifica (APPROVED, D-33, D-35):** Ward is a Pacifican. He rides into California in the winter of 1854, while the Republic is trying Walker's men and writing the venture licence; he is questioned as a deserter from Walker and released, and signs on the *Westward Ho!* the same month. In the autumn of 1859, on his way to China through San Francisco, he swears allegiance to the Republic. That an oath is enough is PROPOSED here: canon leaves the exact naturalization rules OPEN (§4) and residence is a member's power (§8), so this settles California's rule for him (R-16). He holds the Qing's rank by letter of commission without becoming a Chinese subject, as Gordon later does. His brother Harry stays an American. The company's licence is held by its Pacifican officers, Ward first among them, with Yang Fang's San Francisco correspondents as sureties.
- **Partners:** the Shanghai taotai Wu Xu and the banker Yang Fang (Taki), whose Takee bank pays the corps (Hist.).
- **The company inside Ward's force:** the Foreign Arms Corps is raised in June 1860 from the wharves; its first assault on Songjiang fails; by mid-July Ward has added about eighty Filipino "Manilamen" under Vincente Macanaya, and Songjiang falls at the cost of 62 killed and 100 wounded out of about 250; at the first Qingpu his jaw is shattered and he does not leave the field (Hist.). The Pacifican company is one company of this polyglot force: Pacifican officers and sergeants, its ranks Westerners and Manilamen and, from 1861, Chinese. In 1861 the Republic's war at home recalls some of its officers. Ward is shot at Cixi on 21 September 1862 and dies the next day at Ningpo (Hist.).
- **After Ward:** Burgevine takes command and is dismissed after striking Yang Fang at the Takee bank on 4 January 1863 and taking about $40,000 (Hist.); Gordon takes command in late March 1863 (Hist.). The Pacifican company keeps its own captain (name OPEN) and serves under Gordon's orders by its articles.
- **Suzhou, December 1863.** Gao Yongkuan's party kills Tan Shaoguang on 4 December and surrenders the city on the 5th; on the 6th Gao and seven others are killed by the Qing authorities after Gordon has left (Hist.). Gordon withdraws to Kunshan, threatens Li, and after nearly two months returns to the field (Hist.). The Pacifican company does not return: its articles forbid it to serve a commander who kills prisoners who have surrendered to him, and its captain takes it out of the service. The refusal becomes Article 9 of the Act of 1888 (D9), still called "the Suzhou article".

**C2a. The test of a Pacifican Ward (done; APPROVED as D-35).**
- *For it:* he keeps coming through San Francisco (1850, 1851, 1854, 1859; Carr ch. II); on 24 April 1861 he asked the American consul to let him give up his citizenship, to escape the consuls' jurisdiction over a man in Chinese service (Carr ch. IV), which the Republic's licence answers in advance; he chose flags by how countries were governed, refusing Álvarez because of Mexico's "governmental affairs" (Carr ch. II, quoting Schmidt), and condemned filibustering after Sonora; he integrated where he served (Carr ch. II, V–VI).
- *Against it, answered:* his Union sympathies of 1862 are dropped; nothing else depends on them. His Chinese subjecthood is not needed: he was a Qing officer "commissioned by letter" (Smith ch. 5), and Gordon held Qing rank without naturalizing. A Pacifican licence against the Taiping puts the Republic on record against Admiral Hope's truce with the rebels in 1860–61: friction with London, not a breach, and an early credit with Li Hongzhang and the Shanghai gentry.
- *Conditions:* the oath in 1859, so his service in the Crimea is an American's; Qing rank by letter; Harry American; the licence held by the company's officers, so it survives Ward.

**C3. 1866–75: Japan.**
- **1866–68, Hakodate.** A survey and guard company, many of its men home from the technical mission on the Gulf (J3.2), works the Kayanuma coal and the harbour survey for the shogunate's Hakodate magistracy (precedent: Pumpelly and Blake's survey of Ezo for the shogunate in 1862, Hist.). In the spring of 1868 the port passes to the new government's Hakodate prefecture (Hist.), and the company's contract passes with it. When Enomoto's fleet comes in October 1868 (Hist.), the company will not serve him; it withdraws to the foreign settlement, unlike the French officers who joined him (Brunet, Hist.). Kuroda takes Hakodate in June 1869 (Hist.) and finds the survey and its maps intact.
- **1869–75, the Development Commission.** The Kaitakushi (from 1869; Kuroda its deputy from 1870 and director from 1874, Hist.) hires the same men. Companies of survey and guard work the roads, coal and harbours with the Pacifican advisers (canon: "Pacifican advisers north"; the advisers' chief OPEN, historically Horace Capron, 1871–75). From 1874 their sergeants train Kuroda's settler soldiers, the *tondenhei* (Hist.), as Sitka's garrison settles in the same years (CR-070).
- **The Ainu (R-21).** The tondenhei are settled on Ainu land without the Ainu's consent, and Sitka's assayers say so (canon §14, l. 685). The company men who drilled them come home knowing it. It is a second root, beside Olompali (K6), of the Act's rule that no company serves on land taken from its people except by compact (D10).
- **The army mission (R-22).** After France's defeat (canon) the Pacifican army mission takes sergeant-instructors from the companies. They are discharged from the companies and enlist in the mission, so the mission stays an act of state (D13).
- **The end, 1875.** By 1875 Japanese sergeants train the tondenhei, the advisers' contracts end, and the companies come home. Their maps stay in Sapporo.

**C4. 1878: the letters (scale revised by R-20).** In the war with Russia the Confederation issues letters of marque to about a dozen armed sealers and whalers (MODEL). The Okhotsk is closed by ice in February, when the squadron fights at Vladivostok, so they sail with the thaw and keep the sea through the spring and summer until the Berlin settlement. Russian shipping in the North Pacific is thin, and much of it sails under German and American charters, which the rule of 1856 protects (B1). They take a handful of Russian supply ships off Kamchatka and in the Okhotsk. The prizes go to the confederal prize court at San Francisco and to its sitting at Honolulu, which has Hawaiian assessors. The Hawaiian crews take their shares there, though Hawaiians have not forgotten the Supreme Court judgment of 1876 the Confederation ignored. A few men make fortunes, and everyone in the interior knows their names.

**C5. 1885: Keelung (dates revised by R-19).** The French land at Keelung in August 1884 and hold the port from October 1884 to June 1885 (Hist.). A company licensed late in 1884 waits at Amoy. In the spring of 1885, after Ferry's fall, it lands to guard the Chinese–Pacifican coal company's miners' town and the works being restored while the squadron demonstrates (§16). Pacifica is neutral, and the company holds no line the French attack. Liu Mingchuan, defending Taiwan (Hist.), wants more and is refused; he remembers the refusal and the coal that kept coming.

**C6. 1885: the Customs.** Under Fong's Inspectorate, Pacifican company men serve in the preventive service: the revenue cruisers and the anti-smuggling launches of the Canton delta and the Formosa strait. Until 1888 they serve under licence; in the autumn of 1888 China signs the second treaty of service under the Act (F1).

**C7. Borneo, 1882 (APPROVED, D-7; terms in Part M, APPROVED, D-37).** The Pacifican Borneo company (CR-029 A42, chartered about 1881) raises its constabulary from company veterans under licence (precedent: the British North Borneo company's armed constabulary of 1882, Hist., to verify).

**C8. By 1887 (MODEL):** about 3,500 men have served under licence; about 300 hold interior farms bought with pay and prize money; about 150 have died in service.

## D. The Companies Act of 1888 (APPROVED, D-8; amended by the review as marked)

Passed by the first chambers of the Realm in 1888, with the labour law and the Franchise Law, as the settlers' promise of 1887 (CR-071 H7.8). The venture licence of 1854 is absorbed into it. Its articles:

**D1. Two roads.** Service abroad under a capitulation (D3–D10) and ventures abroad under licence and bond (D11). Letters of marque in a declared war remain a power of the Crown under prize law until Pacifica accedes to the Declaration of Paris (B6).

**D2. The Companies Office.** A small office under the Foreign Secretary, with a board: the Foreign Secretary's delegate, the inland command's commander, a judge of the Court of the Realm and the Land Fund's commissioner. It negotiates capitulations, issues licences, keeps the rolls and pays the warrants. The Office of Public Measures publishes its tables every year: men serving and where, dead and wounded, refusals, warrants redeemed, bonds forfeited.

**D3. Capitulations are treaties.** No company serves abroad except under a treaty of service with a recognised government, signed by the Crown's government and laid before the chambers. A treaty fixes pay, term, station and duties, keeps the company's own officers, courts and chaplains, and provides repatriation at the host's cost.

**D4. Who may serve.** Any citizen from eighteen to thirty at first engagement, of any origin or faith (CR-037), examined or not, and any resident who has declared for naturalisation. Native nations' citizens serve on the same terms, and the dependency's people under F8. Five years' service counts as five years' residence, but the civil examination is still taken in English (CR-021 C3). Armed service is for men; the medical service takes women as nurses from 1888 (APPROVED, D-9).

**D5. The school at Sacramento.** One year under the inland command's officers in California's old capitol, inspected by CR-038's inspectors. Admission is by trial of body and nerve, not by paper: riding, shooting, seamanship (at Benicia), surveying, field medicine and English for those who lack it. Every cadet learns a working tongue of the station he is bound for and earns the school's certificate in it. Where that language is on the Crown's list, a man who passes the interpreter's examination in it alongside examined English casts a tongue vote (CR-021 C3; R-15). Rank inside goes by deeds before witnesses (H6.4, 3). Silas is its patron (H7.1).

**D6. Pay.** The host pays, through the Office, never through a broker (CR-031-R1), at the treaty's rate, which is never below the Realm's benchmark for the rank (CR-038). Half is paid abroad. The other half, the *home half*, is held at the Land Fund at interest and paid at discharge, so that a man comes home with capital as well as land.

**D7. The warrant.** See Part E.

**D8. The commission and the vote (R-13).** A company commission (lieutenant and above, after the school's officers' course and two years' service) is confirmed by the board that examines every commission under the Militia Act of 1882 (Sherman's board, then its successor), so that no Party committee and no company gives rank. Once confirmed it is a commission under the Franchise Law and carries the third vote (T1).

**D9. The Suzhou article.** No company takes part in killing prisoners, in reprisals on the unarmed or in forced labour, and none serves a commander who kills prisoners who have surrendered to him. A captain who is ordered to, or who sees it done, may withdraw his company on his own authority and must report it to the Office within a month; the Office confirms or reverses the withdrawal. A captain who obeys such an order is tried at home.

**D10. The limits.** A company never serves:
- in the Americas;
- in a war against the Realm or a partner;
- for a pretender or a rebellion against a recognised government;
- in a European power's colonial army;
- on land taken from its people except by compact.

**War between partners (R-10).** If two partners go to war with each other, every company serving either stands down from armed duty for the war's length: the Customs' companies keep to unarmed revenue work, and a guard company keeps to its station and drills no one. The Act also forbids filibustering in any form, and arms supplied abroad except by the Realm's open contracts (CR-048 A2). A treaty that breaks these is void, and a company serving under it comes home.

**D11. Ventures.** A licence and bond for any armed or labour-employing venture abroad, only on a concession the host has granted, with the Realm's consular courts where treaties provide them. The labour article goes with the licence: no labour bound by debt, the same wage for the same work, a day of rest in seven, wages paid in money. It covers the Realm's oil companies in Mexico (CR-058) and the Works' mine at Shinkolobwe (CR-049) as much as a sealer. A breach forfeits the bond and the licence.

**D12. Honour at home.** The roll of deeds, read at the counties' festivals of marksmen and riders (H6.4, 3); no preference in the Realm's offices, which are filled by examination only (CR-037).

**D13. Acts of state are outside the Act (APPROVED, D-22).** The Act does not govern the Realm's own forces, the Atlantic Auxiliary or a mission the Crown's government sends abroad. Those are acts of state, decided by the government and answerable to the chambers. A company is never a disguise for one, and a mission is never raised as a company (Part J).

**D14. Companies and CR-037 (APPROVED as amended, R-3).** CR-037 E7's rules for what the Crown charters, funds or licenses apply to the Companies Office and to open companies. Raised companies (Part I) are excepted from E7's rules on membership, because they serve abroad and rule no one at home. The exception holds only while I3's safeguards hold: commissions through the common board, no arms or role at home, no man held.

## E. The warrant (APPROVED, D-10; acres MODEL)

**E1. Ten years for a farm.** Each five-year engagement earns half a farm: 40 irrigated acres in a district on the Land Fund's bought land, or 80 dry-farming acres, or an interior town lot with the difference in Development Fund credit. A second engagement completes the farm: 80 irrigated or 160 dry acres after ten years. The warrant is good only in the interior's settlement districts (CR-071 C5) and only on land the Realm has bought (CR-033 A19). An orchard of forty acres is already a living in the valley; a whole farm is a man's at thirty if he enlisted at twenty.

**E2. The farm ceiling holds.** The labour law sells no household more than a family farm, and ten years' warrants reach that ceiling. This CR therefore fixes the family farm at 80 irrigated or 160 dry acres (a new fact; see Fact changes). Service beyond ten years earns the district's water shares paid up and a Development Fund loan for machines, or a town lot; never more land.

**E3. Riders.**
- **The engagement is five years, the reward ten.** A man is bound by the incentive, not by a ten-year contract: he may leave after five with his half farm and his home half, and re-engage for the second.
- **Wounds and death.** A man discharged for wounds, or killed, earns the whole farm at once, his own or his widow's or parents'.
- **Against the speculator.** The United States' bounty warrants of 1847–55 were sold to speculators and gathered into large holdings (Hist.). Here a warrant cannot be sold until the holder has lived on the land for three years.
- **The nations.** A Native man's warrant is paid as land joined to his homeland by supplementary compact (CR-071 G, D1), held by his nation's custom (I5).
- **The dependency.** A Filipino company man's warrant is a separate warrant under the Organic Act (F8).

## I. Raised companies: freedom of association (APPROVED, D-17 to D-21; D14 applies)

**I1. Two kinds of company.** An **open company** is raised by the Companies Office and takes any man who passes the school's trials. A **raised company** is raised by a body of citizens under a charter from the Office: a nation, a district's settlers, a church's members, a trade, a lodge or any group that can fill a company. Pacifica has raised volunteers this way before: Carson's Taos and Rio Arriba companies under their own captains (CR-066 E), Lot Smith's Mormon cavalry; and historically the Mormon Battalion of 1846. The Realm does not ask who its members are (D14).

**I2. What a raised company decides.**
- its name, and whom it takes: it may take only its own people;
- its language of command, provided its liaison officer has passed the examination in English;
- its colours, carried beside the Realm's, and its regalia and dress beyond the field kit (Ward's *Hua* banner of 1860 is the precedent: Smith ch. 5);
- its own camp before Sacramento, and its own chaplain or healer;
- its honour code, which may be stricter than the Act's and never laxer. Counting coup fits D5's rank by deeds before witnesses; trophies from the dead are forbidden.

**I3. What it may not do.**
- **Lower the floor:** the Suzhou article (D9), the limits (D10), the pay floor (D6).
- **Commission its own officers.** Every commission comes through the officers' course and the board (D8), so no church, lodge or council can hand out third votes.
- **Hold arms or act at home.** Between engagements its arms are in the Realm's arsenals, as under the Militia Act of 1882, and its colours are kept by the body that raised it. Order at home stays with those canon gives it to: the inland command, the militia reserve, the Hawaiian crown's guard and the nations' police ("each people is kept in order by its own").
- **Serve against its own kin.** No raised company is posted where it could face its own people or another Realm nation's.
- **Proselytise under arms.** No company carries a mission, preaches or protects one on the strength of its capitulation.
- **Hold a man.** No raised company may bar a man from the open companies or punish him for leaving; at the end of any engagement he may transfer.

**I4. Mixed stations.** The Office posts raised companies alongside open ones, and no station abroad is held by raised companies of one kind alone.

**I5. The nations: an asymmetry of polity, not of association.** A nation is a compact polity with land and a Senate seat, not an association (CR-071 I), so its company has three things no other raised company has:
- **Consent.** The nation's council consents to each posting.
- **Land.** Its men's warrants are paid as land joined to the homeland by supplementary compact (E3).
- **Its own ventures.** A chartered nation may also hold venture licences as a body, an extension of its power to charter companies at home (CR-071 G, D2.3), and its veterans may become its police (D2.4).

Its drill book is written in the nation's language, which helps that language toward certification on the Crown's list. Some nations will refuse to raise a company. The Hopi are the likeliest: there is no Hopi government to raise one, since each village signs for itself (HP1), and the villages keep the Realm's agents, schools and missions off the mesas (HP5).

**I6. Hawaii (dates corrected by R-25).** From 1907, when the Act of Settlement restores Hawaii's legislature (§26), the Hawaiian crown may charter raised companies under an act of its own, as it does its districts (CR-057), for Hawaiian seamen in the sealing and whaling ventures. Before 1907 Hawaiians serve in open companies and under the Confederation's licences.

**I7. Candidates (APPROVED in principle, D-21).**
- **Nations:** Chiricahua (Apache Pass, 1862; the US Army's Apache scouts are the historical parallel), Nez Perce and Ute (horsemen), Tlingit and Haida (seamen), Shoshone (the Snake road's scouts under the Box Elder compact).
- **Deseret:** a Saints' company, chartered to its members, not to the Church; the Mormon Battalion is its memory.
- **Settlers:** companies of the irrigation districts and of the inland command's sons.
- **Others:** a Chinese-Pacifican company (the name "Six Companies" is taken in San Francisco by the merchant associations, Hist., so it needs another), a Japanese-Pacifican company, an Irish company of Roney's trades.

**I8. Rivalry as agon.** Companies compete at the counties' festivals of marksmen and riders (H6.4, 3), and the best company of the year carries its colours at the head of the sovereign's review, the Regent's until 1907 and Julian's after.

**I9. The risks, for the story.** A settler company from the Feather has no Chinese comrades, and it is the unit whose double loyalty to the Party's lodges and to Silas is tested in 1906. A Saints' company in Korea tempts missionaries to follow it. A nation's council splits over raising one. None of these is a reason to forbid them.

**I10. Scale (MODEL):** the first raised companies in 1889–95; by 1914 about a third of the companies are raised.

## K. The founding companies of 1846 (APPROVED, D-26, D-27)

**K1. The test: a company of the founders.** The founders of 1846 are not a company. They are the convention that proclaims the Republic at Pentecost (§4), and two weeks later Vallejo is a prisoner of the Bear Flaggers at Sonoma. One battle has founders in the ranks: at Olompali on 24 June 1846 "a mixed Californio and Indigenous force including Elias and Caleb defeats a force of Bear Flaggers at serious cost" (§4, canon). The founding company is the force of Olompali, with Elias and Caleb in it.

**K2. What Olompali gives (Hist.).** The battle was fought at the adobe of Camilo Ynitia (1803–1856), headman of the Coast Miwok of Olompali and the only Native American on the northern frontier to secure a large Mexican land grant for his people, Rancho Olompali (1843); he sold most of it to James Black in 1852. Castro's men under Joaquín de la Torre came up from Monterey, about fifty, with some twenty irregulars. In canon they are the Republic's men, and they win.

**K3. Two founding companies at one battle.**
- **The open company: the Company of Olompali.** De la Torre's Californios, the irregulars and the Anglos riding with them, Elias and Caleb among them, raised by the Republic in its first month and open to any man who would fight for it.
- **The raised company: Ynitia's men.** The Coast Miwok of Olompali fight on their own ground under their own headman, beside the Republic's men and not in their ranks: the first raised company, a nation's, fifteen years before the Civil War.
- The Coast Miwok of Olompali are not the people of Elias's Mokelumne guide (CT-MIWOK-GUIDE, whose people, Miwok or Yokuts, stay OPEN); the two must not be merged.

**K4. Ancestors, not precedents.** Both companies fight at home, for the Republic's founding, before the venture licence; neither serves abroad, and neither is a mission (D13). The Act of 1888 claims them in its preamble and its roll:
- The roll of deeds opens with **the dead of Olompali**, by name, not with Elias (CR-049: lost companions are remembered by name).
- The Company of Olompali is never raised again; its colour is carried each year by the best open company at the sovereign's review (I8). Ynitia's colour is kept by the Coast Miwok and carried by the best raised company.
- The preamble's argument is the settlers' (H6.4): the Republic was founded by men no one examined.

**K5. The line from 1846 to 1888.** Olompali (1846) → the refusal of the volunteer expeditions (1851, CR-033) → Walker and the licence (1853–54) → Crabb refused and his backers tried (1857) → Shanghai (1860, the first company abroad) → the Act (1888).

**K6. Elias at Olompali.** Canon gives him the lesson: "winning loyalty by persuasion does not remove the consequences of taking up arms" (§4). The Suzhou article (D9) is that lesson made law. The Chronicler need not say so.

**K7. Caleb's colour (R-28, kept on purpose).** The colour of Olompali carries Caleb's name among the living of 1846. In 1888 it is honoured a year after Silas, the school's patron, killed Caleb at Donner under the Truce's public account. The irony is kept deliberately. The risk is real: each year's review puts Caleb's name in front of Silas's men, and anyone who knows what happened at Donner has a reason to look at the patron. The Chronicler states the ceremony and leaves the irony to the reader.

## M. Borneo (APPROVED: the rule of M2–M7 and shape (b), with Cowie; author, 11:51)

**M1. What canon has.** In 1878 Pacifica, holding Spain's rights over Sulu, takes the Sultan of Sulu's lease of North Borneo for a Pacifican Borneo company, chartered about 1881, in place of the British company; New Calamba (1892) stands on Pacifican-chartered land; Britain keeps Labuan, Sarawak and Brunei, and the border is the slow fuse of the Borneo question after 1900 (CR-029 A42, APPROVED; OPEN-015-08). The company governs its coast as the British company did historically. That is a Pacifican chartered company ruling peoples who signed nothing. The Sultan leased what he claimed, and the Kadazan-Dusun, Murut, Bajau and Suluk of the interior were never asked.

**M2. The rule (proposed).** The lease gives the company the coast, its stations and the Sultan's rights. It gives no interior land. Inland the company holds land, levies taxes and posts men only by compact with the peoples there, as the Northern Commission does in Alaska (CR-070) and the homelands require at home (D10). The constabulary guards the stations, the mails, the estates and New Calamba. It collects no tax on any people whose compact does not provide one, and it enforces nothing inland except a warrant for a named man.

**M3. Labour.** The tobacco boom of the 1880s brought indentured Chinese labour to North Borneo's estates (Hist.). Under the licence (D11) the Pacifican company's estates use paid labour only, recruited without brokers. Its tobacco pays less than Deli's, and its shareholders complain at every meeting.

**M4. Mat Salleh (Hist., 1894–1900).** Datu Muhammad Salleh, a chief of the Lingkabo district and the Sugut River, led the rising against the British company from 1894. The company's men burned his village and declared him wanted. He raided and burned the company's settlement on Gaya Island at midnight on 9 July 1897, and was defeated at Ranau in January 1898. In April 1898 William Clarke Cowie, the company's managing director, came from London and made peace with him at Palatan, letting him hold Tambunan. The company then set up its administration in Tambunan in breach of the agreement, fighting resumed at the end of 1899, and Mat Salleh was killed at Tambunan in January 1900. The rising went on after him for years (sources differ on its end; to verify).

**M5. Three shapes for Pacifica.**
- **(a) The rising that never grows.** In August 1895, at Jambongan (D-38), the company's resident orders Mat Salleh's village burned when he will not give himself up. The constabulary's captain refuses under the Suzhou article, since a reprisal on the unarmed is exactly what it forbids, and withdraws his men. The Companies Office confirms the withdrawal and fines the company. Mat Salleh stays a wanted man with a warrant out, his grievance about the trade passes and tolls goes to a hearing, and there is no Gaya. *For:* it is the Act's first test abroad and the article works. *Against:* the story loses a rising.
- **(b) The peace kept (APPROVED).** The burning is refused as in (a), but the grievance stands, and in July 1897 Mat Salleh burns Gaya anyway. The constabulary pursues the men who burned it under warrant and fights where it is fought, as at Bear River in 1863 (canon CR-060: warrants, a limited engagement, the lodges untouched). It burns no village and takes no hostages. In April 1898 Cowie makes the Palatan peace. When the company's board moves to put its station in Tambunan in 1899, the Companies Office holds it to the agreement on pain of its licence. The peace becomes a registered compact, and Tambunan a compact land under Mat Salleh's people. He dies in his own time (OPEN). *For:* the story keeps the rising, the Act shows its teeth against a Pacifican company's own board, and the historical breach of faith becomes the turn that does not happen. *Against:* a happier outcome than history, which must be earned on the page by the 1897 raid and its dead.
- **(c) The breach and the refusal.** As (b), but the board breaks the Palatan peace in 1899 and orders the constabulary to Tambunan. The captain refuses to serve a commander who breaks his word to a surrendered enemy, the nearest case to Suzhou, and takes his company out. Another company goes in, and Mat Salleh dies as he did historically. *For:* the article's hardest test. *Against:* the Office must then be seen to fail, or the board to defy it, which weakens the Act in its first decade.

**M6. What the Act requires in any shape.** No campaign of extermination (protected intent); no reprisal, no burned village, no hostages (D9); no land inland without a compact (D10); and no Tausug company of the dependency sent against Suluk or Bajau kin (I3). The constabulary's mixed ranks matter: Pacifican, Filipino from 1888, and local men under their own headmen where a compact provides.

**M7. New Calamba (1892).** The land for the colony of the Calamba evictees is bought by compact from its people before the company grants it, which makes New Calamba the first compact land of the Borneo interior. It is guarded by the Philippine companies (F8), and Rizal's self-government there stands on a title no one can call conquest.

**M8. People.** **William Clarke Cowie** (Hist.; a Labuan trader and gun-runner to Sulu before he joined the British company) is the Pacifican company's man and, by 1898, its managing director (APPROVED). The captain of 1895 is OPEN (invented, or a Filipino officer of the dependency's company).

**M9. Storyline text** (§25, after Unsan; also in Part N; the integrated text moves the refusal to August 1895 at Jambongan, D-38, table S):
> <!-- CR-074: Borneo --> **[PROPOSED — CR-074]** In North Borneo the Pacifican company holds its coast by the Sultan's lease and its interior only by compact. In 1894, when its resident orders a Sugut chief's village burned, the constabulary's captain refuses under the Act and the Companies Office upholds him.
