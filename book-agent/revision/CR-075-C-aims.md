## C0. The claim in four sentences

1. The Republic's objects in the war are written down in July 1861, as the preamble of the Council's war resolution, and printed beside the reply and the seven reasons; the reply is what it asks of Lincoln that summer, the preamble is what it will take for peace.
2. There are five objects, each first wanted by a named party: the Council's ground (no American arms on the coast), Vallejo's (an equal treaty in place of 1848), Hawaii's (no search of its ships), Elias's and the merchants' (no people handed back), and California's condition (no army east).
3. Three of the five ask nothing that a Union fighting the South could not give, and the preamble ends with a promise that makes the rest believable: the Republic will treat on these objects at any time and ask nothing beyond them, and what it keeps it will pay for.
4. The aims do not grow. Every later step of the war is taken under one of them by a resolution of its own, and the Terms of 1863 and the treaty of 1865 are the aims priced and drawn on a map. Where Elias acts without that, his own people say so.

## C1. The papers of July 1861 and where the preamble sits

The Republic has four instruments for its war by the end of July 1861. Only the fourth is new.

| Paper | Date | Adopted by | What it says | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Article XIV and the Protectorship | May; in force 1 June 1861 | The Council, with both member governments' assent | Elias Protector for one year | canon (§9; C-1861-PROTECTOR) |
| The May resolution | May 1861 | The Council | Limited coercive operations; civilian protections; no forced allegiance; no annexation by emergency decree; renewed negotiations when adequate redress becomes possible | canon (§9) |
| The reply and the seven reasons | Early July 1861 | Laid before the Council, concurred in, sent to Baker, printed | Three demands of Washington; seven reasons | APPROVED (CR-068 G2, PP3, PP3b) |
| **The war resolution, with its preamble** | Early July 1861, the day of the concurrence | The Council | The preamble: the five objects, the price, the promise to treat. The articles: the mandate (theatre, limits, Honolulu's defence) | PROPOSED; the resolution as the army's mandate is CR-067 A1 |

- C1.1 **Why a preamble, and why in the resolution.** CR-067 makes the war resolution the army's mandate: an operation beyond it, "a new theatre, an annexation, a change of war aim", needs a new resolution. A mandate that changes when the war aim changes needs a written war aim, or nobody can tell whether it has changed. The preamble supplies it. It also answers the May resolution's last clause: the Council is bound to negotiate "when adequate redress becomes possible", and in June Lincoln offered redress (CR-068 G2). The preamble says what *adequate* means. Lincoln's offer paid for the man; the objects say what would have to change for the Republic to stand down.
- C1.2 **The reply and the preamble are different papers.** The reply asks for three things now, as the price of standing down the expedition, and Elias tells Baker he does not expect them to be granted (G2). The preamble says what the Republic will settle the war for. The difference is the point. The reply asks Lincoln for things he cannot grant in a war, and the preamble asks for things he could grant at a peace. Elias tells Baker both (outline C6).
- C1.3 **The articles of the resolution** gather what canon already has as the limits of July: the mandate for one year, the May resolution read in, no column east of the mountains, the sailor's compensation accepted whatever else is refused, and Honolulu's batteries, two frigates and the second turret ship (G2). The theatre is the Union's posts and stations on the Columbia and the Sound (operations: CR-059; canon §10). Part C does not redraw any of these. It adds the preamble above them and a reading of "the mountains" (C7.3).
- C1.4 **Publication (C-2, APPROVED).** The preamble is printed with the reply and the seven reasons in English, Spanish and Hawaiian, as every confederal act is (canon §8). It goes east with Baker's dispatch, so Congress recognizes a state of war before 6 August knowing what Pacifica says it is fighting for (sequence: CR-068 G2). Lincoln's message of 4 July, written before any of it, names no enemy (PP2). That week the two governments each publish a paper: Lincoln's names no enemy and is about the Union, and the Republic's names its enemy and its objects and nothing else.

## C2. The preamble (draft text)

Draft text, PROPOSED (question C-5). It is short because it is meant to be quoted. The wording follows the approved table (outline C), with the changes in C3 marked there.

> **The Council of the Confederation of Pacifica**, having laid before both member governments the offer of the President of the United States, the Protector's reply and the reasons given with it, and having concurred in the reply, declares the objects of the Confederation in this war, so that its friends and its enemy may know what it fights for and on what terms it will make peace.
>
> **First.** That no naval station, storehouse, garrison or fortified post of the United States remain on the Pacific slope from which the Confederation or either of its members can be struck.
>
> **Second.** That the convention of 1848, and the articles renewed at Carson in 1860, give way to a treaty between equals, and that the frontier between the two nations be drawn by their consent.
>
> **Third.** That the ships of the Confederation and of the Hawaiian Kingdom pass freely in their own waters, and that no foreign warship stop or search them there.
>
> **Fourth.** That the Confederation take no people by decree; that it give up to no government any people of the Pacific slope who have chosen to trade or associate with it and can govern themselves; and that it protect those who are punished for having done so.
>
> **Fifth.** That the Confederation send no army east and seek no conquest on this continent, and that it lend to any American people that holds its own ground.
>
> What the Confederation keeps at the peace it will pay for. It will treat whenever the United States will treat on these objects, and it will ask nothing beyond them.

## C3. The five objects

Each object below has its wording, whose it is first, why, what it does not ask, what would meet it, who reads it otherwise, and how it ends. The research basis is the outline's C8; it is cited here, not repeated.

### C3.1 First: no American arms on the Pacific slope

- **Whose:** the Council's stated ground of concurrence (canon §9): the American squadron's last Pacific stations are the Columbia and the Sound. Reason 2 is the argument; this is the object.
- **What it means.** In July 1861 the squadron is six wooden sloops whose cruise runs from Panama to Alaska and through Hawaiian waters (HUNT 301–302). With California's harbours closed and Sausalito sealed (canon §9), what it has left on the coast is anchorages without forts: in our world there were no fortifications at all in Oregon or Washington, because every federal dollar had gone to San Francisco Bay (HUNT 234). The first object asks that this remain so for good: no station, no storehouse, no garrison and no fortified post on the slope.
- **Sausalito.** The storehouse is not named, because it is already sealed and its stores interned (canon §9); it falls with the convention (second object). The treaty grants "no storehouse … anywhere" (CR-062), which meets both objects at once.
- **What it does not ask:** the Union's trade, its merchants on the river or its ships in Pacifican ports. Warships may come in for refuge or repair (CR-062) because the object concerns bases and says nothing about visits.
- **What would meet it:** the evacuation of the last post on the slope. In 1867 that is Port Townsend (CR-062).
- **Who reads it otherwise:** Washington reads it as the loss of the Northwest. Elias does not deny it; it is reason 2's arithmetic (G2).
- **How it ends:** met in 1867, when Port Townsend is evacuated after the exchange (outline G).
- **The reading of "the Pacific slope" (C-3, APPROVED; boundary PROPOSED, C-6).** In the preamble the slope is the country between the ocean and the crests of the Sierra Nevada and the Cascades: California, the Willamette, the lower Columbia and the Sound. The posts beyond the crests (The Dalles, Walla Walla, Colville; Fort Churchill in Washoe; Fort Bridger; Fort Union in New Mexico) are not named in the first object. Canon already treats them that way: in October 1861 the posts east of the Cascades are cut off from the sea and ordered to march out by the Mullan Road, and they go east under the Walla Walla convention of July 1862 without an assault (canon §10). Fort Union's evacuation in 1867 follows from the line drawn by consent (second object) and New Mexico's compact (fourth), not from the first (to adjust in Part G; C9.3).

### C3.2 Second: an equal treaty in place of 1848

- **Whose:** Vallejo's. He signed the convention in 1848 and refused Buchanan's guarantee because "a guarantor has a right to come in" (canon §4). In 1865 he signs its end (CR-062). The object is his before it is the Council's, and he moves it at the open vote (outline I1).
- **What it ends:** the articles that came out of the naval confrontation of 1846–48: San Francisco, Monterey and San Diego open to American warships; the revocable storehouse; California's ports closed to the enemies of the United States in its wars; the return of deserters; the renunciation of the Basin pending a boundary commission (§4). Carson renewed the commercial articles in 1860 (§8). It also ends the most-favoured-nation clause and the whalers' privileges as obligations owed to one power.
- **What it offers in their place:** a treaty between equals and a frontier drawn by consent. The Republic does not promise in 1861 where the line will run. That waits on the fourth object.
- **Why in 1861 (E2, APPROVED).** Baker's note of May invokes the renewed articles: California is to close its ports to the insurgents' ships and agents and return deserters. Elias and Vallejo answer that the article covers the enemies of the United States "in its wars", and Washington calls the South a rebellion. The Union's own position in 1861 was to deny that a war existed while claiming a belligerent's rights (JONES ch.1–2). The device cuts one way only, and Elias says so to Baker (E2). What the note teaches California is the object: every article of 1848 is a question Washington can ask and Monterey has to answer. Before May the convention was an old bargain, and after the note it is a leash.
- **The pledge of 1848 not to alienate (C-9).** The convention also pledged California never to cede, lease or place under protection any part of its territory to a European power (§4). The second object does not attack that pledge. It ends with the convention in 1865 (CR-039), and the treaty is silent on it (CR-062). PROPOSED: in the open vote Vallejo says the Republic keeps the rule as its own and owes it to no one. It gives the same pledge to Russia for Alaska in 1867 (CR-062 K4), and that is the precedent made good. The Republic gives the pledge freely to a seller and will not owe it to a neighbour.
- **What would meet it:** the peace declaring the conventions of 1848 and 1860 ended by the war, and the Western line in place of the renunciation of the Basin (CR-039; CR-062 T8).
- **Who reads it otherwise:** to Washington the convention was the price of independence (CR-028: Washington took independence for the Texas road), and ending it is a breach. Seward's party called 1848 the Trist surrender (§9). Both readings are fair. The object says the bargain was struck under the guns of 1846, and the second object is the Republic's answer to the first.
- **How it ends:** met in 1865 to 1867.

### C3.3 Third: free passage in Pacific waters

- **Whose:** Hawaii's, by name. Victoria's party insists that it stand as an object of its own, apart from the first (that was C-1's alternative, refused). Her argument is F1 turned round (F-2, APPROVED). In our world Hawaii's foreign minister told Washington in 1861 that the king had no force to make his neutrality respected and needed an American warship at Honolulu to protect the whalers (KUYK 65–66). In Pacifica the American warship that would protect Honolulu is the one that boarded its packet. Her delegation's reasons are the first and third of the seven (G2).
- **What it means.** "Their own waters" is the reply's "Pacifican and Hawaiian waters" (§9), the seas between the islands and between the islands and the coast, where the packets run. The minutes define them (D-a): Victoria and Wyllie give the packet routes, and Sherman and Vallejo note for the record that the law of nations recognizes a nation's waters only to a marine league from its shore. Beyond that, stopping a neutral to search it was a belligerent's right in war (JONES ch.2), and in peace a right of search existed only by treaty (JONES ch.4). So the object claims more than the law of nations gives, and the Council knows it. It is written as a political object, not as a legal claim.
- **Why it cannot be granted in war, and need not be granted at peace.** The Union cannot give up search while it blockades (G2, reason 1). Once the Republic is a belligerent, its ships are enemy ships, so in the war the object is moot. It looks to the peace and to the next American war in which the Republic is neutral. The peace ends the belligerent right, and after 1867 the Union has no station on the slope from which to send a cruiser (first object).
- **What would meet it (CT2, Case A, APPROVED):** the peace and the first object together. The treaty is silent on search, and the schoolbook does not claim an article that does not exist.
- **Hawaii's own law of the sea.** Hawaii acceded in 1855 to the Russo-American principles, free ships making free goods (KUYK 63–65). Those principles limit what a belligerent may seize. They do not forbid search. Hawaii's position on paper is therefore narrower than its object, and Wyllie says so. Victoria's answer is that the object is about Hawaiian ships, not the cargoes on them.
- **Who reads it otherwise:** Washington reads it as a claim to close the Pacific. London (Lyons's circle, Wyllie's correspondents) reads it as a new mare clausum and says nothing while the Union is the one searching.
- **How it ends:** met by the peace (outline G).

### C3.4 Fourth: the West's peoples by their own choice

- **Whose:** Elias's, with the merchants of Portland and Astoria behind it (reason 5) and the reply's second and third demands as its first form (§9).
- **Its three clauses.** (1) *No people by decree*: the May resolution's "no annexation by emergency decree" (§9), raised from a limit on the Protector into an object of the Republic. (2) *None given up*: any people of the slope who have chosen to trade or associate with the Republic, and can govern themselves, will not be handed back at the peace. (3) *Protection*: the Republic protects those who are punished for their choice, as reason 5's merchants are when they are named to the marshal as disloyal (G2).
- **"Can govern themselves" (wording changed from the table's "can hold its own ground"; C-5).** The clause means a people with a body that can speak for it, such as a convention, a legislature, a territorial assembly or a council of a nation. That is the test every later instrument meets: Oregon's compact with Whiteaker in the chair (CR-059), Deseret's articles with Young's counsellors (CR-060), the Rio Grande compact opened by the territorial assembly at Santa Fe (CR-061), Washoe's legislature and county officials (CR-060). The table's phrase "can hold its own ground" is kept for the fifth object, where it means a people with its own government and army. Using one phrase for two tests would let the Union say the Republic means to hold ground for the peoples it takes in.
- **Beyond the slope (C-3, APPROVED).** The object promises nothing to the Basin or the Rockies. A people there that asks for the Republic's association is answered by the Council, by a resolution of its own (C7). Deseret's initiative of 1862 is the first case (CR-060).
- **E6 (a), California's price (APPROVED, Part E).** No associated land becomes a member without the consent of both founding states. The fourth object says the Republic will not hand peoples back. It does not say they will vote in its Council. That is what lets Burch's Californians and Victoria's Hawaiians both accept it.
- **What would meet it:** at the peace, every associated people keeps its choice, and every resident of a ceded district chooses his citizenship and keeps his property, courts and civil protection without distinction of colour (CR-062).
- **Who reads it otherwise.** Lincoln reads it as a programme of dismemberment and says so in 1863 (outline I3), and his reading is fair (C6). Oregon reads it three ways (E8): Whiteaker's neutrals as a promise to be left alone, Nesmith's Unionists as a threat, Lane's Southern men as too little.
- **How it ends:** met, with Washoe as the exception the Chronicler records. Washoe was retaken by arms in 1864 under the first reservation of 1863, and its people did not ask (W4; C7.2).

### C3.5 Fifth: no conquest on this continent

- **Whose:** California's condition before it is Elias's rule (E4). About as many Southern-born as Northern-born men lived in the state, and neither half would fight its native section (ELL 183). The Republic can fight only a war that sends neither half east. In canon it is "his own first reason and Hawaii's last" (G2, reason 7), and Victoria's party enters it as the Protector's own.
- **Its three parts.** No army east, which is the articles' "no column east of the mountains" stated as an object (C7.3). No conquest on this continent, which covers the Republic's own war and the Union's: the Republic "will not see one American people conquered by another, because it would be next" (G2). And credit to any American people that holds its own ground, which is the Richmond credit of 1863, "lent for independence" (CR-068 G3, G4).
- **What it does not ask:** anything of Washington. It is a rule for the Republic, and it says nothing about the South's cause or slavery. The Republic forbids slavery on its own soil and does not reform a foreign country with its treasury (CR-068 G4).
- **What would meet it:** the Republic's own conduct. No Pacifican army goes east. The credit is lent and repaid in gold. Richmond is recognized in May 1865, and recognition is "no term of this peace" (Terms, 1863).
- **Who reads it otherwise.** Richmond reads it as a promise of an ally and is disappointed; no army comes. Union men in California read it as cover for the South, and Starr King preaches that from his pulpit (P21). The Chronicler (W4) records its cost: the South held, and four million were not examined (CR-068 G4).
- **How it ends:** kept, at the cost W4 records.

## C4. The price

- C4.1 **The sentence.** "What the Confederation keeps at the peace it will pay for." No sum is named, and nothing is said about what will be kept. The price is announced in 1861 so that it can be paid in 1865 without looking like a ransom. It is the seed of the third head of 1863 (the public domain paid for in gold) and of the $40 million in six instalments (CR-062).
- C4.2 **How he knows that a price will be taken (D4).** Ralston and the houses' New York correspondents know what New York lost when the treasure steamers stopped (CR-069; the bankers' memorial of January 1862 confirms it later). The Calculation Office knows what a Treasury paying in paper will need in coin (P6). Elias's own rule, B2, is to argue from capability: the Republic can pay, and a Union that is paid is not conquered.
- C4.3 **Who doubts it.** Burch: why pay for what Washington never earned? Hawaii's delegation: who pays, and in what share? That is left to the peace; the vendor credit of 1867 is the answer (ledger C-1867-VENDOR-CREDIT; MODEL rows in the outline's Model note). Sherman: a price named in 1861 will be raised in 1865 by whoever is winning. The promise in the next sentence answers him.
- C4.4 **The promise to treat (C-10).** "It will treat whenever the United States will treat on these objects, and it will ask nothing beyond them." It is the May resolution's "renewed negotiations when adequate redress becomes possible" turned into a standing offer. It is also the first form of the Terms' "neither raised nor lowered by the fortunes of the war" (1863). Elias makes the offer again in *Two Houses* (an armistice and commissioners) and in 1864, and it is accepted only in 1865.

## C5. Why five, and why this order

- C5.1 **Why not one.** A single object, security, would make 1865 look like loot: everything held in 1867 would have to be called security (outline C6). A territorial object ("the Pacific slope to the mountains") would break the May resolution and repeat the Washoe defeat of 1860 at once, because Victoria's coalition beat Article XIV on exactly that ground (canon §8).
- C5.2 **Why not fewer (C-1, APPROVED).** Folding the third into the first loses Hawaii's object by name. Leaving the fifth outside as a rule loses California's condition, and then the half of California that will not fight the South has nothing in writing.
- C5.3 **The order.** First comes the Council's ground, because it is the reason it concurred. Second and third are the two member governments' own objects, California's and then Hawaii's, in the order of the Articles. Fourth is Elias's, because it is the one he has to defend. Fifth is the rule that binds all the others. Burch's motion (C6) would have put a territorial object first. The order is the Council's answer to it before Elias gives his.
- C5.4 **What three of them cost Washington.** The third is met by the peace itself. The fifth asks nothing of the Union. The second asks for a new treaty that a Union at peace could sign without losing anything except what it gained by the guns of 1846. The first and the fourth cost it the Northwest, and they are the ones the war is fought over. Elias tells Baker this when he hands him the reply (outline C6): the reply asks more than Lincoln can grant (G2), and the preamble says what the Republic will settle for.

## C6. Burch's motion and Elias's answer (C-4, APPROVED)

- C6.1 **The man (Hist.).** John Chilton Burch was born on 1 February 1826 in Boone County, Missouri, across the river from Franklin, so he is a Boonslick man two years younger than Elias (canon §1). He studied law at Jefferson City and was assistant adjutant general of Missouri. He came to California in 1850 and worked in the mines for a year. He was clerk, then district attorney, of the new Trinity County, sat in the state assembly in 1856 and the state senate after it, and was one of the two pro-Southern Democrats California sent to the House in 1859 (Biographical Directory of the United States Congress; WAITE 119). His letter of 22 November 1860 proposed a Republic of the Pacific of California, Oregon, New Mexico, Washington and Utah (ELL 181). He spoke at Weaverville to Trinity's Democrats and "Compromise Union men" on 25 May 1861 (WAITE 315). He practised law in San Francisco after the war and died there in 1885.
- C6.2 **In Pacifica (PROPOSED; seat OPEN, C-8).** There is no House seat for him. Recommended: in 1859, the year he went to Congress in our world, Trinity sends him to the Confederal Assembly, which can revoke the Protector's emergency measures (ledger C-1861-PROTECTOR). He is a Southern-rights Democrat in a republic at war with the Union, and that makes him the government's ally for the first time in his life. He and Elias did not know each other in Missouri. Elias left as a boy, and Burch knows the name only from the Franklin that the river took.
- C6.3 **The motion (July 1861).** The Assembly sits while the Council deliberates. Burch moves an address to the Council asking it to name the Rockies line as the Republic's frontier, as Gwin and Latham drew it in our world (ELL 180), and the five members of his letter as its object. His arguments are his letter's and the separatists': the Union will never be weaker; what is not named now will be refused later; and California's Southern men will fight for a republic of the West but not for a river. The address is referred to the Council.
- C6.4 **The answer in the closed sitting (D3; sealed).** What comes by its own choice need not be named, and what is named will be called conquest. The interior follows its supplies (D3), and the Republic does not need to claim what will come to it. A named frontier would be a war aim that the May resolution forbids. Hawaii would refuse it, as it refused Article XIV over Washoe in 1860, and the Union would use it in every paper it sends to London. Elias adds what he can say only in a closed room: Burch's republic would be California swallowed by the interior, and E6 (a) is California's protection against exactly that.
- C6.5 **The answer in public.** The fourth object, with its narrow wording, is the Council's reply to the address. The reason for the wording stays in the sealed minutes until the Chronicler opens them.
- C6.6 **What Burch does then (PROPOSED).** He votes the war supply and against the wording. He is right about one thing, that the Union will refuse later what is not named now, and wrong about the remedy. By 1867 the Republic holds very nearly the republic of his letter: the five he named, within lines close to those the territories had in 1860 (New Mexico's eastern line was already 103°W, and Utah's and Washington's ran to the divide). It got there by the fourth object, not by his. The Chronicler sets the letter of 1860 beside the map of 1867 and lets the reader draw the conclusion.
- C6.7 **Lincoln's use of him (B5).** In 1863 Lincoln's public letter can set Burch's address, printed in the Assembly's journal, beside the fourth object, and he is fair to do so. A Pacifican legislator asked for the Rockies in July 1861, and the Republic got most of them by 1867. Elias does not answer that the address lost. He answers with the Terms: the line will be drawn by consent and paid for. The sealed minutes would prove the reason but not the result, and Elias does not publish them.

## C7. The aims do not grow

- C7.1 **The rule (CR-067 A1).** An operation beyond the war resolution needs a new resolution. PROPOSED: every new resolution names the object it serves. That is the whole mechanism. The aims are checked by the Council's own record, not by Elias's restraint, and Sherman has a written limit to hold him to (E5).
- C7.2 **The later instruments and the objects they cite:**

| Instrument | Date | Object cited | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Walla Walla convention | July 1862 | First (the slope's posts) and fourth (protection) | The regulars beyond the Cascades go east with honours (canon §10) |
| The Rio Grande resolution | April 1862 | Fourth (protection for the valley's people who trade with the Republic) and fifth (with Richmond's forces, treat and do not fight) | A new theatre, voted under the limits of 1861 (CR-061 C0a). The weakest citation: the valley is beyond the slope and nobody in it has asked. Sherman says so, and Victoria's party records it as it recorded its doubt of the fourth object. The assembly's compact of 1863 makes it good after the fact; the Council's unspoken reason, to be in the valley before Texas's claim (CR-061 C0a), stays unspoken |
| The invited guard on the Salt Lake road | October 1862 | None | Elias's own act without a vote; Sherman and Victoria say it needed one (CR-067 A1; CR-060). The exception that proves the rule |

- C7.3 **"No column east of the mountains" (G2), read (C-7).** The limit of July 1861 is the fifth object's "no army east". PROPOSED: "the mountains" are the Rockies' front ranges, and "east" is the plains, the Union's own ground toward the States. The Rio Grande valley lies between the ranges, and the column of 1862 needed its own resolution because it was a new theatre (CR-061 C0a), not because it crossed that line. No Pacifican column goes onto the plains in the war. Fort Union, on the plains' edge, is not attacked and is evacuated only after the exchange (CR-062). The roads from Fort Benton, Denver and Raton stay open by treaty (CR-062).
- C7.4 **What the rule does not prevent.** The map grows inside the objects. Between 1861 and 1865 the Republic comes to hold far more than the Columbia and the Sound (outline A2), and every step has to be argued as a choice made by a people, as protection, or as the first reservation. The Chronicler can count them, and he does (W1: "the word held"; W4: Washoe).

## C8. The open vote

Canon records the concurrence and Victoria's party's reservations on the reasons (G2). PROPOSED: the vote on the preamble follows the closed sitting the same day (D-a).
- **Vallejo** moves the second object and speaks for the whole on California's behalf (I1; E2).
- **Victoria's party** votes the whole. It enters, as it did for the sixth reason, its doubt of the fourth: a promise to peoples Hawaii has never seen, made in Hawaii's name (I1; B6). The doubt is recorded and is the ground she uses at every renewal.
