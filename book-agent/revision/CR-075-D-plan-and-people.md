## D0. The claim in four sentences

1. Elias does not make the plan of 1861 alone. In the days after Lincoln's offer arrives, he calls the people who know each part to two closed sittings of the Council. They state what is established, what is conjecture and what would settle it, and Sherman, as the appointed respondent, attacks every part.
2. What comes out is an estimate in seven parts. Each has a name against it: the Union's sequence (Sherman), the West's distance (Ericsson, Judah, Marcus, the Calculation Office, Beale), the interior's market (Huntington, the Assay Office, Hays, Beale, Hunt), the coin (Ralston), the Union's calendar (Broderick, John Sherman's letters), the fallback (Marcus, the Office), and what Elias himself adds from his own life.
3. The respondent wins his main point. A limited war stays limited only if its objects are written down so that they can be met, and that is why the preamble of Part C has the form it has.
4. The estimate is right in its direction and wrong in five particulars. Each error is traced: two are his own, two were judgment calls by the men who knew best, and one, the strength of the Union's paper money, nobody in the room foresaw.

## D2. The form: a challenge evening used for war (D-1, APPROVED)

- D2.1 **The custom.** At the Laboratory a result goes to a challenge evening before it goes to the journal. An appointed respondent attacks it, and the evening distinguishes what is established from what is conjecture and from what would settle it (§8; CR-049; the custom as CR-050 shows it in 1908). In 1861 the Laboratory is two years old (1859) and the custom older, from Genth's school. Most of the Council have never attended one. Elias has, many times.
- D2.2 **The rules as adapted (PROPOSED):**
  1. Each part is given by the person who knows it, in his own words, and his paper is entered in the minutes.
  2. Each part states three things: what is established (with its source), what is conjecture, and what would settle it, and when.
  3. The respondent is appointed in advance and given the papers a day before.
  4. Elias asks questions, puts the parts together, and says for each case what the Republic will do. He does not give a part himself except his own (D3.7).
  5. Nothing is decided. The decisions are taken on the Council's night, in the Council's ordinary form.
  6. The minutes are sealed. The Chronicler has them (outline D-a).
- D2.3 **Who is in the room (D-6, APPROVED).**
  - *The Council:* both member delegations.
  - *In full:* Marcus Vance (P2) and Sherman (P1), who as Inspector-General reports to the Council (CR-067).
  - *For their own parts only:* Ralston (P5), Huntington (P4) and Ericsson (P3). Each comes in, gives his part, answers the respondent and leaves.
  - *By paper, read in:*
    - the Calculation Office (Sylvester, P6);
    - Judah (P7);
    - Beale for himself and Hays (P10);
    - Hunt from San Bernardino (P10);
    - Broderick (P11);
    - the Assay Office's returns and the Portland merchants' letters (P12);
    - Wyllie's letter, read by Hawaii's delegation (P9).
  - *Not in the room, by design:*
    - Baker (P13);
    - Halleck, who has gone (P14);
    - Burch, whose address is read and answered (Part C, C6);
    - Starr King, whose sermons are on the table (Part E, E6.4).
- D2.4 **Why this is Elias's strength.** §8 says he directs money more readily than he can command troops. His skill is knowing whom to ask and how to test what he is told. The sitting shows it without telling it. He speaks less than anyone else at the table and asks the questions the Council will be asked later by its own voters.

## D3. The estimate, part by part

Each part gives the claim, then who gives it and what he brings (*how he knows*), then the respondent's attack and what Elias says the Republic will do. The outline's table D-c has the summary. The research is cited, not repeated.

### D3.1 The Union's sequence (D1). First sitting.

- **Claim.** The Union cannot fight two wars and will not try. Its war is a sequence, the South first and then the West, and it will keep the West cheap. The Republic's task is to make the West unwinnable cheaply and the South unwinnable soon.
- **Given by:** Sherman, before he turns respondent. He knows the old army's officers as men, from West Point and the service, and he has his brother John's letters from Washington up to the spring (P1; John is senator from 1861, SHER 38). His judgment of 1861 in our world serves him here without Louisiana (SHER 106–120):
  - secession means war, and the war will be long and huge;
  - a free-trade South would undersell the Eastern ports, so the North must blockade;
  - the first volunteer armies will fail, and their leaders will be cast aside;
  - the Union must keep its cause separate from slavery, or the war will have no end.
- **With him:**
  - *Wyllie's letter:* the South will hold, and its belligerency should be recognized (KUYK 65; P9). Hawaii's delegation reads it with a note that the king refused recognition.
  - *Seward's foreign war:* Lincoln refused to make war on Monterey that spring (canon §9). The Union's President has already chosen the sequence once.
- **Established:** the blockade proclaimed; Lincoln's refusal of Seward; the three-month men before Washington.
- **Conjecture:** that the South holds for two years.
- **What would settle it:** the first battle in Virginia. Sherman says it will be lost by whoever attacks first with raw men, and Bull Run is three weeks away.
- **The respondent's attack (Sherman against himself, by custom).** If the Union wins quickly in Virginia, it will not need two wars. If it loses, it will build the largest army in the hemisphere and will not stop at the South. Either way the West is next. His forecast of the war's length is the strongest argument for acting now, and he knows it: it is reason 4 before Elias states it.
- **What the Republic will do:** act in July, while the Union has no army on the Pacific; send no army east (the fifth object); lend to the South when it holds its own ground (from 1863, CR-068 G3–G4).
- **Right** (CR-068 G3).

### D3.2 The West's distance (D2). First sitting.

- **Claim.** The West cannot be reinforced cheaply. The sea road through Panama must pass the turret ships, and the overland road crosses a thousand miles of plains and passes that the Union does not supply. What reaches the Sound comes in regiments, not armies.
- **Given by:**
  - *Marcus (P2)*, on what an army needs to move and be fed;
  - *Ericsson (P3)*, on what the Union's yards can build and how fast. The Navy Department refused his armoured ship in 1854, and the Union has no turret ship (CR-068 G3);
  - *the Calculation Office (P6)*, on Panama's tonnage from the shipping registers;
  - *Judah's paper (P7)*, on the passes and their seasons;
  - *Beale's paper (P10)*, on which roads cannot be used. In our world he told the Union's commander that the Texas expedition by way of Mexico was impracticable (HUNT 22).
- **Established:**
  - the Pacific squadron is six wooden sloops (HUNT 301–302);
  - there is no fort on the Columbia or the Sound (HUNT 234, 304);
  - the regulars in the Northwest are few and thinly posted (HUNT 26, 220–226).
- **Conjecture:**
  - how many regiments the Union will send, and when;
  - how long until it has turret ships of its own.
- **What would settle it:** the first troopships at Panama.
- **The respondent's attack.** The two men giving the part disagree. Ericsson says the Union's engine works, which he knows (P3), can build armour as soon as someone gives them a design, and that the Union will find a designer. Marcus says that whatever it builds it will not send round the Horn while it blockades. Sherman sides with Ericsson on the ships and with Marcus on the men. When and whether the Union builds turret ships of its own is left to canon (CR-068 G3: it has none at Hampton Roads).
- **What the Republic will do:**
  - take the river's mouth before any regiment can arrive;
  - put the first turret ship on the river, not at sea (canon §10);
  - keep the second for Honolulu, which is Hawaii's price (CR-068 G2).
- **Right for the war's length.** The four Iowa regiments are what Marcus predicted (canon §10).

### D3.3 The interior's market (D3). Second sitting.

- **Claim.** The interior follows its supplies. The camps of Washoe, and those of Idaho and Montana when they come, and the Saints at Salt Lake are fed from Sacramento, Portland and San Francisco and paid in Californian coin. Once the Columbia is held and the Union's war has cut the overland roads, the interior has to choose its market. The fourth object tells it that the Republic will not force the choice.
- **Given by:**
  - *Huntington (P4)*, from his house's books: what Sacramento's stores send over the Sierra and what the camps pay for it in;
  - *the Assay Office's returns (P12)*: how much of the interior's dust is coined at San Francisco;
  - *Beale's paper and Hays's (P10)*: the Basin and its nations;
  - *Hunt's letter (P10)*: Young's mind;
  - *de la Guerra (P8)*: New Mexico and its Spanish-speaking towns.
- **Established:** the Northwest's men will not enlist for paper while private firms pay in coin (HUNT 227–228); the Saints gave the Union almost nothing (WAITE 174).
- **Conjecture:** that a market decides allegiance.
- **What would settle it:** the first winter of the war on the posts east of the Cascades. When it comes, they have no flour to buy, and the valley sells on Portland's credit (canon §10).
- **The respondent's attack, led by two of the part's own authors.**
  - *Beale:* the nations of the road are not a market. They will make their own terms, and the Union's broken treaties are a fact the Republic must not use dishonestly (HUNT 232–233).
  - *Hunt:* Young waits on events, expects the nation to bleed itself, and will not be commanded (WAITE 174).
  - *Sherman:* a market that decides allegiance can be bought back.
- **What the Republic will do:**
  - pay in coin for everything (as the Rio Grande column later does, CR-061);
  - post its prices;
  - forbid agents in Oregon (canon, CR-059);
  - answer any people that asks, by a resolution of its own (Part C, C7).
- **Right in direction, wrong in method.** The Saints come on their own terms (D5).

### D3.4 The coin (D4). Second sitting.

- **Claim.** The coast's gold stops going east. A Union paying in paper will in the end need gold more than it needs the West, and the price should be named in advance so that it can be paid.
- **Given by:**
  - *Ralston (P5)*: New York's gold position through Eugene Kelly & Co., the New York house of Donohoe, Ralston & Co. (LAV 149–151; CR-069);
  - *the Calculation Office (P6)*, on the Union's Treasury reports;
  - *the Reserve Bank's governor (name OPEN)*, on the cover.
- **Established:** the coast sent New York about forty millions in gold a year (canon, the bankers' memorial of 1862, CR-069). Nothing replaces it.
- **Conjecture:** that the Union will suspend coin payments within a year, and that a Treasury paying in paper cannot pay for long. Everyone at the table, Ralston and the Office included, believes the second (D5).
- **What would settle it:** the New York banks' coin at the end of the year. In canon they suspend at the end of December (§9).
- **The respondent's attack, which is Ralston's.** The houses will keep their coin at home only if they are asked, not ordered. An export ban would send the coin to London by every private route and make the houses the Republic's enemies. Nobody needs the principle that summer: the treasure steamers stop in July (CR-069), and the coin stays home without being asked. The minutes record it as Ralston's rule. In 1862 the question comes back in a new form, coin leaking to London by private routes, and Elias reaches for an export ban. Ralston applies his own rule of 1861 to it and brings the houses' word instead: the Steamer Day Agreement (canon, CR-069). The scene of 1862 keeps its discovery. What Elias learns there is how the rule works in practice.
- **What the Republic will do:** announce the price with the objects (Part C, C4). Sherman objects that a price named in 1861 will be raised in 1865 by whoever is winning. The promise to treat on the objects and ask nothing beyond them is Elias's answer to him (Part C, C4.4).
- **Right** (CR-069). Ralston's method is used, not Elias's.

### D3.5 The Union's calendar (D5). Second sitting.

- **Claim.** The Union holds elections in wartime. If the South holds until November 1864, a Union government may treat. The Republic must have its terms in print by then and ask nothing more afterwards.
- **Given by:**
  - *Broderick's paper (P11; D-3, APPROVED)*, on how Democrats count. He learned politics in New York's wards and customhouse, and patronage was his life's study (WILL 12–16). His paper: if the war is long, the Union's Democrats will run on peace in 1864. The Northwest's votes will go to whichever party promises the river back. The Union's own electorate is not of one mind. Hist.: a third of California's voters opposed the war in 1861 (ELL 190–191).
  - *John Sherman's letters*, read by his brother (P1): Republican Washington is confident and will not treat early (SHER 110–111).
- **Established:** the Union's constitution and its calendar.
- **Conjecture:** that a war still undecided in 1864 brings a peace candidate.
- **What would settle it:** the conventions of 1864.
- **The respondent's attack.** A Union at war may not hold a fair count. If it does not, the terms in print are useless.
- **What the Republic will do:** print its objects now (Part C) and its terms when it can say what peace costs (the Terms of 1863, canon). It asks nothing more after printing them. If the count is not fair, the fallback applies (D3.6).
- **Right** (CR-065; CR-068 G8).

### D3.6 If he is wrong (D6; D-5, APPROVED: a sentence in 1861, the particulars in 1864). First sitting, closing.

- **The sentence of 1861.** If the South falls in 1862 or 1863, the Republic holds the river and the passes and fights a longer, dearer war that it can still win. If the Union treats early, the objects are the terms.
- **Given by:**
  - *Marcus and Ericsson (P2, P3)*, on what can be held: the river with the turret ship and the passes in winter;
  - *the Calculation Office (P6)*, on what the Republic can pay for and for how long.
- **The respondent's attack, which is Hawaii's.** Victoria's delegation asks what the long war costs Honolulu. The answer is the price taken on the Council's night: shore batteries, two frigates and the second turret ship (CR-068 G2).
- **The particulars** come in 1864 (CR-068 G8) and are not written here.

### D3.7 Elias's own part (P15)

He gives one part, and he gives it last, because it is not evidence. He describes what his own life taught him about how the interior chooses:
- the Santa Fe trail and the Taos winters, where a town's allegiance followed the wagons that fed it (§1; CR-066);
- the Comanche peace in Texas, where a nation made terms with whoever could keep them (§2);
- Querétaro in 1847, where a government that could not hold California recognized the republic that did (§4).
His reading of Lincoln (MD1): a man who will keep his sequence and will be fair. The Council hears it as his experience, not as proof, and the minutes record it that way.

## D4. The respondent's case (D-1, APPROVED)

Sherman supports the protection and objects that the objectives have grown (canon §9). At the end of the second sitting, by custom, he sums up. Argument only; text OPEN.
1. *The war will not stay small.* He gave the reasons himself in the first sitting (D3.1). A war against the Union, begun with a river, will be judged by what it holds at the end.
2. *Raw men do not invade.* The Republic's army is trained (the Defense Law of 1855, §8), but its reserves are men with farms. What it can hold is what it can supply.
3. *A limited war stays limited only if its objects are written down, in a form that can be met.* If the Council will not write them, he will report to it that the army's mandate has no limit he can inspect. This is the point he wins, and it gives the preamble its form: each object says what would meet it, and each later resolution names the object it serves (Part C, C2, C7).
4. *We cannot have two kings.* In our world he said it of a state against the federal power (SHER 120). Here he says it of the Protector against the Council: the Protector commands within the resolution, and the resolution is the Council's (CR-067).
5. *Casey (D5).* He knows Silas Casey from the old army as a careful officer who wrote the book of drill (Hist.). He tells Marcus that a drillmaster is not a fool and that a defended landing at Port Townsend is not a raid. Marcus answers from the evidence: Casey's posts, guns and stores are at Fort Steilacoom, and a careful officer defends what he has fortified, not an open anchorage. On what the Republic knew in June it is the better reading. The minutes record both, and it is a judgment call that goes wrong, not folly.

What Sherman does not do is oppose the war. He goes on supporting the protection, and he inspects Port Townsend afterwards without blaming Marcus for the government's objectives (canon §10). The respondent's work is to test the plan, not to defeat it.

## D7. The minutes

The minutes of the two sittings are sealed and the Chronicler has them (outline D-a). Elias never publishes them, even when Lincoln sets Burch's address beside the fourth object in 1863 (Part C, C6.7). The minutes would prove the reason for the wording but not the result. They also record his own errors, Ralston's rule, Sherman's warning about Casey and Marcus's reasons, and an assumption about paper money that nobody questioned. The Chronicler uses them to judge him. That is what an archive of challenge evenings is for.

## D8. Logan Turner and Carson, outside the room

The outline's note stands. Logan Turner leaves with the eastern technical mission in 1861.
