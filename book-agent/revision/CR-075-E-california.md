## E0. The claim in four sentences

1. California goes to war in 1861 for reasons it held before Elias stated them: what a returning Union would bring (land courts, a tax on the miners, the Senate that rejected California's treaties), and the convention of 1848, which Washington pulled like a leash in May.
2. Its money and its Americans are divided, and the aims are written so that each half can consent: the city's houses get the coin kept at home and the trade back at the peace, the Southern men get no war on the South, and the Union men get no war in the East.
3. California's price is that the West it pays for will not join the Confederation without its consent. Its people put their assent on record in their own words: Vallejo's answer to Baker, Andrés Pico's answer to Beale, and the vote of May 1862.
4. The assent is never safe. It is won again in May 1862, after Port Townsend and the flood, by Vallejo's argument, the Oregon account, the houses' agreement and the Republic's refusal to silence the man preaching against the war.

## E1. How California decides

California's government has to say yes several times, through different bodies, and each is a place where the war can be stopped.

| Body | What it decides | When | Who speaks for California there |
|---|---|---|---|
| The member government (President Vallejo and his Council of State) | Assent to Article XIV | May 1861 | Vallejo (canon §9: he "supports California's assent") |
| California's delegation in the Confederal Council | Concurrence in the reply; the war resolution and its preamble; California's price | Early July 1861 | Vallejo moves the second object (Part C, C8) |
| California's assembly and senate | Renewal of the Protectorship | May 1862, 1863, 1864 | The renewal debates (E10) |
| The Confederal Assembly | It can revoke emergency measures (ledger C-1861-PROTECTOR) | Any time | Burch, for Trinity (Part C, C6) |
| The public record | Opinion put on paper | 1861–62 | Beale's letter and Pico's answer (E7); Starr King's pulpit (E6) |

- E1.1 **What canon gives and what it lacks.** Canon has Hawaii's renewal crises (the Willamette flood condition of 1862, the narrow renewal of 1863, Victoria's conditions of 1864) and none of California's. California pays most of the men and gold, but in canon it only assents. Part E gives its assent reasons, voices and one crisis.
- E1.2 **The California of 1861 is not the one of our world.** It has governed its own land, gold and frontier since 1846. It has had compulsory service since the Defense Law of 1855 (§8), its own reserve bank since 1857, and a navy shared with Hawaii. California in our world complained of Washington's neglect. Pacifica's California has had nothing from Washington to complain about except the convention and the guns behind it.

## E3. The first reason: what a returning Union would bring

- E3.1 **The lock is gone (canon §9, PROPOSED in CR-028).** Since 1850 California's safety rested on the sectional balance: Southern senators would not admit a free California, and Northern Whigs would not conquer one (§4). Secession removes it. For the first time since 1848, a Congress can vote a war on California, and its majority includes the men who call 1848 the Trist surrender. Every Californian who reads a newspaper knows this by May 1861. It is the reason for assent before the packet affair gives the occasion.
- E3.2 **What that means to a voter (Hist. as the measure).** In our world annexation brought California three things, and in Pacifica each is what a Union that came back would bring:
  - *The land courts.* The Land Act of 1851 put every Mexican grant through years of litigation, settlers were ejected without compensation for their improvements, and California was refused the land fund other new states received (ELL 4–35). Vallejo lost his Soscol grant at the Supreme Court in March 1862 after years in the courts (ROS 219), and in his memoirs he blamed the lawyers more than the bandits (ROS 200). In Pacifica the register is the title (§5). Every Californio, and every Yankee with a town lot, holds a title that Washington's courts have never examined.
  - *A tax on the gold.* A Californian member warned Congress in December 1860 that a California tied to the North would be taxed to pay for the war, perhaps by a levy on the miners (ELL 182). In Pacifica the royalty is California's, and the assay is the Republic's (§5).
  - *The Senate that broke the treaties.* Hist.: the eighteen treaties made with California's nations in 1851–52 were refused by the United States Senate in executive session on 8 July 1852 and filed under an injunction of secrecy, which was lifted only on 18 January 1905 (checked, Research). In Pacifica the same treaties are the Republic's own: the homelands, the compact of protection and the Land Fund's annuities (§5; CR-033). A returning Union brings the Senate that refused them.
- E3.3 **Who says it.** Pablo de la Guerra says it first, in Spanish, in the assent debate of May 1861 (PROPOSED). He was Vallejo's fellow envoy at Washington in 1847–48 (§4) and knows what the American courts did in Texas and New Mexico. Vallejo says it to Baker in the answer of May (E4). Elias says it in the seventh reason, as the Union's sequence.
- E3.4 **Its limit.** This reason makes California willing to defend itself. It does not make California willing to take the Columbia. That needs the second and fourth reasons, and the first object (no American arms on the slope).

## E4. The second reason: the leash (E-1, APPROVED)

- E4.1 **The note.** In the first week of May 1861, Baker delivers a note on Seward's instruction, by the same express that brings the proclamation of 19 April (PROPOSED; the date to fix with the packet affair, which comes after it, E-6). It invokes the convention of 1848 as renewed at Carson:
  - California's ports are to be closed to the vessels of the insurgent states and to their agents;
  - deserters from the Pacific squadron are to be returned, Southern-born men among them.
  It is addressed to the Chancellor, since external relations have been confederal since 1856, and copied to the President of California, since the convention is California's.
- E4.2 **Why Washington sends it.** The squadron is guarding the gold steamers and hunting privateers (HUNT 20–21). Washington's agents report Southern men in San Francisco asking for bullion, shipbuilding and recognition (canon §9), and the note is aimed at them. Some of those dealings are Elias's own. They are the preliminary Southern dealings that Victoria requires him to disclose that month (canon §9). The note is fair to send, and it touches him.
- E4.3 **The answer (joint, Chancellor and President, about ten days later; argument only, text OPEN).**
  1. *The article covers the enemies of the United States "in its wars".* The proclamation calls the South's men insurgents and its privateers pirates. If Washington says it is at war, the article applies, and with it the South's belligerency, which Washington denies to every other government (Hist.: JONES ch.1–2). If it is not at war, the article is silent.
  2. *The caution (E-1).* Elias says to Baker in person that the device cuts one way. If Washington admits a war, California will close its ports to Confederate warships as the article binds it. The Republic loses nothing by that. It already receives no privateer of any flag, by Hawaii's rule of 1854 (KUYK 58) and the Confederation's note of 1856, which issues no letter against a partner or a neutral (CR-074).
  3. *Deserters.* The article applies to men who desert American warships, and the Republic keeps it while it lasts, as it did in 1849. Its sheriffs find few (CR-034, the first quarrel under the convention). A resigned officer is not a deserter.
  4. *Agents.* No article names agents. Anyone who keeps the Republic's law is protected by it. That is true, and it protects Elias's own correspondents too. The answer says so in general terms, and Victoria makes him say it in particular to the Council (canon §9).
- E4.4 **What the note teaches.** Before May the convention was an old bargain. After the note, every article of 1848 is a question Washington can ask and Monterey has to answer. That is the second object (an equal treaty in place of 1848), and it is why the packet affair is the occasion of the war rather than its cause (E-1).
- E4.5 **Vallejo's arc.** Vallejo was the most pro-American man to sign in 1846 (§4) and signed the convention in 1848. In 1861 he answers the note that turns it into a leash, and in 1865 he signs its end at Washington, beside Sherman (CR-062). He does not need to change his mind about Americans for this. He has to see that the convention lets Washington ask California to take part in its wars, and in May 1861 he does. His bitterness in our world was over land lost through American courts (ROS 200, 219). In Pacifica that bitterness never formed, and the reason it never formed is the first reason.

## E5. The third reason: the city's money, divided

- E5.1 **For the war:**
  - the Comstock is California's capital, protected by Carson's property guarantees (§8);
  - Oregon's flour, the Sound's timber and coal and the Clearwater's gold come down the Columbia (reason 6);
  - the gold already goes to London directly. Hist.: Volney Howard argued in 1861 that a California free of the tariff and the navigation laws could raise a government's revenue from its own customs (ELL 184–185). In Pacifica that argument was won in 1846.
- E5.2 **Against it:**
  - the New York houses, whose treasure steamers stop in July (CR-069);
  - every merchant who buys in New York on credit;
  - the fear of 1860 (LAV 151): blockade, privateers after the gold, the southern stage road cut.
  In our world the opponents of a Pacific republic argued that trade, people and ships all came from the North (ELL 184). In Pacifica those opponents are the New York houses' party.
- E5.3 **The bridge: Ralston (P5).** Ralston is Ohio-born, sympathized with the South from his New Orleans years and voted for the Chivalry ticket (LAV 150–151). In our world he came out for the Union after Sumter (LAV 152–154). In Pacifica he takes the Republic's oath in July 1861 (CR-069), after the same division, and for the same reason: he backs the government that exists (LAV 156). He makes the Republic's promise believable to the houses because he is one of them. What he promises is the fourth part of the estimate (Part D, D4): the trade comes back at the peace, and the coin stays home in the meantime by the houses' own word. On 1 March 1862 that word becomes the Steamer Day Agreement (CR-069). The houses keep their coin because they are asked, not ordered, which is Ralston's method rather than Elias's.
- E5.4 **Huntington and Sacramento (P4).** The interior's trade runs through Sacramento's stores, and a Union that held the Columbia would run it through Portland on New York's credit. Sacramento is for the war in July 1861 and in trouble by January 1862 (E10).

## E6. The fourth reason: two halves, and the naturalized

- E6.1 **The two halves (Hist. as the measure).** About as many Southern-born as Northern-born men lived in the state in 1861, and a paper argued that neither half would take up arms against its native section (ELL 183). The Assembly's minority refused to make California "the ally of one section" (ELL 185). Los Angeles's paper asked why the city should help invade its friends (WAITE 188). In September 1861 the anti-war Democrats polled over a third of the vote, strongest in the south (ELL 190–191). In Pacifica the proportions are a story assumption; what matters is the shape.
- E6.2 **What each half consents to.** The fifth object holds both. The Southern men consent to a war against the Union because it is not a war for the Union's conquest of the South, and the Republic will lend to the South without sending it an army. The Union men consent, or at least do not resist, because the Republic sends no army east and asks nothing of the Union that a peace could not give. Neither half gets what it wants most: the Southern men get no alliance, and the Union men get no peace.
- E6.3 **Burch (P20; Part C, C6).** Burch speaks for the Southern men in the Confederal Assembly. He gets the fifth object and loses the Rockies. His men want Richmond recognized in 1861 and an alliance by 1862. Canon gives them neither (recognition waits until May 1865). What keeps them is the fifth object and, from April 1862, the Rio Grande resolution's clause to treat with Richmond's forces and not fight them (CR-061 C0a; E10).
- E6.4 **Thomas Starr King (P21; E-4).**
  - *Hist.:* born in New York City in December 1824, the same year as Elias. His father died when he was fifteen, and he educated himself for the ministry while working as a bookkeeper at the Charlestown navy yard to support his younger brothers and sisters, and was ordained at twenty-one without a degree. He came to a small, debt-ridden Unitarian church in San Francisco in April 1860. He was five feet tall with a voice that filled any hall, stumped the state for Lincoln in 1860, and was credited with keeping California in the Union (LAV 154–155). He told the volunteers at Camp Alert that a soldier sick through his own neglect was a kind of traitor (HUNT 36). He was born on 17 December 1824 and died in San Francisco on 4 March 1864, of diphtheria and pneumonia (checked, Research).
  - *In Pacifica:* he is an American citizen, a minister in a foreign republic, with a pulpit and no vote. He preaches that the Republic must not make war on the Union. This is the New York houses' case put as a moral one: the Union is the cause of free government, and to fight it is to fight for the South whatever the fifth object says. His congregation is the Union men's party in the city, and his sermons are printed.
  - *What the Republic does with him:* nothing. It does not suspend the writ (reason 5 sets the Republic against a government that did), does not expel him, and does not close his church. That is the strongest answer the Republic can give him, and the Union men notice it. In the renewal of May 1862 Elias's defenders cite him as the proof that the Republic is not Lincoln's government (E10).
  - *His page room (E-10, decided).* The pulpit only: no collection, no platform, no scenes of his own. He is heard through his printed sermons and through what others say about him.
- E6.5 **The naturalized.** Sherman chose the Republic in 1857 (§8). Halleck never did and took the May steamer east (CR-064). Between them stand thousands of American-born men who naturalized for a register title or a chair. Reason 5 is theirs too. A Union that suspended the writ and held Merryman against the Chief Justice (G2) would also claim its own citizens, and in its own courts it would call their naturalization void. They chose the Republic once, and they will not be reclaimed.

## E7. The Californios: Beale's letter (E-5, APPROVED)

- E7.1 **Hist.** On 3 June 1861 Beale, Lincoln's new surveyor general, wrote to Andrés Pico, the victor of San Pasqual and a leading Breckinridge Democrat, asking whether he stood for the Union at all hazards or for a Pacific republic. Pico declared for the Union without conditions and offered his services and money, his brother Pío said the same, and the letters were printed and moved Californio opinion (THOM 130–131).
- E7.2 **In Pacifica (PROPOSED).** Beale is in the Republic's service as the voice to the nations, and the Picos are founders: Pío was the Republic's provisional president in 1846, and Andrés's lancers beat Kearny at San Pasqual (§4). After the preamble is printed in July 1861, Beale writes to Andrés Pico on his own initiative, without being asked, and publishes the letters. He asks whether Pico stands by the five objects, and in particular whether the fifth binds the south of the state, where the Southern men are strongest.
- E7.3 **The answer.** Pico answers for the objects at all hazards and offers to raise lancers. Pío Pico writes the same, and adds a sentence for the second object: the convention he did not sign in 1848 is one he will be glad to see ended. The letters are printed in Spanish and English. In the autumn of 1862 Andrés Pico raises the Californio lancers for the Rio Grande column (canon, CR-061 C3).
- E7.4 **Why it matters.** The Californios are the Republic's founders and the south's landholders. Their answer, given in print, holds the southern counties where the Southern men are strongest. Beale does it because he knows from 1861 in our world how much a printed answer can do. Elias did not think of it and is glad of it. This is B8 in small: a man acting on what he knows, ahead of the government.
- E7.5 **San Pasqual (E-8, APPROVED; checked against canon).** Hist.: Beale, a naval officer, came out from San Diego with Gillespie's party and joined Kearny's column just before the battle of 6 December 1846. On the night of 8 December, with Kit Carson and an Indian scout, he crept through the Californio lines to San Diego for help (checked, Research). Canon §4 has the battle, Pico's victory, Carson "among the beaten" (CR-066) and Kearny's paroled survivors embarking at San Diego (CR-028), and is silent on Beale. Nothing conflicts. PROPOSED: Beale and Carson get through, as in our world, and what they bring back is not a relief column. Stockton, bound by the standstill and counting its cost (§4), opens the talks that end in the paroles. The letter of 1861 is from a man of the beaten column to the man who beat it. Pico knows it, and his answer says nothing about it.

## E8. Who opposes, and what it costs

- E8.1 **The opposition in California:**
  - the New York houses' party (E5);
  - the Union men of the city and Sacramento, Starr King's congregation (E6);
  - the party of 1856 that nearly refused the Articles (§8) and still doubts any undertaking made in Hawaii's company;
  - the families whose sons serve under the Defense Law (§8), who pay the war's cost in men.
- E8.2 **Sherman.** He supports the protection and objects to the growing objectives (canon §9). The preamble gives him a written limit (Part C, C7). He is not an opponent of California's assent; he is the man who will tell California's legislature when the war has outgrown it.
- E8.3 **The cost.** California pays most of the war's men and gold (§9; MODEL in `finance/CR-059-068-workbook.md`). Part E changes no amounts. E9's price adds no cost. E10's crisis is political, not financial.

## E9. California's price: no new member without the founders (E-2, APPROVED as (a))

- E9.1 **The article (PROPOSED form).** An article of the war resolution of July 1861, beside Honolulu's batteries and frigates: *no land or people associated with the Confederation during the war shall become a member of it without the consent of both member states, given by their legislatures.*
- E9.2 **Why California asks it.** Its fear is the one the Pacific-republic current in our world never faced: a West conquered with California's men and gold, outvoting California in its own Confederation. Burch's republic of five (Part C, C6) would be California swallowed by the interior. The article puts the interior's admission in the founders' hands. It is California's answer to Burch as much as to the war.
- E9.3 **Why Hawaii agrees.** The article gives Hawaii the same consent, and Hawaii fears the same thing more. In 1865 Lot names it: Hawaii's half of the Council ends when the new states come in, each with a delegation equal to its own (canon §12). The article of 1861 does not prevent that. It makes it happen only with Hawaii's vote.
- E9.4 **The check against 1866 (as E-2 required).** Canon fits: the Confederal chambers and California entrench Oregon's charter, and Hawaii's legislature ratifies it on Victoria's message (§12). PROPOSED: every charter of 1866 goes the same way, ratified by both founding states, and accession comes at the exchange of 1867. The Enabling Law of 1865 already needs "ratification by both founding states" (§12). The article of 1861 is that requirement written down four years earlier, for the case it was made for. No fact changes.
- E9.5 **What it does not do.** It gives California no weight in the Council after admission, and no veto over the war's operations. It is a price paid at the door.

## E10. The renewal of May 1862 (E-3, APPROVED)

- E10.1 **The winter against it** (the flood checked, Research: in our world Sacramento went under on 10 January 1862, the day of the governor's inauguration, and the legislature moved to San Francisco for six months; where California's legislature sits in this timeline is to check):
  - *Port Townsend.* Casey beat the landing in the autumn of 1861, and the Union kept its anchorage on the Sound (canon §10). Sherman's report blames actual failures and refuses to blame Marcus for the government's objectives (canon), and the report is laid before both legislatures.
  - *The Iowa regiments* are on their way through Panama (canon §10), and the federal recovery attempt is expected in the late spring (canon §10). The renewal is voted before the turret ship has fought.
  - *The flood.* Hist.: the winter of 1861–62 brought the greatest flood in California's record; Sacramento was under water and the Northwest's movements stopped (HUNT 32, 231). In Pacifica it falls on California's capital of trade and on the war's supply road at once.
  - *Washington's paper and the houses.* The Union stops paying in coin at the end of December, and Lincoln chooses paper in February (canon §9, CR-069). The New York houses' party says the war has cut California off from its customers for nothing.
  - *The Rio Grande.* The Council votes the Rio Grande resolution in the first days of April (CR-061 C0a), a new theatre a month before the renewal. Sherman says the objects are growing, and the Union men quote him.
- E10.2 **What carries it (PROPOSED):**
  - *Vallejo on the leash.* He reads Baker's note of May 1861 to the assembly, then the second object. If the Republic stands down now, the convention of 1848 is still in force and Washington will ask every question in it again, with an army behind it (E4).
  - *The Oregon account.* From 1862 the customs collected at Astoria and Portland go into a published Oregon account that pays Oregon's courts and county officers (canon §10). It is printed in Monterey that spring. It shows that the fourth object works: no one annexed, no one sworn, Oregon governing itself on its own revenue.
  - *The coin held.* The Steamer Day Agreement of 1 March 1862 (CR-069): the houses keep their coin at home by their own word, and the Republic's banks did not suspend when the Union's did. The New York houses' party loses its best argument in the city that should have been its strongest.
  - *Starr King's pulpit.* The man preaching against the war is free and preaching, in a city where nobody has been arrested for opinion. The Union in May 1862 has suspended the writ. California's Union men vote, or abstain, on that.
  - *The armistice offer.* In the last week of April Elias has offered Washington an armistice on the lines held and a meeting at a neutral place (CR-068 PP3c). It is at sea while the assembly debates. Vallejo puts it to the members plainly: the Republic has asked for peace on its objects, and a renewal refused now would give away the lines it asked to stand on before Washington has answered.
  - *The Rio Grande clause.* The April resolution's instruction to treat with Richmond's forces and not fight them is what Burch's Southern men take home (E6.3).
- E10.3 **The vote.** It is carried narrowly in California's assembly and more easily in its senate (margins OPEN). Hawaii renews the same month on Victoria's Deady condition (canon §10). Victoria uses Sherman's report as evidence for her conditions (canon §10). California uses it as evidence that the war is being run honestly. The two legislatures renew for different reasons, and that is how it goes in each later year.
- E10.4 **Who is on the page.** Vallejo, de la Guerra, Burch (from the Confederal Assembly, where he does not vote on the renewal but speaks for it in the press), Starr King outside, and Sherman's report on the table. Elias does not speak in the debate, and nobody asks him to. The renewal is California's to give.
- E10.5 **1863 and 1864 (E-11).** California renews in 1863 without a crisis: the Columbia is held, and the Rio Grande compact and the Deseret articles are signed. Hawaii's renewal of 1863 is the narrow one (CR-068 CT16). In 1864 California renews readily, because Washoe is its capital and the first reservation of 1863 brings it back (canon §10). Hawaii renews only with conditions, which begin with "no annexation" (CR-060 C6). The two members diverge on Washoe. That divergence is the clearest sign that California's reasons were always its own.

## E11. Oregon beside California

Oregon is not a member in 1861 and has no vote on the aims, but its people decide whether the fourth object works.
- **Its own reasons (Hist. as the measure):**
  - isolation and Washington's neglect, which Whiteaker's message of 1860 complained of (JOH 161) and which is sharpened in Pacifica by the refusal of admission in 1859 (CR-059);
  - the coin and trade that already run through San Francisco (CR-059; HUNT 227–228);
  - the neutrality its Breckinridge Democrats wanted: no part in a war on the South (JOH 197–209).
- **The compact is that programme.** Canon's Oregon compact has no allegiance, and Oregon's men, ports and revenues serve no military purpose outside Oregon, with Whiteaker in the chair and Deady as its court (§10). That is the Northwest's own neutrality of 1861 made into an instrument. Whiteaker gets what he wanted in our world (P17).
- **Its opposition.** The Douglas Democrats, who called neutrality cowardice (JOH 194), and Nesmith, who wanted Oregon tied to the North (JOH 186; P19). The compact has to live with them. In 1866 they nearly win: Oregon chooses Pacifica narrowly (canon §12).
- **Its expansionist.** Lane (P16). In our world he defended secession and was believed to be coming home to found a Pacific republic (JOH 156–162). Elias keeps him at arm's length in 1861, and in 1866 he speaks for accession at Roseburg (canon §12).
- **California's view of Oregon.** E9's article is California's protection against Oregon as much as against the Basin: Oregon will not be a member until California and Hawaii say so, and in 1866 they do.
