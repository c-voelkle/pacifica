## F0. The claim in four sentences

1. Hawaii goes to war in 1861 against its own instinct. A kingdom that in our world asked Washington for an American warship to protect its whalers has in Pacifica been boarded by that warship, and Victoria makes the Council see that the protection Hawaii would have asked for is the danger it now faces.
2. The kingdom is divided:
   - the planters gain from the war;
   - Honolulu's whaling trade and its young seamen lose by it;
   - the mission families oppose a war on the country they came from;
   - the Hawaiian-language press reads the Union as the settlers' government.
   The king refuses Wyllie's wish to recognize the South, as he did in our world.
3. Hawaii also wants power for itself:
   - its islands;
   - a navy at Honolulu;
   - Honolulu as the North Pacific's harbour;
   - weight at sea against California's growth on land.
   The islands are written into the first object as defence and into a sealed minute as the kingdom's claim. The rest comes with the price and the peace, so Hawaii still asks for nothing outside the objects.
4. From 1862 onward it is the keeper of the aims. At each renewal its condition holds the Protector to a different object, and the doubt it entered on the fourth object in 1861 is the ground of all three.

- F1.1 **The king.** Hist.: Kamehameha IV refused in 1861 anything that looked like recognition of the South. Wyllie wanted to grant belligerent rights, and the king privately called him an old woman for it (KUYK 65–66). PROPOSED: the king does the same in Pacifica. He lets Victoria carry the authorization, as canon has it, and keeps for himself the one refusal that is his to make: no recognition of Richmond. Kamehameha IV dies on 30 November 1863 (Hist.), and from Lot's accession the refusal is Lot's (F-7). It holds until May 1865 (canon).
- F1.2 **Victoria's office.** Hist.: Kamehameha IV made her Kuhina Nui in 1855, and Wyllie's constitutional amendments of 1860–62 aimed to reduce the office's power (KUYK 36, 121–123). In Pacifica she keeps the office under Lot (CR-014). In 1861 she is the kingdom's officer who can sign beside the king, and Wyllie's wish to reduce the office (Hist.) is a pressure she has to answer by using it well.
- F1.3 **Where she is in 1861 (D-9, APPROVED).** She comes to Monterey in May with Hawaii's demands and stays through the Council's night. The pattern is canon's for 1865: the Council sits at Monterey from June to August and Victoria leads Hawaii's delegation (§12). The king governs at Honolulu.

## F2. The reasons and the price (canon, CR-068 G2), and Victoria's argument (F-2, APPROVED)

- F2.1 **What canon has.** Hawaii's delegation concurs on the first three of the seven reasons:
  1. the squadron's claim to stop a neutral packet at sea on suspicion;
  2. the squadron lives on this coast;
  3. a kingdom that gave its defence to the common navy must see the navy defend it.
  It records its doubt of the sixth and enters the seventh as the Protector's own. Its price is Honolulu's batteries, two frigates and the second turret ship.
- F2.2 **Victoria's argument (F-2).** Hist.: in June 1861 a report of a Confederate privateer in the Pacific reached Honolulu. The American commissioner, on Seward's instructions, asked what Hawaii would do about Confederate ships in its ports. Wyllie answered that the king had no force to make his neutrality respected, and that Honolulu, with millions in American whaling property, needed an American warship present (KUYK 65–66). In Pacifica, Victoria turns that history round:
  - Hawaii gave its defence to the common navy in 1856 so that it would never again have to ask a great power for a warship;
  - the warship that Washington would send to "protect" Honolulu is the kind that boarded its packet and killed its sailor;
  - so a kingdom that cannot make its neutrality respected must either have the Confederation's navy do it or have the Union's navy do it for its own purposes.
  This is the third reason, given in her own terms. It is why the third object stands by Hawaii's name (Part C, C3.3).
- F2.3 **Who the sailor was (Hist. as the measure).** Hawaii's part in the whaling trade was to supply the ships and to man them. Thousands of young Hawaiian men went to sea on whaleships and merchantmen (KUYK 138). PROPOSED: the sailor killed in the boarding is one of them, serving on a Hawaiian packet. The canon sailor stays unnamed (CR-062 T9). His death makes the boarding a matter for the families of the coast as well as for the court.

## F3. Wyllie (P9)

- F3.1 **His estimate.** Hist.: Wyllie believed in 1861 that the South would win its independence (KUYK 65). In Pacifica his letter is read at the first sitting (Part D, D1.2, D3.1). He is the Council's strongest voice for the conclusion that the South holds, and its strongest voice for recognizing Richmond early. The king and Victoria are the check.
- F3.2 **His other instinct (PROPOSED; F-3).** Hist.: from 1857 to 1863 Wyllie pursued a general treaty by which the maritime powers would guarantee Hawaii's independence and neutrality, with Bowring as envoy. Bowring thought the American war an invincible impediment (KUYK 55–57). In Pacifica, in the summer of 1861, Wyllie proposes that the third object be secured at the peace by such a guarantee of the Pacific's neutral waters, signed by Britain, France and the United States.
- F3.3 **Vallejo answers him with 1848.** Vallejo refused Buchanan's guarantee because "a guarantor has a right to come in" (canon §4). A guarantee of Hawaii's waters by three navies would give each of them that right. Hawaii chose the Confederation in 1856 instead of a guarantee, and the war tests that choice. Victoria sides with Vallejo, and the proposal is not taken up. It is the reason the third object is met by the peace and the first object, and not by an instrument (CT2, Case A).

## F4. The kingdom divided

- F4.1 **The planters: for, quietly.**
  - *Hist.:* Hawaiian sugar grew fastest in 1861–66 because of the war. Prices rose from June 1861, the best sold at San Francisco for up to 13½ cents a pound in 1864–65, and the American war tariff took part of the gain (KUYK 140–146). Exports averaged little more than half a million pounds a year in 1855–57 and over nineteen million in 1870–72 (KUYK 140).
  - *In Pacifica:* Hawaiian sugar enters California inside the Confederation's customs union, and no American tariff touches it. The war is the planters' boom, and they do not say so in public.
  - This is why both of canon's sugar measures of 1864 reach Honolulu where it matters: Lincoln's offer of a treaty admitting Hawaiian sugar free of duty, and the army and fleet's contract for Hawaiian sugar at a fixed price for the length of the war (canon §12). The contract outbids the offer because it pays now.
- F4.2 **Honolulu's whaling trade: against.**
  - *Hist.:* Honolulu lived by supplying whaleships with provisions, refits, cash for wages and men. The whalers' bills of exchange were the town's remittance to New England and Europe, and the trade was already in depression in the later 1850s, before the war halved the American fleet (KUYK 138–139).
  - *In Pacifica:* from July 1861 the American whalers are enemy ships and stop calling at Hawaiian ports. Hist.: the Confederate cruisers destroyed forty-six whalers (KUYK 138); whether they hunt in the Pacific in this timeline is left to canon. Honolulu's chandlers and shipyards, mostly in American hands, lose their customers, and the young men who signed on at Honolulu lose their berths.
  - The Republic issues no letters of marque (CR-074: reserved in 1856, first issued in 1878). Its war is not made against the whalers.
  - *American whalers in Hawaiian ports in July 1861 (F-4):* PROPOSED, as a note in this CR only (F-4; no storyline sentence): they are given thirty days to sail, under a published safe-conduct to leave the Confederation's waters. The Republic does not seize them.
- F4.3 **The mission families: against.** American-born or American-descended, they hold seats in Hawaii's delegation and its legislature. In 1865 two of them vote against the Enabling Law (§12). They oppose a war on the country they came from. Lincoln aims at them in 1863, expecting the mission churches to carry the legislature (CR-068). They are what makes the renewal of 1863 narrow.
- F4.4 **The Hawaiian-language press: for, in its own way.** The Hawaiian-language press, *Ka Hoku o ka Pakipika* (1861–63), was Kalākaua's paper against the mission press, and in 1863 it prints both of Lincoln's papers and the account of the nations (CR-068). Its readers are a Native people under their own king. They read the Union as the government of the settlers and missionaries' sons who already own much of their best land. The paper begins in the autumn of 1861 (Hist.; first issue to check), after the packet affair of May, so it does not report the boarding as news. From its first issue it names the sailor's island and keeps the affair in print, and the mission press calls the boarding an officer's excess (F-6).
- F4.5 **The court and Britain.** The king and his circle look to Britain (Hist.; KUYK 65–66: British and French neutrality decided Hawaii's own in August 1861). In Pacifica, Britain's neutrality proclamation of May 1861 recognized both the Union and the South as belligerents (JONES ch.2). The court reads it as permission: if London does not take Washington's side, Honolulu need not either.

## F5. The kingdom's own power, and its islands (APPROVED, author, 19:58)

- F5.1 **What Hawaii wants for itself:**
  1. *The islands:* Johnston back, Palmyra held, the American guano claims gone, Midway kept from becoming an American coaling station.
  2. *A navy at Honolulu.* Canon's price (two frigates, the second turret ship, the shore batteries; CR-068 G2) is more than safety. It makes Honolulu the Confederation's second naval base, and the kingdom a naval power in its own right.
  3. *Honolulu as the North Pacific's harbour.* In the later 1850s the New England owners sent out their own supplies, agents and money to cut Honolulu's merchants out of the whaling profits (KUYK 139). A Pacific with no American station leaves Honolulu as the only port of refit and coal between the coast and Asia.
  4. *Weight inside the Confederation.* California grows on land by the fourth object, so Hawaii wants a sphere at sea to balance it: the land to California, the sea to Hawaii. It is Lot's fear of 1865 (canon §12), met in advance.

  The aim is the Kamehameha house's: the king, Victoria and Lot, with Wyllie, who had promoted guano since 1853 (KUYK 283 n.35). It is not the planters'. They want the sugar market (F4.1).
- F5.2 **The islands in our world (Hist.; checked 18 November 2026).**
  - *The northwest chain.* In 1857 Kamehameha IV sailed to Nihoa on the *Manuokawai* and reaffirmed its annexation. He then sent Captain John Paty on to Laysan and Lisianski, which were declared the kingdom's (1 and 10–11 May 1857).
  - *Johnston.* In March 1858 the schooner *Palestine*, chartered by William Parker and R. F. Ryan, claimed Johnston under the Guano Act. Where she sailed from is OPEN. In June the *Kalama*, under the king's commission, took down the American flag. The king proclaimed the annexation on 27 July 1858, and later that year withdrew because the American claim was prior.
  - *The equatorial islands.* The American Guano Company of New York held Baker and Jarvis, with Howland disputed with the United States Guano Company. G. P. Judd was the company's agent at Honolulu (1858). C. H. Judd took twenty-three Hawaiian workmen to Jarvis in February 1858. The islands' labour, food and water came from Honolulu on ships such as the *Josephine*, and Honolulu men governed them.
  - *The Navy.* The confirming voyage came from Panama, not from California: in 1857 the sloop *St. Mary's* (Commander Charles H. Davis) went from New Granada to Jarvis and Baker, and reported from Honolulu in October.
  - *Midway.* In July 1859 Captain N. C. Brooks, of the bark *Gambia*, which was Hawaiian-registered and owned by B. F. Snow of Honolulu, claimed it for the United States. The *Lackawanna* took formal possession only on 28 August 1867.
  - *Palmyra.* Claimed for the United States in 1859 by G. P. Judd of the *Josephine*. Commissioned for the kingdom on 26 February / 1 March 1862 in the names of the king and the Kuhina Nui. Taken by Zenas Bent and J. B. Wilkinson on 15 April 1862. Proclaimed by Lot as Minister of the Interior in June 1862.

  So the American claims rested on Honolulu's harbour, Hawaiian labour and an occasional sloop from Panama. The Sound played no part.
- F5.3 **The chain in Pacifica (APPROVED as a chain; details PROPOSED).**
  1. *1856–57.* The New York companies need what they needed in our world: Honolulu's ships, water, food and men. Honolulu is now a Confederation port. The guano men take out the Republic's venture licence (canon, CR-074: "guano men on the equatorial islands" are among the first holders), and the labourers' contracts are registered at Honolulu. The licence covers the voyage and the labour, not the flag: Washington's Act lets its own citizens plant theirs.
  2. *1857.* The kingdom moves first in its own waters: Paty annexes Nihoa, Laysan and Lisianski, as in history, ahead of the American claimers.
  3. *1858: Johnston.* The *Palestine* claims it in March and the *Kalama* in June. The king withdraws, as in history, but here it is on the Council's advice: a rock is not worth a quarrel with Washington while the convention of 1848 stands, and the common navy has no ship to spare. Victoria remembers it. It is the kingdom's loss under the shadow of the American navy, and the history behind its island aim.
  4. *1859.* Brooks, on a Honolulu-owned and Hawaiian-registered ship, claims Midway for the United States, and Judd's *Josephine* claims Palmyra. The kingdom protests the use of its flag (if the *Gambia* sailed under a Pacifican licence, Brooks forfeits his bond). The claims stand on paper only.
  5. *July 1861.* The war cuts the claims off. The Union cannot protect them: it has no Honolulu, its last stations are on the Columbia and the Sound, and Panama is far away. To keep working, the companies need their Hawaiian licences and Hawaiian men. The kingdom lets them carry on, because the Republic seizes no private property and issues no letters of marque (F4.2; CR-074). From then on the islands are worked under Hawaii's protection in fact.
  6. *April 1862: Palmyra.* The kingdom takes it under the commission of February–March in the names of the king and the Kuhina Nui. It is the first use of the sealed minute (Part C, C1.5). The same year the kingdom quietly revives its Kalama claim to Johnston.
  7. *1865.* The Pacific article records what the war has made true (canon, CR-029): the Union renounces claims it can no longer protect, the companies keep their workings, and their cargoes land duty-free at the ports their labourers come from.
  8. *1867.* No *Lackawanna*: Midway was renounced in the spring.
- F5.4 **How it is written in 1861 (Part C).** In public, it is the first object's island clause: no American station "on any island of the Pacific from which the Confederation or either of its members can be struck". In private, it is the sealed minute on the Council's night: the Confederation will support at the peace the kingdom's claims to the islands of its domain, including any it takes in the war. The public clause is defence. The minute is the kingdom's ambition. The Republic's further purpose in the Pacific is not stated in 1861 or in this CR.
- F5.5 **Victoria's two roles.** She is the keeper of the published objects (F6), and she holds an unpublished one of her own. She holds Elias to the letter of the fourth object at every renewal, and he knows about the minute, because he spoke for the clause that covers it. Neither says so to the other. The Chronicler has both the minutes and the renewals and sets them side by side.

## F6. Hawaii as the keeper of the aims (F-5)

In 1861 Victoria's party entered its doubt of the fourth object: it was a promise to peoples Hawaii had never seen, made in Hawaii's name (Part C, C8). PROPOSED: at each renewal the kingdom's condition holds the Protector to one object, and the doubt of 1861 is the ground of all three.

| Renewal | Canon condition or event | The object it holds him to |
|---|---|---|
| 1862 | Compliance with Deady's ruling after the Willamette flood (canon §10) | The fourth object, third clause: protection under the people's own law, not the garrison's |
| 1863 | Put over to the last days of the term; Elias goes to Honolulu and answers before the legislature, beginning in Hawaiian (CR-068 CT16, PP4c) | The fifth object: why the Republic's gold stands behind four million who will never be examined. He answers with the seventh reason, which Victoria's party entered in 1861 as the Protector's own |
| 1864 | No annexation; Carson's guarantees kept; Hawaii's control of entry at its own ports; Washoe's own choice asked and refused (CR-060 C6; CR-068 CT8) | The fourth object, first clause: no people by decree. It answers the guard put on the Salt Lake road in 1862 without a resolution (Part C, C7.2) |

- F6.1 **Why Hawaii and not California.** California's reasons are its own, and in 1864 Washoe is California's capital (Part E, E10.5). Hawaii's only interest in the West is that the war it pays for stays inside the objects. So Hawaii reads the objects as written and enforces them, and California reads them as interests.
- F6.2 **What it costs Elias.** Each condition is narrower than the last renewal, and each is won with an argument from the preamble. He accepts every one except Washoe's own choice in 1864, and that one is carried out in 1866 by the Republic's choice rather than Hawaii's (CR-060 C6). The Chronicler records it as the keeper's one loss.

## F7. What Hawaii gets at the peace (F-1, APPROVED: no new aim)

- The first object: no station on the slope; Port Townsend evacuated in 1867 (CR-062).
- The third object: met by the peace and the first object, with the treaty silent on search (CT2, Case A).
- The sailor's claim: his family keeps its private claim before the mixed commission (CR-062 T9, Victoria's condition, PROPOSED there).
- Its own guarantees: the protected provisions of the Enabling Law, with control of entry and the Kuhina Nui's office among them (canon §12; CR-068 CT8).
- The Pacific article (canon, CR-029): the Union renounces its guano claims to six islands and quitclaims Palmyra, which Hawaii annexed in 1862. It comes under the first object's island clause and the sealed minute (F5.4), not a new aim, so F-1 stands.
- What it cannot get: its half of the Council. Lot names the loss in 1865 (canon §12), and California's price of 1861 (Part E, E9) gives Hawaii a vote on each admission but no protection against the result.

## F8. The law of the sea (cross-reference)

Part C, C3.3 carries it. Hawaii acceded in 1855 to the Russo-American principles (free ships make free goods; neutral goods in enemy ships free) and did not accede to the Declaration of Paris until 1867 (KUYK 63–65). The Confederation's note of 1856 is Hawaii's own position (CR-074). Search in war is a belligerent's right (JONES ch.2), and the third object claims more than the law of nations gives. Hawaii's crisis of 1861 rests on its hulls, not on the law.
