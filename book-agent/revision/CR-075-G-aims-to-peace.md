## G0. The claim in four sentences

1. The peace of 1865–67 is the preamble of 1861 carried through two more instruments: the six heads of 1863 turn the objects into principles a Union government could sign, and the treaty of 1865 turns the principles into articles and a map.
2. Of everything Pacifica keeps in 1867, all but three items follow from an object or a rule of 1861:
   - Washoe, retaken by arms against the fourth object's spirit;
   - the empty parks of the Rockies, held by the snow and paid for;
   - Alaska, which is bought and stands outside the war.
3. The peace delivers each object in a different way:
   - the first by evacuation;
   - the second by a new treaty;
   - the third by the peace itself;
   - the fourth by votes and compacts;
   - the fifth by the Republic's own conduct;
   - the price by $40 million in six instalments.
4. The Chronicler can say what the Republic went to war for and whether it got it, because the Republic wrote it down before the war and never changed it. That is CT1's verdict (Case B), and Part G is the record he uses.

## G1. The chain of instruments

| 1861: the object (Part C) | 1863: the head or reservation (CR-068 CT10) | 1865: the article (CR-062) | 1867 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **First:** no American station, storehouse, garrison or fortified post on the slope, or on any island of the Pacific from which the Confederation or a member can be struck; the sealed minute on the kingdom's islands (Part C, C1.5) | No head of its own: by August 1863 the slope is held except Port Townsend, which reservation (b) addresses (the last American station on the Sound), and the islands are worked under Hawaii's protection (Part F, F5.3) | T3, the evacuation of Port Townsend; T5, no naval station and no storehouse anywhere, warships for refuge or repair only; T7, the Pacific article (CR-029) | Port Townsend evacuated after the exchange; the guano claims renounced and Palmyra quitclaimed; no *Lackawanna* at Midway |
| **Second:** an equal treaty in place of 1848; the frontier drawn by consent | Head 2 (each territory's future by its inhabitants' act) draws the frontier; head 4 (the ports open to American commerce) replaces the unequal articles with equal ones | T8, the conventions of 1848 and 1860 ended, Carson's guarantees kept; T2, the line; T5, commerce on most-favoured terms; T9, claims waived on the 1848 model | The treaty between equals is in force |
| **Third:** no search of Pacifican or Hawaiian ships in their own waters | No head | Silent (CT2, Case A) | Met by the peace and by the first object |
| **Fourth:** no people by decree, none given up who chose, protection for the punished | Head 1 (no state of the Union asked) and head 2; reservation (a) (a state made by Congress before the peace may be occupied) | O1–O7, Oregon by its voters' choice; T10, residents choose their citizenship; T10a, the Colorado article; T12, Native title by separate agreements; T15, Idaho and Washington Territory | Oregon's vote (October 1866); the charters of 1866; the compacts; Washoe under reservation (a) |
| **Fifth:** no army east, no conquest on this continent, credit for a people that holds its ground | Head 5 (nothing asked concerning the South as a term of this peace); the recognition of Richmond "no term of this peace" | T13, the treaty neither recognizes Richmond nor disposes of the Union's claim to the South | Recognition in May 1865, outside the treaty |
| **The price:** what the Republic keeps it will pay for | Head 3 (the public domain and the public works paid for in gold) | T4, $40 million in gold, six instalments through 1872 | First instalment at the exchange |
| **The promise to treat:** at any time, on these objects, asking nothing beyond them | Head 6 (not raised by victory or lowered by defeat); reservation (b) (the heads stand for any President who will treat) | The treaty is negotiated inside the heads (T2) | Kept: the Senate consents to what Monterey printed in 1863 |
| **California's price** (Part E, E9) | — | O6, Pacifica ratifies through both chambers and both founding states | The charters of 1866 ratified by both founding states |

- G1.1 **What the chain shows.** Each instrument is narrower than the one before it and says nothing the earlier one did not allow. The heads of 1863 do not mention the first or the third object, because by then the first was a fact on the ground and the third could only be met by the peace. The treaty's map, sum and articles were negotiated in 1865 inside the heads (CR-062 T2). McClellan's negotiators met nothing in December 1865 that Monterey had not printed in 1861 or 1863. That was the purpose of the promise to treat.
- G1.2 **The Senate.** In April 1867 the Senate consents to a cession of territories, not states (§12), paid for in gold, of lands whose peoples had chosen by vote or compact. Three of the objects reach the Senate in forms it can vote for: head 1 (no state), head 2 (the peoples' own act) and head 3 (paid). It is not asked to call any of it a surrender.

- G2.1 **The count.** Of the nineteen rows, two are not holdings (the silence on fugitives and the private loan). Of the other seventeen, fourteen pass plainly. Three stand apart, and each is a different kind:
  - *Washoe* breaks the spirit of an object;
  - *the empty parks* are covered only by the price;
  - *Alaska* has nothing to do with the war.
  The Chronicler names the first. The second is covered by a single line (G-3), and the third needs none.
- G2.2 **Where the line moved inside the heads.** CT10 says it: "the heads are principles, and the map moves inside them." Washoe, western Montana and the guano islands are the three movements canon names. Part G adds the parks, which canon already has (CO4) but nobody had tested against the objects.

## G4. What the objects asked and the peace did not deliver

- **Washoe's own choice.** Hawaii asked for it in 1864 and was refused (CR-060 C6). Washoe voted on its charter in 1866 by the Republic's choice, which met the fourth object late and not as written.
- **The third object in writing.** Not asked for, by decision (CT2, Case A). Wyllie's guarantee was refused (Part F, F3).
- **Hawaii's half of the Council.** It was never one of the objects, and it ends with the admissions (Lot, 1865, §12). California's price gave Hawaii a vote on each admission and no more (Part E, E9.5).
- **The four million.** The fifth object asked nothing concerning the South, and the peace asked nothing. The Chronicler records what that cost (W4; CR-068 G4).

## G5. The Chronicler's record (CT1, Case B)

CT1 replaces CR-068's verdict on 1861 (M9; G2's "a war for the West against an adversary who had offered him redress") with this: Lincoln's offer was honest and answered the wrong that had been done; it could not answer what the Republic went to war for, which the reply and its preamble said; the choice was the founder's.

Part G is what that verdict rests on. The Chronicler has the preamble, the Terms, the treaty and the sealed minutes (Part D, D7), and he scores the peace against the preamble, not against the map:
- the first, second and third objects met, the third by the peace;
- the fourth met, with Washoe against it;
- the fifth kept, with its cost named;
- the price paid;
- the promise kept.
CR-068's W1 ("the word held") is this table in three words, and W4 is its exception column. Part G adds no new judgment to the Chronicler's. It gives him the count.

## G6. Who reads the peace against the preamble in 1867

- **Burch** (Part C, C6.6): his republic of five is very nearly the map, reached by the fourth object, not by his address.
- **Lincoln** at Springfield (canon: he lives there until 1872): he read the preamble as a programme of dismemberment in 1863 (outline I3), and the map of 1867 is enough to keep him reading it that way. The count of how the land came is also there for him to read.
- **Victoria**: her doubt of the fourth object, entered in 1861, is answered by Washoe, and she says so.
- **Sherman**, who signs the treaty beside Vallejo (CR-062): the written objects he insisted on in June 1861 (Part D, D4) are the ones he signs off in December 1865.
