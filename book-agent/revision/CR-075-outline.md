## 0. What canon already fixes

- **1848:** the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention (§4, CR-028): American warships may use San Francisco, Monterey and San Diego, with a revocable storehouse at Sausalito; California's ports are closed to the enemies of the United States in its wars; deserters from American warships are returned; American residents have civil guarantees; most-favoured-nation commerce; the Basin is renounced pending a boundary commission. The commercial and naval articles run twelve years. Vallejo signs it; Buchanan's guarantee of independence is refused ("a guarantor has a right to come in").
- **1850:** the Treaty of Monterey fixes the eastern line; California gives up the Basin. The Republic's safety rests on the sectional lock: Southern senators will not admit a free California, Northern Whigs will not conquer one (§4, CR-028).
- **1859:** the House refuses Oregon's admission; Buchanan's negotiators ask for a stronger privilege at Sausalito, and Pacifica refuses (§8, CR-059).
- **1860:** Washoe. Hays's crossing, Victoria's defeat of Article XIV, the Carson Convention: Washoe recognised as American, and the commercial articles of 1848 renewed for a further term (§8, CR-028). Washington reinforces the Northwest.
- **1861:** secession removes the Southern bloc; "the men who call 1848 the Trist surrender sit in a Congress the South no longer balances" (§9, CR-028, PROPOSED). Lincoln refuses Seward's foreign war with Monterey among its objects (CR-068). Sausalito is sealed. Southern agents solicit bullion; Northern representatives ask the Confederation to supply or finance federal armies (§9). The packet affair; Article XIV; the Protectorship from 1 June; a May resolution for limited coercive operations, with no annexation by decree (§9). Lincoln's offer in June; the reply and the **seven reasons** (CR-068 G2); the Council concurs "on the ground that the American squadron's last Pacific stations are the Columbia and the Sound"; Hawaii's price (batteries, two frigates, the second turret ship). The offensive in mid-July; Congress recognises a state of war before 6 August.
- **1862–1864:** the Columbia held; the interior comes in by compacts (Oregon 1861, Deseret 1862–63, the Rio Grande 1863); the Richmond credit "lent for independence" (February 1863); the **Terms of Monterey**, six heads, August 1863, "neither raised nor lowered by the fortunes of the war"; Washoe retaken in the spring of 1864 under the first reservation (CR-060, CR-068 G6–G7).
- **1865–1867:** the armistice (May 1865); the Western treaty (December 1865, ratified 9 April 1867): the conventions of 1848 and 1860 ended, the Western line in place of the Basin's renunciation, Port Townsend and Fort Union evacuated, no storehouse anywhere, the ports open on published terms, $40 million in gold, the claims waived (CR-039, CR-062).
- **The verdicts:** the Chronicler's three causes (CR-068 W1, APPROVED); the schoolbook's formula (W2, **deferred**: it "should be more patriotic and not give the win to another country in the first sentence").

The end state and the sequence are fixed. This CR supplies **what the Republic set out to win, why each government agreed, and the plan by which Elias expected to win it**, so that 1865 is the result of 1861 and not its afterthought.

## A. The gap

- A1. **The aims as canon has them are a reply, not a purpose.** In July 1861 they are the reply's three demands (no search in Pacifican and Hawaiian waters; no prosecution of the Columbia's inhabitants who traded with the Republic, and the writ open to them; Oregon's convention to meet with no army in Oregon) and the removal of the squadron's two stations. Nothing in 1861 names the interior, the convention of 1848 or the price, yet all three are in the Terms of 1863 and the treaty of 1865.
- A2. **The outcome is much larger than the aims.** The Columbia and the Sound in 1861 become the Northwest, the Basin, Deseret, Idaho, western Montana, Washoe, New Mexico to 103°W, Arizona and the Western Slope by 1865. Between them stand only "choices rather than inevitability" (§9) and the Terms. Read cold, that looks like territory picked up along the way: the "Ajax/Odysseus" risk again, at the scale of the war.
- A3. **California's reasons are Elias's arguments.** CR-068 assigns California the second, fifth and sixth of the seven reasons (the squadron on its coast; Oregon's merchants; the river's wheat, timber, coal and gold). No Californian actor wants anything in his own words; Vallejo only "supports California's assent". California pays most of the war's men and gold, and its legislature renews the Protectorship three times, but canon has no Californian renewal debate.
- A4. **The Chronicler's frame leans on Lincoln.** M9: "the moment Lincoln is more reasonable than Elias and Elias knows it"; G2: "He chose a war for the West against an adversary who had offered him redress." With no aims of its own, the Republic's war can only be measured against Lincoln's offer, and it loses that comparison.
- A5. **The schoolbook cannot name a Pacifican cause first**, because canon gives the Republic no objects of its own to have won. The first cause becomes "the South held" (W2).

## B. Design rules (the author's standing principles, applied here)

- B1. Reasons correspond to causes; the Council and Hawaii's delegation get clear, weighty reasons for a dangerous undertaking (CR-068, 10 November 2026).
- B2. Elias argues from capability, not fairness; he does not take the Union's frame; he holds his Republic to be just without needing it to be equal (Two Houses feedback, 11 November 2026).
- B3. Elias never relies on his adversary's honesty: he has a plan that wins against the odds (10 November 2026).
- B4. The aims of 1861 must foresee the terms of 1865. Whatever Pacifica keeps in 1867 is either named in 1861 or follows from a rule named in 1861.
- B5. Lincoln stays fair, honest and adversarial; he is not used as a mouthpiece against Elias's character (11 November 2026).
- B6. Victoria is not a sentimental brake. Hawaii's reasons are Hawaii's interests (CR-068 G2: her party concurs on the first three reasons and doubts the sixth).
- B8. The war is not a one-man or a two-man show. Elias's leadership is believable because he gets the right people on his team and uses what they know; every part of his plan answers *how does he know?*, and the people around him act on their own interests (author, 18 November 2026, 13:20; Part D).
- B7. The Union fights for what it values (no "unrealistic lack of confrontation", 8 November 2026). No annexation by decree (§9). Pacifica wages no campaign of extermination (protected intent).

## C. The aims of 1861

*Research citations in Parts C to F use the short keys of `outputs/sessions/20261118-cr075-war-aims/research-notes/README.md` (ELL Ellison, WAITE Waite, RICH Richards, SHER the Sherman letters, HUNT Hunt, ROS Rosenus, JONES Jones by chapter, KUYK Kuykendall II, LAV Lavender, WILL Williams, THOM Thompson, JOH Johannsen), with printed page numbers. Hist. = our world.*

**Proposal (PROPOSED).** The aims are written down in July, when the Council concurs in the reply: they form the preamble of its war resolution ("the objects of the Republic in this war"), printed with the reply and the seven reasons. They are five, in this order:

| ID | Aim | Whose it is first | What it asks of Washington | Seed of |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| C2 | **An equal treaty: the end of the convention of 1848.** The articles that came out of the naval confrontation (the storehouse, the warships' right of entry, the ports closed to Washington's enemies, the deserters, the renunciation of the Basin) give way to a treaty between equals, with a frontier drawn by consent. | California (Vallejo's) | A new treaty in place of 1848 and Carson | "The peace declares the conventions of 1848 and 1860 ended by the war"; the Western line replaces the renunciation of the Basin (CR-039) |
| C3 | **Free passage for Pacific ships.** No search of Pacifican or Hawaiian vessels in the Republic's and the kingdom's waters. | Hawaii's | The reply's first demand | Met by the peace itself (C8; CT2) |
| C4 | **The West's peoples by their own choice.** The Republic takes no one by decree. | C5 | **No conquest on this continent.** The Republic sends no army east and will lend to any American people that holds its own ground. | California's condition before it is Elias's rule (C8, E4); "his own first reason and Hawaii's last" (canon) | Nothing; a rule for the Republic | Head 5 ("nothing is asked concerning the South"); the Richmond credit |

The **price** is not an aim but is announced with them: what the Republic keeps it will pay for. That becomes head 3 (the public domain paid in gold).

- C6. **Why five and not one.** A single aim (security) would make 1865 look like loot. A territorial aim ("the Pacific slope to the mountains") would break the May resolution and repeat the Washoe defeat of 1860 at once. Five aims, three of them asking nothing that a Union fighting the South could not afford to give, let Elias tell Baker the truth: the reply asks more than Lincoln can grant (G2), and the aims say what the Republic will settle for.
- C7. **The aims do not grow.** Operations beyond the July resolution need a new resolution (CR-067). Each later step (the Deseret articles of December 1862, the Rio Grande compact of 1863, Washoe in 1864) is taken under C4 or the reservation of 1863, never as a new object. Where Elias acts without a vote, as with the invited guard at Salt Lake in 1862, Sherman and Victoria say so (canon, CR-060). The Terms of 1863 are the price list for the aims of 1861. That is what "the word held" means in W1.

### C8. What the research shows for each aim

- **C1 is credible because the Union's Pacific strength in 1861 was small, stationed and lightly fortified.** Hist.: the whole Pacific squadron was six wooden sloops, under 7,000 tons together, about 100 guns and under 1,000 men, half of them twelve to twenty-four years old, guarding the gold steamers and visiting Hawaii to protect the whalers (HUNT 301–303). There were no fortifications at all in Oregon or Washington; every federal dollar had gone to San Francisco Bay (HUNT 234), and in 1862 there was "not one fortified point" on the Columbia (HUNT 304). The regulars were withdrawn east from September and gone by 21 December 1861 (HUNT 26). Oregon's own Douglas press thought the coast stayed in the Union only because Washington held the sea (JOH 161). That Hist. fact does not carry over: in Pacifica the Bay was never American, so Washington's Pacific money went north, to the Columbia bar and Port Townsend after 1859 (CR-059 H7), and defences were strengthened after Washoe (canon §10). The posts are new, few and lightly built (Part C, C3.1). The Republic's navy is the change that makes the question open, and the squadron that has lost California's harbours lives at the Columbia, the Sound and Panama (canon; reason 2).
- **C2 rests on a real legal bind (E2).** Hist.: in 1861 Washington denied that a war existed and called the South's sailors pirates, while claiming a belligerent's rights of blockade and search (JONES ch.1–2); Lyons warned Seward that his port closure left foreign powers the choice between recognizing the South or submitting (JONES ch.1). An article binding California to close its ports to "the enemies of the United States in its wars" cannot be invoked without admitting a war. The device cuts one way only (E2's caution).
- **C3 is met by the peace, not by an article.** Hist.: the right to stop and search neutral merchant ships for contraband was a belligerent's right that neutrality conceded during a war (JONES ch.2), and the *Trent* turned on it (JONES ch.3); in peace a right of search existed only by treaty, as in the Seward–Lyons slave-trade treaty of 1862 (JONES ch.4). The reply's first demand asks the Union to give up a wartime right while it blockades, which it cannot (canon G2). The peace ends the right; after 1867 the Union also has no Pacific station. → CT2, Case A.
- **C4 answers a Pacific-republic current that existed, without adopting it.** Hist.: in 1860–61 every member of California's congressional delegation backed a separate Pacific republic at some point (WAITE 166, 189); Gwin and Latham drew its eastern line at the Sierra Madre and the Rockies (ELL 180); Congressman Burch's letter of November 1860 named its members as California, Oregon, New Mexico, Washington and Utah (ELL 181), very nearly Pacifica's outcome of 1867; New Mexico's delegate Otero wanted a republic uniting New Mexico and California (WAITE 172). But the Northwest's majority did not want a Pacific republic; it wanted to be left out of the war (JOH 163–164, 186–187, 197–209). → The narrower wording ("on the Pacific slope", C-3) is now in the table, and the broad reading has a domestic party behind it that Elias has to refuse (C-4).
- **C5 is California's condition.** Hist.: about as many Southern-born as Northern-born men lived in the state, and a newspaper of January 1861 argued that neither would take up arms against his native section (ELL 183); the Assembly's minority would not make California "the ally of one section" against another (ELL 185); the separatists knew their republic stood or fell with the South (WAITE 166, 169). In Pacifica the Republic can only fight a war that sends neither half east. Elias's rule is the condition on which both halves of California consent.

## D. The plan and the people: what Elias expects, how he knows, and who acts on their own

**The principle (author, 18 November 2026, 13:20).** The war is not a one-man or a two-man show. Elias is a great leader, and his leadership is believable because he gets the right people on his team and uses what they know. So every element of his plan answers the question *how does he know?* with a person, a document or his own experience, and every person on the team has interests and judgments of their own, and sometimes acts against him or without him. Canon already has the pattern: Ralston talks him out of an export ban and gives him the Steamer Day Agreement instead (CR-069); Sherman and Victoria tell him the invited guard at Salt Lake needed the Council's vote (CR-060); Young sends the Legion to Bannack on Deseret's own decision (CR-060); Deady overrules the garrison in the Willamette flood (CR-059).

### D-a. How the plan is made: the closed sitting of July 1861

*Superseded by Part D: two closed sittings in late June 1861, before the Council's night (D1, D-8).*

**Proposal (PROPOSED).** The aims are public; the plan is not. In a closed sitting of the Council in July 1861 the estimate is set out the way the Laboratory sets out a conjecture at a challenge evening (canon §8, CR-049): what is established, what is conjecture, and what would settle it. Each part is given by the person who knows it, not by Elias. An appointed respondent attacks it, and the respondent is Sherman, who supports the protection and objects to the growing objectives (canon §9). Elias's part is to ask the questions, put the parts together, and say what the Republic will do in each case. The minutes are sealed; the Chronicler has them. None of the plan depends on Lincoln keeping faith (B3).

This is the Polytechnic's custom used for war, and it is Elias's strength: §8 says he "continues to direct money more readily than he can command troops"; his skill is knowing whom to ask and how to test the answer.

**What the research adds to the respondent.** Hist.: in the first months of 1861 Sherman judged that secession meant war and that the war would be long and huge; that a free-trade South would undersell the Eastern ports, so the North would have to blockade; that volunteers were unfit for invasion and the first leaders would fail; that the Union and slavery must be kept distinct or the war would have no end; and that the task was to conquer so as to leave respect behind (SHER 106–120). In October he asked for 200,000 men to hold Kentucky alone and was called mad for it (SHER 133). The Pacifican Sherman can reach the same judgments without Louisiana. So the respondent does not only urge caution: he is the man who tells the Council the war will not stay small, and that a limited war stays limited only if its aims are written down.

## E. California's own reasons, and how California's assent is won

**Proposal (PROPOSED).** California's government agrees for reasons of its own, held by named people, and its legislature renews three times. The proposal gives each party in California its reason and its price. The research gives each reason a historical basis: in our world almost every argument below was made in California in 1860–61, for or against a Pacific republic.

- E1. **The lock is gone, and what a Union return would bring** (all parties). California's safety since 1850 rested on the sectional balance in Congress (§4). Secession removes it: for the first time since 1848 a Congress can vote a war on California, and its majority includes the men who called 1848 the Trist surrender (§9). What Polk expected to fall "of itself" can now be taken. This is California's reason 7, held before Elias states it. **What that means to a Californian voter** is concrete. Hist.: California's grievances against Washington in our world were the land courts that kept the old grants in litigation for years and ejected settlers without compensation, the neglect of its public lands and frontier, and the fear of a federal tax on the miners (ELL 4–35, 82–91, 182). In Pacifica none of these exists, because California governs its own land, gold and frontier; they are exactly what a Union that came back for the coast would bring. California fights for its register titles, its royalty and its own frontier policy.
- E2. **The convention of 1848 is a leash, and in 1861 Washington pulls it** (Vallejo; the Californios; the Republic's nationalists). New, PROPOSED: in May 1861 Baker delivers a note invoking the renewed articles: California's ports are to be closed to the vessels and agents of the insurgent states, and deserters returned. To comply is to join the Union's war. Elias's and Vallejo's answer turns the note round: the article covers the enemies of the United States "in its wars", and Washington calls the South a rebellion and its privateers pirates. Either Washington admits a war (and so the South's belligerency), or the article does not apply. Hist.: this was exactly the Union's position in 1861: it denied a war while claiming a belligerent's rights (JONES ch.1–2). **The caution:** the device cuts one way only. If Washington did admit a war, the article would bind California to close its ports to Confederate ships. Elias says so to Baker; the Republic loses nothing by it, since it bars privateers from its ports anyway, as Hawaii did by its rule of 1854 (KUYK 58). The note teaches California that every article of 1848 is a question Washington can ask and Monterey must answer. **Vallejo's arc:** the most pro-American man to sign in 1846, the signer of 1848, leads California into the war that ends his own convention, and signs its end in 1865 (canon §12). Hist.: Vallejo's own bitterness in our world was at the American lawyers who took the Californios' land through the courts, and in March 1862 he lost his Soscol grant at the Supreme Court (ROS 200, 219).
- E3. **San Francisco's money** (the merchants, divided). For: the Comstock is Californian capital under American guarantees at Carson, with Nye's volunteers at Fort Churchill; Oregon's flour, timber and coal and the Clearwater's gold come down the Columbia (reason 6); Pacific trade under a search regime. Against: the New York houses, whose treasure steamers stop in July (CR-069). Elias's answer to them is D4: the trade comes back at the peace, on terms the Republic will set. Ralston (P5) is the man who makes it believable to them. Hist.: in 1860 the city's men feared that war would cut the southern stage road, bring blockades and send privateers after the gold steamers (LAV 151); Volney Howard argued that a California free of the tariff and the navigation acts could ship its gold straight to Europe and raise $8–9 million a year in duties (ELL 184–185); the opponents answered that trade, people and ships all came from the North (ELL 184). In Pacifica the first argument has already won, and the second is the New York houses' case.
- E4. **The Americans in California** (divided). Southern men want a war on the Union for the South's sake; Union men want none. C5 holds both: the Republic does not fight for the South, and will not let it be conquered. The naturalized (Sherman's choice of 1857) have their own reason: they chose the Republic once and will not be reclaimed by a government that has suspended the writ (reason 5). Hist.: about as many Southern-born as Northern-born men lived in the state, and neither half, a paper argued in January 1861, would fight its native section (ELL 183); the Assembly's minority refused to make California "the ally of one section" (ELL 185); Southern Democrats in our world backed a Pacific republic chiefly to keep California from aiding the North (LAV 150), and the separatists knew their republic depended on the South surviving (WAITE 166, 169). Los Angeles's paper asked why the city should help "invade, impoverish, and despoil" its friends (WAITE 188). In Pacifica this half of the state supports a war against the Union only because C5 forbids a war against the South; the expansionists among them want more than C4 gives (C-4).
- E5. **Who opposes, and what it costs.** Sherman supports protection and objects to the growing objectives (canon §9); the aims give him a written limit to hold Elias to. The New York houses; the Assembly's party of 1856 that ratified the Articles narrowly; the families who send sons under the Defense Law of 1855. Hist.: in September 1861 the Union parties polled 86,980 and the anti-war Breckinridge Democrats 32,751, over a third of the vote, strongest in the southern counties (ELL 190–191); in Pacifica that third is the opposite of an opposition, since the Republic's war is against the Union, and the Union men are the ones to win. **Beale's test:** in June 1861 Beale wrote to Andrés Pico, a Breckinridge Democrat, asking whether he stood for the Union or a Pacific republic, and printed the answer (THOM 130–131). In Pacifica the Picos are founders of the Republic (§4); the same kind of public letter, asking the Californio leaders where they stand on the aims, is a way for the government to put opinion on the record.
- E6. **California's price** (OPEN; recommended (a)). Hawaii's price was batteries, frigates and a ship. California's options:
  - (a) **No new member without the founding states.** No land taken or associated in the war becomes a member, with a Council delegation, without the consent of both founding states. It meets the Californian fear that the conquered West will outvote the state that paid for it; it is the fear Lot names for Hawaii in 1865 (§12). It is also the answer to the Pacific-republic current (C8): California will not be swallowed by the republic Burch imagined. To check against the charters of 1866 and their ratification.
  - (b) The war paid from the royalty and the Reserve's gold, with no new Californian tax. Hist.: no-tax-for-coercion was the separatists' slogan of 1861 (ELL 183, 187–188).
  - (c) Sausalito's storehouse and Mare Island's yard to the Republic's navy at once. Hist.: Mare Island and Benicia were stripped by shipments east in 1861 (HUNT 20, 302).
- E7. **The renewals of 1862, 1863 and 1864 in California.** Canon has Hawaii's crises and none of California's. Recommended: one Californian renewal at risk, in May 1862, after Port Townsend and the Willamette flood, carried by Vallejo on E2 and by the first Columbia customs in the published Oregon account. OPEN. Hist.: the winter of 1861–62 brought the great flood that put Sacramento under water and stopped the Northwest's movements (HUNT 32, 231).
- E8. **Oregon beside California (new).** Oregon is not a member in 1861 and has no vote on the aims, but its people decide whether C4 works. Hist.: the Northwest's Breckinridge Democrats wanted neutrality, no part in a war on the South, and Whiteaker refused to back the administration (JOH 197–209); its Douglas Democrats called neutrality "cowardice" (JOH 194); Whiteaker's message of 1860 complained of Washington's neglect (JOH 161). C4's "will not hand back" is addressed to the neutral half; the Unionist half is reason 5's "people who trade with us" only in part.

## F. Hawaii's reasons (cross-check)

CR-068 G2 settled Hawaii's reasons and price (reasons 1 to 3, the batteries, the two frigates, the second turret ship; her party's doubt of the sixth; the seventh recorded as the Protector's own). This CR changes none of it. It adds C3 as Hawaii's aim by name, and the research sharpens two reasons.

- F1. **The neutral without a navy.** Hist.: in June 1861 a report of a Confederate privateer in the Pacific reached Honolulu; the American commissioner, on Seward's instructions, asked what Hawaii would do about Confederate ships in its ports; Wyllie answered that the king had no force to make his neutrality respected and that Honolulu, with millions in American whaling property, needed an American warship present; the king proclaimed neutrality on 26 August 1861, after Britain and France had done so (KUYK 65–66). → In Pacifica the dilemma is inverted. Hawaii has given its defence to the common navy (1856), and the American warship that would "protect" Honolulu is the one that boarded its packet. Reasons 1 and 3 are this history turned round.
- F2. **The war is Hawaii's sugar boom.** Hist.: Hawaiian sugar expanded fastest in 1861–66 because of the war; prices rose from June 1861, and in 1864–65 the best sold in San Francisco at up to 13½ cents a pound, partly offset by the American war tariff; whaling was halved by the war and the Confederate cruisers (KUYK 138–146). → In Pacifica Hawaiian sugar enters California inside the Confederation's customs union, with no American tariff: Hawaii's planters gain from the war that Hawaii's government enters with doubts. That is why the army's fixed-price sugar contract of 1864 and Lincoln's offer of duty-free sugar the same spring (canon §12, CR-068) both reach Honolulu where it matters.
- F3. **Wyllie's estimate.** Hist.: Wyllie believed in 1861 that the South would win its independence and wanted belligerent rights granted; the king refused anything that looked like recognition (KUYK 65). → Wyllie is a source for D1 and D5 (P9), and the voice for recognizing Richmond early; the king and Victoria are the check, as in canon (recognition in May 1865).
- F4. **The law of the sea already Hawaii's.** Hist.: Hawaii acceded in 1855 to the Russo-American principles (free ships make free goods; neutral goods in enemy ships free) and did not accede to the Declaration of Paris until 1867 (KUYK 63–65). Canon's Confederation note of 1856 (CR-074: Paris's rules on goods kept, letters of marque reserved) is Hawaii's own position. If Hawaii wants C3 in writing at the peace, the model is this principle, not a ban on search (CT2).

## H. The schoolbook formula

With aims of its own, the first sentence can be Pacifica's. Candidates (to replace W2; A10 follows):

- **H1 (recommended).** *"We held the river. Our coin held. Our word held."* (1) *We held the river:* the Americans could not take back the West where the Republic chose to stand, on the Columbia, in the passes and on the Rio Grande, and they could never spare the men, because the Founder saw to it that their other war lasted. (2) *Our coin held:* we paid in gold and they in paper, so the West chose our money, and in the end so did their Treasury. (3) *Our word held:* the Founder said in 1861 what we were fighting for and in 1863 what peace would cost, and asked nothing more when we were winning.
- **H2.** *"We fought for three things and won them all: no foreign navy on our coast, no unequal treaty, and the West's right to choose."* Then the three causes.
- **H3.** *"The Founder knew the Americans could not fight two wars, and he made sure they had to."* The teacher's line, prouder and harder; the Chronicler's W4 answers it.

The Chronicler's W1 keeps "the South held" as his first cause; the schoolbook and the Chronicler disagreeing about the first cause is part of the design (W4).
