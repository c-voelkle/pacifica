## 0. What canon already fixes

- **1848:** the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention (§4, CR-028): American warships may use San Francisco, Monterey and San Diego, with a revocable storehouse at Sausalito; California's ports are closed to the enemies of the United States in its wars; deserters from American warships are returned; American residents have civil guarantees; most-favoured-nation commerce; the Basin is renounced pending a boundary commission. The commercial and naval articles run twelve years. Vallejo signs it; Buchanan's guarantee of independence is refused ("a guarantor has a right to come in").
- **1850:** the Treaty of Monterey fixes the eastern line; California gives up the Basin. The Republic's safety rests on the sectional lock: Southern senators will not admit a free California, Northern Whigs will not conquer one (§4, CR-028).
- **1859:** the House refuses Oregon's admission; Buchanan's negotiators ask for a stronger privilege at Sausalito, and Pacifica refuses (§8, CR-059).
- **1860:** Washoe. Hays's crossing, Victoria's defeat of Article XIV, the Carson Convention: Washoe recognised as American, and the commercial articles of 1848 renewed for a further term (§8, CR-028). Washington reinforces the Northwest.
- **1861:** secession removes the Southern bloc; "the men who call 1848 the Trist surrender sit in a Congress the South no longer balances" (§9, CR-028, PROPOSED). Lincoln refuses Seward's foreign war with Monterey among its objects (CR-068). Sausalito is sealed. Southern agents solicit bullion; Northern representatives ask the Confederation to supply or finance federal armies (§9). The packet affair; Article XIV; the Protectorship from 1 June; a May resolution for limited coercive operations, with no annexation by decree (§9). Lincoln's offer in June; the reply and the **seven reasons** (CR-068 G2); the Council concurs "on the ground that the American squadron's last Pacific stations are the Columbia and the Sound"; Hawaii's price (batteries, two frigates, the second turret ship). The offensive in mid-July; Congress recognises a state of war before 6 August.
- **1862–1864:** the Columbia held; the interior comes in by compacts (Oregon 1861, Deseret 1862–63, the Rio Grande 1863); the Richmond credit "lent for independence" (February 1863); the **Terms of Monterey**, six heads, August 1863, "neither raised nor lowered by the fortunes of the war"; Washoe retaken in the spring of 1864 under the first reservation (CR-060, CR-068 G6–G7).
- **1865–1867:** the armistice (May 1865); the Western treaty (December 1865, ratified 9 April 1867): the conventions of 1848 and 1860 ended, the Western line in place of the Basin's renunciation, Port Townsend and Fort Union evacuated, no storehouse anywhere, the ports open on published terms, $40 million in gold, the claims waived (CR-039, CR-062).
- **The verdicts:** the Chronicler's three causes (CR-068 W1, APPROVED); the schoolbook's formula (W2, **deferred**: it "should be more patriotic and not give the win to another country in the first sentence").

The end state and the sequence are fixed. This CR supplies **what the Republic set out to win, why each government agreed, and the plan by which Elias expected to win it**, so that 1865 is the result of 1861 and not its afterthought.

## A. The gap

- A1. **The aims as canon has them are a reply, not a purpose.** In July 1861 they are the reply's three demands (no search in Pacifican and Hawaiian waters; no prosecution of the Columbia's inhabitants who traded with the Republic, and the writ open to them; Oregon's convention to meet with no army in Oregon) and the removal of the squadron's two stations. Nothing in 1861 names the interior, the convention of 1848 or the price, yet all three are in the Terms of 1863 and the treaty of 1865.
- A2. **The outcome is much larger than the aims.** The Columbia and the Sound in 1861 become the Northwest, the Basin, Deseret, Idaho, western Montana, Washoe, New Mexico to 103°W, Arizona and the Western Slope by 1865. Between them stand only "choices rather than inevitability" (§9) and the Terms. Read cold, that looks like territory picked up along the way: the "Ajax/Odysseus" risk again, at the scale of the war.
- A3. **California's reasons are Elias's arguments.** CR-068 assigns California the second, fifth and sixth of the seven reasons (the squadron on its coast; Oregon's merchants; the river's wheat, timber, coal and gold). No Californian actor wants anything in his own words; Vallejo only "supports California's assent". California pays most of the war's men and gold, and its legislature renews the Protectorship three times, but canon has no Californian renewal debate.
- A4. **The Chronicler's frame leans on Lincoln.** M9: "the moment Lincoln is more reasonable than Elias and Elias knows it"; G2: "He chose a war for the West against an adversary who had offered him redress." With no aims of its own, the Republic's war can only be measured against Lincoln's offer, and it loses that comparison.
- A5. **The schoolbook cannot name a Pacifican cause first**, because canon gives the Republic no objects of its own to have won. The first cause becomes "the South held" (W2).

## B. Design rules (the author's standing principles, applied here)

- B1. Reasons correspond to causes; the Council and Hawaii's delegation get clear, weighty reasons for a dangerous undertaking (CR-068, 10 November 2026).
- B2. Elias argues from capability, not fairness; he does not take the Union's frame; he holds his Republic to be just without needing it to be equal (Two Houses feedback, 11 November 2026).
- B3. Elias never relies on his adversary's honesty: he has a plan that wins against the odds (10 November 2026).
- B4. The aims of 1861 must foresee the terms of 1865. Whatever Pacifica keeps in 1867 is either named in 1861 or follows from a rule named in 1861.
- B5. Lincoln stays fair, honest and adversarial; he is not used as a mouthpiece against Elias's character (11 November 2026).
- B6. Victoria is not a sentimental brake. Hawaii's reasons are Hawaii's interests (CR-068 G2: her party concurs on the first three reasons and doubts the sixth).
- B7. The Union fights for what it values (no "unrealistic lack of confrontation", 8 November 2026). No annexation by decree (§9). Pacifica wages no campaign of extermination (protected intent).

## C. The aims of 1861

**Proposal (PROPOSED).** The aims are written down in July, when the Council concurs in the reply: they form the preamble of its war resolution ("the objects of the Republic in this war"), printed with the reply and the seven reasons. They are five, in this order:

| ID | Aim | Whose it is first | What it asks of Washington | Seed of |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | **No American arms on the Pacific slope.** No naval station, storehouse, garrison or fortified post from which the Union can strike the Confederation: the Columbia, the Sound, and the storehouse at Sausalito. | Both; the Council's stated ground (canon) | Evacuation of the last stations; no storehouse anywhere | The evacuation of Port Townsend and Fort Union; "no storehouse is granted anywhere" (1865) |
| C2 | **An equal treaty: the end of the convention of 1848.** The articles that came out of the naval confrontation (the storehouse, the warships' right of entry, the ports closed to Washington's enemies, the deserters, the renunciation of the Basin) give way to a treaty between equals, with a frontier drawn by consent. | California (Vallejo's) | A new treaty in place of 1848 and Carson | "The peace declares the conventions of 1848 and 1860 ended by the war"; the Western line replaces the renunciation of the Basin (CR-039) |
| C3 | **Free passage for Pacific ships.** No search of Pacifican or Hawaiian vessels in the Republic's and the kingdom's waters. | Hawaii's | The reply's first demand | See CT2 |
| C4 | **The West's peoples by their own choice.** The Republic takes no one by decree. It will not hand back any people west of the plains that has chosen to trade or associate with it and can hold its own ground, and it will protect those who are punished for having done so. | Elias's, with California's merchants | The reply's second and third demands | Head 2 of the Terms of 1863 ("each territory's future is settled by the act of its own inhabitants"); the compacts; the charters of 1866 |
| C5 | **No conquest on this continent.** The Republic sends no army east and will lend to any American people that holds its own ground. | Elias's ("his own first reason and Hawaii's last") | Nothing; a rule for the Republic | Head 5 ("nothing is asked concerning the South"); the Richmond credit |

The **price** is not an aim but is announced with them: what the Republic keeps it will pay for. That becomes head 3 (the public domain paid in gold).

- C6. **Why five and not one.** A single aim (security) would make 1865 look like loot. A territorial aim ("the Pacific slope to the mountains") would break the May resolution and repeat the Washoe defeat of 1860 at once. Five aims, three of them asking nothing that a Union fighting the South could not afford to give, let Elias tell Baker the truth: the reply asks more than Lincoln can grant (G2), and the aims say what the Republic will settle for.
- C7. **The aims do not grow.** Operations beyond the July resolution need a new resolution (CR-067). Each later resolution (the Deseret articles of December 1862, the Rio Grande compact of 1863, Washoe in 1864) is passed under C4 or the reservation of 1863, never as a new object. The Terms of 1863 are the price list for the aims of 1861. That is what "the word held" means in W1.

**Questions:**
- C-1. Five aims as above, or fewer (C3 folded into C1; C5 left as a rule outside the aims)?
- C-2. Is the preamble public in July 1861 (recommended: yes, it is Elias's protection against the charge of 1863), or given to the Council in closed session and printed only in 1863?
- C-3. C4's wording: "west of the plains" commits the Republic to the Rockies in 1861. Alternative: "on the Pacific slope", with the Basin and the Rockies coming in under C4 only when their peoples ask (Deseret's initiative of 1862). Recommended: the narrower wording, because canon has Deseret and the Rio Grande coming to the Republic, not the reverse.

## D. Elias's estimate: how he expects to win

**Proposal (PROPOSED).** The aims are public; the plan is not. In a closed sitting of the Council in July 1861 Elias gives his estimate of how the war is won and what he will do if he is wrong. Its minutes are sealed; the Chronicler has them. The estimate is his answer to B3: none of it depends on Lincoln keeping faith.

- D1. **The Union cannot fight two wars and will not try.** Its war is a sequence: the South first, then the West. It will keep the West cheap (CR-068 G3 confirms this expectation in practice). Pacifica's task is to make the West unwinnable cheaply and the South unwinnable soon.
- D2. **The West cannot be reinforced cheaply.** By sea through Panama, against the turret ships; overland, across a thousand miles of plains and passes the Union does not supply. What reaches the Sound comes in regiments, not armies.
- D3. **The interior follows its supplies.** The mining camps of Idaho, Washoe and Montana and the Saints at Salt Lake are fed from Sacramento, Portland and San Francisco and paid in Californian coin. Once the Columbia is held and the Union's war has cut the overland roads, the interior has to choose its market; C4 tells it the Republic will not force the choice.
- D4. **The coin.** The coast's gold stops going east (CR-069). A Union paying in paper will in the end need gold more than it needs the West, and the price will be named in advance so that it can be paid.
- D5. **The Union's own calendar.** It holds elections in wartime. If the South holds until November 1864, a Union government may treat; Pacifica must have its terms in print by then and ask nothing more after (G6, G8).
- D6. **If he is wrong.** If the South falls in 1862 or 1863, the Republic holds the river and the passes and fights a longer, dearer war it can still win (the reasoning CR-068 G8 gives for 1864, here given first in 1861). If the Union treats early, the aims are the terms.
- D7. **What the estimate gets wrong** (so that he is clever, not prophetic): Port Townsend holds (§10); the Union fights hard on the Rio Grande (CR-061); the Saints come on their own terms and Young will not be commanded (CR-060); Washoe needs arms in 1864, against C4's spirit (W4); and the war lasts a year longer than he planned.

**Questions:**
- D-1. A sealed estimate, or does Elias give it only to Marcus and Sherman? Recommended: the Council in closed session, so that Hawaii's delegation and Vallejo's government agree to a plan and not only to aims.
- D-2. Is D6's fallback (the long war) in the estimate of 1861, or does it first appear in 1864 as canon has it? Recommended: both: a sentence in 1861, the particulars in 1864.

## E. California's own reasons, and how California's assent is won

**Proposal (PROPOSED).** California's government agrees for reasons of its own, held by named people, and its legislature renews three times. The proposal gives each party in California its reason and its price.

- E1. **The lock is gone** (all parties). California's safety since 1850 rested on the sectional balance in Congress (§4). Secession removes it: for the first time since 1848 a Congress can vote a war on California, and its majority includes the men who called 1848 the Trist surrender (§9). What Polk expected to fall "of itself" can now be taken. This is California's reason 7, held before Elias states it.
- E2. **The convention of 1848 is a leash, and in 1861 Washington pulls it** (Vallejo; the Californios; the Republic's nationalists). New, PROPOSED: in May 1861 Baker delivers a note invoking the renewed articles: California's ports are to be closed to the vessels and agents of the insurgent states, and deserters returned. To comply is to join the Union's war. Elias's and Vallejo's answer turns the note round: the article covers the enemies of the United States "in its wars", and Washington calls the South a rebellion and its privateers pirates. Either Washington admits a war (and so the South's belligerency), or the article does not apply (Hist., to verify: the belligerency question of 1861 and the Prize Cases of 1863; research R3). The note teaches California that every article of 1848 is a question Washington can ask and Monterey must answer. **Vallejo's arc:** the most pro-American man to sign in 1846, the signer of 1848, leads California into the war that ends his own convention, and signs its end in 1865 (canon §12).
- E3. **San Francisco's money** (the merchants, divided). For: the Comstock is Californian capital under American guarantees at Carson, with Nye's volunteers at Fort Churchill; Oregon's flour, timber and coal and the Clearwater's gold come down the Columbia (reason 6); Pacific trade under a search regime. Against: the New York houses, whose treasure steamers stop in July (CR-069). Elias's answer to them is D4: the trade comes back at the peace, on terms the Republic will set.
- E4. **The Americans in California** (divided). Southern men want a war on the Union for the South's sake; Union men want none. C5 holds both: the Republic does not fight for the South, and will not let it be conquered. The naturalized (Sherman's choice of 1857) have their own reason: they chose the Republic once and will not be reclaimed by a government that has suspended the writ (reason 5).
- E5. **Who opposes, and what it costs.** Sherman supports protection and objects to the growing objectives (canon §9); the aims give him a written limit to hold Elias to. The New York houses; the Assembly's party of 1856 that ratified the Articles narrowly; the families who send sons under the Defense Law of 1855.
- E6. **California's price** (OPEN; recommended (a)). Hawaii's price was batteries, frigates and a ship. California's options:
  - (a) **No new member without the founding states.** No land taken or associated in the war becomes a member, with a Council delegation, without the consent of both founding states. It meets the Californian fear that the conquered West will outvote the state that paid for it; it is the fear Lot names for Hawaii in 1865 (§12). To check against the charters of 1866 and their ratification.
  - (b) The war paid from the royalty and the Reserve's gold, with no new Californian tax.
  - (c) Sausalito's storehouse and Mare Island's yard to the Republic's navy at once.
- E7. **The renewals of 1862, 1863 and 1864 in California.** Canon has Hawaii's crises and none of California's. Recommended: one Californian renewal at risk, in May 1862, after Port Townsend and the Willamette flood, carried by Vallejo on E2 and by the first Columbia customs in the published Oregon account. OPEN.

## H. The schoolbook formula

With aims of its own, the first sentence can be Pacifica's. Candidates (to replace W2; A10 follows):

- **H1 (recommended).** *"We held the river. Our coin held. Our word held."* (1) *We held the river:* the Americans could not take back the West where the Republic chose to stand, on the Columbia, in the passes and on the Rio Grande, and they could never spare the men, because the Founder saw to it that their other war lasted. (2) *Our coin held:* we paid in gold and they in paper, so the West chose our money, and in the end so did their Treasury. (3) *Our word held:* the Founder said in 1861 what we were fighting for and in 1863 what peace would cost, and asked nothing more when we were winning.
- **H2.** *"We fought for three things and won them all: no foreign navy on our coast, no unequal treaty, and the West's right to choose."* Then the three causes.
- **H3.** *"The Founder knew the Americans could not fight two wars, and he made sure they had to."* The teacher's line, prouder and harder; the Chronicler's W4 answers it.

The Chronicler's W1 keeps "the South held" as his first cause; the schoolbook and the Chronicler disagreeing about the first cause is part of the design (W4).

**Questions:** H-1. H1, H2, H3, or a combination (H2 as the heading, H1 as the three causes)?

## Canon tensions

### CT1 — The Chronicler's verdict on 1861 (CR-068 M9 and G2)

**Current:** M9: "the moment Lincoln is more reasonable than Elias and Elias knows it: a limited war becomes a territorial one because Elias refuses the offer." G2: "He chose a war for the West against an adversary who had offered him redress."
**Case A (adapt):** keep both lines; the aims stand beside them, and the reader weighs them.
**Case B (amend):** M9 and G2's verdict become: Lincoln's offer was honest and answered the wrong that had been done; it could not answer what the Republic went to war for, which the reply and its preamble said; the choice was the founder's. No fact changes.
**Recommendation:** B. A keeps the frame of A4, which the aims exist to replace.

### CT2 — The reply's first demand and the treaty (C3)

**Current:** the reply asks Washington to renounce search in Pacifican and Hawaiian waters; the treaty of 1865 as canon has it says nothing about search.
**Case A (adapt):** C3 is met in substance by C1: after 1867 the Union has no Pacific station from which to search anyone. The treaty is silent, and the schoolbook does not claim it.
**Case B (amend):** the treaty of 1865 gains an article on Pacific waters: no visit or search of Pacifican or Hawaiian vessels within named limits in peace or war. To check against the Senate debate of 1867 (CR-062).
**Recommendation:** A, unless the author wants Hawaii's aim won in writing; then B.

### CT3 — CR-068's assignment of the seven reasons

**Current:** CR-068 CT3 records California's reasons as the second, fifth and sixth of the seven.
**Amended:** California's reasons are E1 to E4; the seven reasons stay Elias's arguments to the Council, unchanged. A note in CR-068's record, no change to the storyline's seven reasons. No fact changes.
**Recommendation:** amend.
