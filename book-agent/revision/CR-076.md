## The author's brief

> For the next CR, I think it's important to document some of the cultural divergence between Pacifica and the historical American West, especially with regards to its "ability" centered warrior-scientist ethos and how this affected people's daily life. One thing that I think would have to be very different is the role played by alcohol and the debilitating and destructive condition of alcoholism. Of course Pacificans would not be teetotalers or try to prohibit the stuff altogether. There should however be a set of guardrails both in and outside of the homelands including clear social taboos against drinking alone, drinking in the day, losing self-control in public, along with legal disincentives such as progressive taxing, minimum unit pricing, promotion of alcohol-free social gathering spaces as alternatives to the saloon, possibly a state monopoly at some point. The framing should never be moralistic or take the shape of a crusade against sin, but rather always come from a scientific and public health perspective and argue for the physical protection of bystanders as well as the promotion of personal health and ability. Alcohol is considered ok in rituals where someone is truly successful and has cause to celebrate and relax, however its consumption is looked down on when someone hasn't really achieved much. It is never glamorized or viewed as a rite of passage or linked to romance or status and it comes with clear health labels that disclose long term health risks and present the consumer with an informed choice.
> The homelands should have their own regulated on-reservation sales with earmarked tax revenue funding healthcare and infrastructure while eliminating the incentives for border town predation. Chronic poverty and idleness should be avoided in all areas including the homelands. Tribal governance and norms surrounding alcohol should be respected rather than stripping the tribes of legal jurisdiction over outsiders selling high-proof liquor or using it as trade currency.
> Feel free to suggest more ideas that you think would help for this overall theme. (19 November 2026, 13:39)

## A. The principle (from the brief; PROPOSED as wording)

**A1. Not a sin, a measurable harm.** Pacifica never argues against drink as sin. It argues from two measured facts: a man in drink is less able than he was an hour before, and the harm he does falls on people who did not drink with him (the woman at home, the man on the next scaffold, the passenger on his train). The first fact is his own business until it becomes the second.

**A2. The test is the one the ethos already uses.** *On the Public Arts* asks of every work whether it stands. The same question is asked of a man's evenings: does he come to his work able? The Republic does not ask whether he is good.

**A3. Not prohibition.** Wine, beer and spirits stay lawful, made and sold at home. California's wine is an industry the Republic is proud of. What is regulated is strength, price, hour, place and information.

**A4. Never false.** The state may not lie (CR-049, CR-051). Its case against heavy drinking is made only with true figures, and its label says only what the Office can show. This is the sharpest difference from the Union's later "scientific temperance" teaching, which taught schoolchildren things that were not true (Part I).

**A5. The cup belongs to achievement.** Drink is the sign of a thing done, poured by others for the one who did it. Drinking without cause, alone or by day is what a man does who has nothing to show; that is why it is looked down on, and why the custom, not the law, does most of the work.

## B. Where the rule comes from ("how does he know?") (PROPOSED)

**B1. Taos, 1834–1837.** Simeon Turley, from the same Boonslick country as the Thornes, runs his mill and distillery at Arroyo Hondo north of Taos from about 1830; his wheat whiskey, Taos Lightning, buys the Plains nations' robes and the trappers' beaver. Reuben, a Santa Fe trader and mule man, deals at Turley's like everyone else on the Taos road. The boy sees what the robe trade looks like the morning after: a camp that has sold its winter's work for a keg. This is the picture Elias carries, not a sermon. *(Turley is historical; his trade and its Indian customers are documented. Reuben's dealings with him are a story assumption. Reuben is not made a drunkard, and his drowning stays his own choice of crossing, CR-066.)*

**B2. The diggings, 1848–1851.** The camps and Sacramento's saloons give Elias the second picture: killings over cards, claims lost drunk, and volunteer companies that muster in saloons to go after Native villages (the expeditions Vallejo's government refuses in 1851). The licence commissioners' mounted police (CR-033, PROPOSED there) learn that most of their killings start in a saloon after dark.

**B3. Lahaina, September 1859.** Historically, Alexander Liholiho, Kamehameha IV, shot his secretary and friend Henry Neilson at Lahaina on 11 September 1859 in a fit of jealous rage; Neilson lingered as an invalid and died in 1862, and the king's guilt darkened his last years. In this timeline Victoria, married to Elias ten months, is her brother's Kuhina Nui. The kingdom is spared a crisis only because Neilson lives. She draws her own conclusion from it, about her brother and about herself (G1). *(Research R3: confirm the part drink played; accounts differ between jealousy and drink, and the CR must not overstate it.)*

**B4. The fellows bring the science.** The travelling fellowships run from 1854 (CR-049). A Carquinez physician brings home Magnus Huss's *Alcoholismus chronicus* (Stockholm 1849; German 1852), the first clinical account of chronic alcohol disease, and later the Gothenburg company of 1865 (Part D10). A Pacifican mutual life office copies the British practice of keeping abstainers and drinkers in separate sections, so that by the 1870s the Republic has its own figures to set beside the British ones (the United Kingdom Temperance and General Provident Institution found claims in its temperance section at about 70% of expectation against about 96% in its general section). Pacifica needs no preacher; it has an actuary.

**B5. Deseret's city store.** Historically, Salt Lake City ran a municipal liquor store in the 1860s, renting Brigham Young's distillery from 1861 to 1867, and in 1863 the city council told Young that the liquor business brought in more than all city taxes. A grand jury later found the city still buying from Young and Wells at four dollars a gallon when better liquor could be had at a dollar and a quarter. Pacifica learns both lessons from Deseret when it accedes: a public store keeps the trade in hand, and an official who supplies the public store corrupts it (D10).

**B6. Hawaii's law and its insult.** Historically the kingdom licensed sales from 1838 and its penal code forbade any sale of spirits to a Native Hawaiian, a racial rule upheld by the kingdom's court in 1863 (*Rex v. Booth*) and repealed in 1882 under Kalākaua amid controversy. A rule that trusts the foreigner and not the Hawaiian cannot survive in a Confederation built on Victoria's kingdom. Pacifica replaces it with a rule for everyone (G1).

## C. The customs: the unwritten rules (PROPOSED)

These are carried by families, the halls, the Party's lodges, the Companies and the estates. The law backs a few of them; most it leaves alone.

**C1. Never alone.** Drink is taken in company, at a table, with food. A man who drinks by himself is understood to have something wrong with him, and his friends' first duty is to notice.

**C2. Never by day.** No drink before the day's work is done. Spirits are an evening thing only. *(Open: whether Californio and Hispano counties keep wine at the family table at midday as a county custom, Q-3.)*

**C3. Never out of hand in public.** To be drunk where others must deal with you (in the street, at the works, on the road, in the hall) is a public failure, more shameful than the drinking. It costs a man his standing in his lodge or Company, and an officer or examined official his post (D7).

**C4. The earned cup; no one pours his own.** At the proving of a work, a master's leave-taking (CR-049), a Company's return, a victory, an examination passed, the opening of a dam, the others fill the cup of the one who did the thing. Pouring for yourself is a small, recognised vulgarity. Japanese and Korean partners arrive with the same custom of filling one another's cups (*oshaku*), and in Hawaiian ceremony ʻawa is served by another hand; the convergence makes it feel old to every part of the Realm. A man who has done nothing that week can drink with his friends, but nobody toasts him, and he knows it.

**C5. Never glamour, rite of passage, romance or rank.** A boy does not become a man by drinking; he becomes one by his first proving, his journeyman's piece, his first season in a Company. Courting is done sober, in the hall, on the river, at the county showing; a man who plies a woman with drink is despised as one who cannot win her otherwise. Rank is never shown by what a man can afford to drink or how much he can hold; the man who boasts of his capacity is laughed at. The prestige media under Victoria and Kalākaua follow the same rule in their pictures and later their films (Part H).

**C6. What is admired instead.** The man who rows, climbs, surveys, wins the county contest and goes home clear-headed. Fitness belongs to the warrior-scientist ethos from the start (Lot's love of horses and rough country, Elias's stamina; CR-049's arenas for settler virtues, CR-071 H6.4).

## D. The law (PROPOSED; dates and numbers for the author)

**D1. The treaties' article (1851–52).** Every treaty of 1851–52, and every later compact, carries a spirits article: no spirits enter a homeland except as the nation's council decides; no annuity, price, wage or debt is ever paid in spirits; a trader who breaks the article loses his licence and his bond, and answers to the court the nation names (Part F). This is the Republic's own design, not the American Trade and Intercourse Acts' ban on liquor in Indian country (1832–34), which decided for the nations instead of with them.

**D2. No spirit ration.** The Republic's navy and militia have no daily spirit ration from their founding (the United States Navy ended its grog ration only in 1862, the Royal Navy kept it until 1970). The one issue is ordered after a victory or a deed of the ship, as the Royal Navy spliced the mainbrace; in Pacifica that is the only time it is issued. The Pacific Companies' Act of 1888 writes the same rule into every company's code (CR-074).

**D3. The iron road and the engine.** From the first Pacifican railways, a man on duty with drink in him is dismissed without appeal (the rule American railways later called Rule G). The same rule passes to the mines' hoists, the dams' switchboards and the ships' engine rooms. California, the most motorized place on earth (CR-052), adds the motor law in the 1910s: a driver in drink loses his licence, and the Office measures by breath or blood once the method exists (Widmark's method dates from 1922).

**D4. The ladder duty (Spirits Law of 1852; confederal from 1866).** Vallejo's government taxes drink on Elias's figures by strength, not by volume: each band of strength pays more per degree than the band below it, so beer and table wine are taxed lightly and spirits heavily. The duty is assayed: the State Assay Office (CR-073) tests strength as it tests gold. The Confederation's Measures Law of 1866 makes the ladder and the assay common to every member. *(The bands and rates are MODEL; Q-4.)*

**D5. The floor (1866).** No measure of pure spirit may be sold below a published floor price, and no seller may give a discount for quantity. Cheap drink, not drink, is what kills the robe camp and the mining town. The floor is printed on the bottle.

**D6. The label (1866, extended with the science).** Every bottle and barrel carries the Assay Office's strength and the floor, and from the first a plain statement of what the figures show. The label grows as the science does, and says no more than it can show:
- 1866: strength; floor; the life office's figures on deaths among men who drink daily.
- c. 1888 (the Office of Public Measures, CR-071): the liver, the nerves and the memory (Huss, then Wernicke 1881 and Korsakoff 1887); deaths by drink in the county tables.
- c. 1900: drink in pregnancy (Sullivan's Liverpool study of 1899).
- 1910s: the cancers of the mouth and throat as the clinics find them.
No picture, no slogan and no exhortation; the reader is presumed able to decide. *(The precise dates follow the research; R6.)*

**D7. Drunkenness excuses nothing.** A crime done in drink is punished as if sober, and more heavily where drink made it likely. A seller who serves a man already drunk shares liability for the harm he does that night. An officer, Commissioner, judge or examined official seen out of hand in public answers to his board or bond; repeated, it ends his career. *(The Groveland machinist, CR-051, already fits this.)*

**D8. The sobering room and the roll.** No one is jailed for being drunk. The police take him to the sobering room at the county hall (before the halls, the county surgery), he sleeps it off and pays a fine scaled to a day's wage. After the third time in a year a court may put him on the county's roll, and no store or house may sell to him for a term. The roll is the same for everyone, citizen or guest, of every nation and faith; it is a court order on a man, never a rule about a people.

**D9. Hours and credit.** Spirits are served in a public house only after the evening hour and never on credit; a debt for drink cannot be sued for. No employer may pay wages in drink or in tokens good at his own bar (the truck ban; consistent with CR-071's labour article).

**D10. The Spirit Stores (county option from 1867; Realm-wide from 1888).** From the accession of Deseret a county board may take the retail of spirits into a public store run on the Gothenburg pattern: salaried managers paid no commission on sales, a fixed modest return on the capital, the rest of the surplus to the county's surgery and, later, its hall. The store buys at public auction from licensed distillers and no official may own or supply a distillery that sells to it (the Salt Lake lesson, B5). The labour law year of 1888 makes the stores universal for spirits sold to take away; beer and wine stay in private hands under licence and the ladder duty. *(Name OPEN; Q-6. Alternatives: no monopoly at all; a monopoly from 1866; a monopoly that also covers public houses.)*

**D11. Age.** No sale to anyone under twenty-one. *(Q-7.)*

**D12. Where the money goes.** The ladder duty and the stores' surplus are earmarked by law to the county surgeries and the Board of Health, and from the 1890s to the county halls (Part E). Drink pays for the rooms where people gather without it. The Senate prints the account every year beside the county tables.

## E. The dry rooms: what replaces the saloon (PROPOSED)

**E1. Before the halls.** Honolulu's Bethel fountain and reading room (the Cold Water Army of 1843) are the first models. In San Francisco and Sacramento the 1850s bring coffee rooms on the Honolulu pattern, serving Hawaiian coffee from Kona (a Confederation trade from 1856), with newspapers in four languages, chess and the shipping lists. The Party's lodges meet dry from 1866 (CR-072). Chinese tea houses and, later, Japanese bathhouses give the cities more rooms where people meet without drink.

**E2. The county halls (1890s, CR-038).** Every county hall is dry by its charter. It is open until late, with the reading room, the night classes, the notices of work, the sick and burial funds, the gymnasium or boat shed, the dance on festival nights and the sobering room at the back. It is where young people meet. The saloon is not abolished; it is simply outcompeted in most counties, and the Office's tables show the counties where it is not.

**E3. Arenas.** Rowing, climbing, shooting, survey races, the county contests with declared terms (CR-049) and, from the 1890s, football and baseball leagues of the halls and Companies. Evenings that once ended in the saloon end on the river.

## F. The homelands (PROPOSED; the author's brief in detail)

**F1. The nation decides.** Whether drink enters a homeland at all, and on what terms, is the nation's decision under the treaty's spirits article (D1). Some nations keep their country dry; some license; some change their minds. The Realm enforces the nation's choice and never makes it for them.

**F2. Jurisdiction over outsiders.** An outsider who sells or gives spirits on a homeland in breach of the nation's ordinance answers to the court the nation names. Before a nation is chartered, the Realm's court enforces the nation's ordinance as written. Once chartered (CR-071's ladder), the nation's own court of first instance and its own police try him, citizen of a county or not; the Realm's courts hear only his appeal. *(This deliberately reverses the American path, where tribal courts lost criminal jurisdiction over non-members; R9.)*

**F3. The nation's own store.** A nation that licenses may open its own store on its land, run on the same pattern as D10, selling at the Realm's floor and collecting the ladder duty. It keeps all of it. The treaty earmarks it: half to the nation's clinic and nurses, half to its roads, wells and water, with the account printed in the Office's two columns beside the nation's other tables. A nation may move the shares between the two, but not out of them.

**F4. No border-town predation.** Three rules close the gap a border town lives on:
- The floor price and the ladder duty apply everywhere in the Realm, so liquor is never cheaper across the line.
- No county may license a public house or store within ten miles of a homeland line without the nation's consent. *(Distance PROPOSED; Q-9.)*
- On leased ground inside a homeland (Leadville's districts, Globe, Bisbee, Lewiston; CR-071), the lease sets the terms of drink, and the nation consents to them with every renewal.

**F5. Never currency.** No spirits are paid as annuity, price, wage, bounty or debt anywhere in the Realm (D1, D9). A contract that pays in drink is void, and a merchant who takes annuity claims for drink debts loses the claims (closing one of the leaks CR-033 names).

**F6. Against idleness and poverty.** Royalties and lease fees go into the nation's patrimony (CR-071), never as cash shares to every member; the patrimony pays for schools, clinics, the nation's enterprises and its raised companies (CR-074). Work is close at hand: leases worked by the nation's own men where it chooses, the Companies abroad, the county works beside the homeland, the Polytechnic's scholarships. The rule is the same as for everyone in the Realm: the Republic pays for able men, not idle ones.

**F7. What the tables still show.** None of this makes the problem vanish. The Office's county tables still show drink deaths higher on some homelands than in the counties beside them, and lower on others. The difference between the two kinds of homeland is printed, and the nations argue about it in their councils. Pacifica's claim is only that its figures are better than the Union's reservations and that they are true.

## G. Hawaii and the Philippines (PROPOSED)

**G1. Victoria and the kingdom's law of 1860.** After Lahaina, Victoria takes the pledge, not as a convert but as a woman who has watched her brother, and keeps it as Kuhina Nui and Queen except for the earned cup on the days the custom allows. She does not preach it. In 1860, as Kuhina Nui, she carries a new spirits law through the kingdom's legislature with Lot's support: the racial clause goes and one rule applies to Hawaiian and foreigner alike (the ladder, the licence, the hour, the sobering room). The missionaries' party opposes the repeal; the Hawaiian members carry it. The kingdom's law is the pattern the Confederation adopts in 1866. *(Victoria's drinking stays out of canon as the author decided; this is the mechanism.)*

**G2. ʻAwa.** Hawaiian ʻawa (kava) is not spirits. It stays under Hawaiian custom and the kingdom's own law, never under the ladder duty. *(The kingdom's historical attempts to criminalize ʻawa are not repeated.)*

**G3. The Philippines.** The Spanish wine monopoly caused the Basi revolt of 1807 in Ilocos, and Pacifica ends Spain's monopolies in 1876 (CR-029). So the islands get no Spirit Stores: the Philippine Council keeps its own law, and the 1883 Municipal Ordinance adds only the label, the floor and the sobering room. The towns may close their own tuba and lambanog shops by vote.

**G4. Japan.** Japan's own liquor tax is a pillar of the Meiji treasury and stays Japan's business. The Concord shares only the label standard (Hayama, 1928; PROPOSED).

- **H4. - **H5. The first splice.** A Pacifican ship's company drinks the only issue the navy allows, after a deed worth it (the occasion for the author).

- **H8. Kalākaua.** The historical Merrie Monarch drank hard. In this timeline his feasts are famous and his cups are always poured by others for something done: a voyage, a song, a launching. *(Q-12: whether he keeps a private weakness the Chronicler records.)*
- **H9. The Groveland machinist, 1922 (CR-051).** Already canon: the one drunk evening in the valley's history that matters.
- **H10. Historical figures.** Thomas M. Logan, the Sacramento physician who historically founded California's State Board of Health in 1870 and kept its vital statistics, keeps the Republic's drink tables from the 1850s (R7). Magnus Huss as the fellows' correspondent in Stockholm.

## I. The Union beside it (PROPOSED)

**I1.** The Union's temperance movement keeps its American shape: a moral crusade, the Woman's Christian Temperance Union (1874), compulsory "scientific temperance instruction" in its schools from the 1880s (which taught pupils, among other untruths, that a drop of alcohol was a poison), the Anti-Saloon League (1893). The Office's press prints the Union's school texts beside its own label, without comment.
**I2.** Whether the Union adopts national Prohibition (historically 1920–1933) is the author's decision (Q-13). If it does, Pacifica refuses to supply it: the Spirit Stores sell nothing for export over the line, and rum-running comes from the Gulf and Canada, not from Pacifica. The giant its neighbours need not fear (CR-058) does not make money on its neighbour's law.

## J. Questions this CR leaves for later

- **J1.** The Chinese and Japanese guest workers' bunkhouses and the gambling halls of the Chinatowns: the same rules apply; whether a later CR is needed.
- **J2.** Women's drinking: the brief's customs apply to everyone. No special rule is proposed.
- **J3.** Medicine: spirits as medicine (common in the period) are prescribed and dispensed by the county surgery only; patent medicines must carry their strength on the label from 1866 (a quiet blow to the "tonics" that sold alcohol and opiates in the Union).
- **J4.** Opium at home. CR-029 lets private merchants carry opium abroad. At home the consistent rule would be the same framework (strength assayed, label, floor, no sale by day, the roll) for smoking opium in the Chinatowns. This CR proposes nothing; it flags the question for the author.

## N. Storyline text (draft; all PROPOSED)

*§1, after "Reuben pays him in mules and favours to keep the boy out of the cantinas":*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** The cantinas are not the worst of it. Ten miles north at Arroyo Hondo, Simeon Turley, a Boonslick man like the Thornes, mills wheat and distils the whiskey the trappers call Taos Lightning, and Plains bands bring their winter's robes to his mill to trade for it. Reuben deals at Turley's like every trader on the road. One morning in the winter of 1835–36 the boy rides past the camp of a band that traded there the night before. He never describes what he saw, but he remembers it as the price of a keg.

*§5, after the treaties of 1851–52 (the Land Fund passage):*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** Every treaty carries a spirits article: no spirits enter a homeland except as the nation's council decides, no annuity, price, wage or debt is ever paid in spirits, and a trader who breaks the article loses his licence and bond and answers to the court the nation names. In 1852 Vallejo's government taxes drink on Elias's figures by its strength, not its volume, each band of strength paying more per degree than the one below, so that beer and the Republic's wine pay little and spirits a great deal. The Assay Office tests the strength as it tests gold. Elias's table for the council shows where and when the diggings' killings happen: most of them in a saloon, after dark. He does not argue that drink is a sin, and the distillers' petition against him cannot find a sermon to answer.

*§8, after the marriage of November 1858:*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** On 11 September 1859 at Lahaina, Alexander shoots his secretary and friend Henry Neilson in a jealous rage. Neilson lives as an invalid, and the king never forgives himself. Victoria goes to Maui and sits with Neilson. She takes the pledge that winter, without preaching it, and keeps it for the rest of her life except for the cup others pour her on a day that has earned it. In 1860 she carries the kingdom's new spirits law with Lot's support: the old clause forbidding any sale to a Hawaiian goes, and one rule of strength, licence and hour applies to Hawaiian and foreigner alike. The missionaries' party votes against the repeal and the Hawaiian members carry it. ʻAwa stays under Hawaiian custom.

*§12, with Deseret's accession (1866–67):*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** The Confederation's Measures Law of 1866 makes Hawaii's and California's rule common: the ladder duty, a printed floor below which no measure of pure spirit may be sold and no discount for quantity, and a label on every bottle with the Assay Office's strength, the floor and the life offices' figures on men who drink every day. Deseret brings the city liquor store Salt Lake has run since 1861. At the accession hearing the commissioners show the council the store's purchases: from the President's own distillery, at four dollars a gallon when better spirits sell at a dollar and a quarter. Brigham Young, who hates drunkenness and has made whiskey for the city all the same, keeps the store and gives up the distillery. From 1867 any county board may take the retail of spirits into a public store on the pattern Gothenburg adopted in 1865, with salaried managers, a fixed return on capital and the surplus to the county's surgery, and no official may supply it.

*§19 or §25, the customs (with CR-049's customs paragraph):*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** The customs that make skill visible also put drink in its place. The cup belongs to a thing done: at a proving, a master's leave-taking, a company's return, a dam opened or an examination passed, the others fill the cup of the one who did it, and no one pours his own. Japanese and Korean partners bring the same habit of filling one another's cups, and in ceremony ʻawa is served by another hand, so the custom feels old to every part of the Realm. A man who drinks alone, drinks by day or is drunk where others must deal with him has nothing to show and is known for it. Boys do not become men by drinking, men do not court women with it, and no one shows his rank by what he can hold. The Republic's ships have no spirit ration; the one issue is ordered after a deed, and the railways dismiss a man found on duty with drink in him.

*§25, after the labour law of 1888 and with the county halls of the 1890s:*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** The labour law year makes the county spirit stores universal for spirits sold to take away; beer and wine stay in private hands under licence. No one is jailed for being drunk: he sleeps it off in the sobering room and pays a fine scaled to a day's wage, and a court may put a man who comes back too often on the county's roll, which forbids any store to sell to him for a term. Drink is never sold on credit, wages are never paid in it, and a crime done in drink is punished as if sober. The duty and the stores' surplus go by law to the county surgeries and, from the 1890s, to the halls, every one of them dry by charter and open late, so that drink pays for the rooms where people meet without it. From 1888 the label carries the Office's figures on the liver, the nerves and the memory; from 1900 it warns against drink in pregnancy. The saloon is not abolished. In most counties it is simply outcompeted, and the Office's tables show the counties where it is not.

*CR-071's homelands and the nations' ladder (§25):*
> **[PROPOSED — CR-076]** Whether drink enters a homeland is the nation's decision. An outsider who sells spirits there in breach of its ordinance answers to the court the nation names, and once a nation is chartered its own court and police try him. A nation that licenses may open its own store at the Realm's floor and keep the whole duty, half for its clinics and nurses and half for its roads and water, printed in the Office's two columns. No county may license a public house within ten miles of a homeland line without the nation's consent, the floor is the same on both sides of every line, and on leased ground the lease sets the terms of drink. Royalties go to the nation's patrimony, not as cash shares. Some homelands stay dry and some do not, and the tables show both.
