## The author's brief

> For the next CR, I think it's important to document some of the cultural divergence between Pacifica and the historical American West, especially with regards to its "ability" centered warrior-scientist ethos and how this affected people's daily life. One thing that I think would have to be very different is the role played by alcohol and the debilitating and destructive condition of alcoholism. Of course Pacificans would not be teetotalers or try to prohibit the stuff altogether. There should however be a set of guardrails both in and outside of the homelands including clear social taboos against drinking alone, drinking in the day, losing self-control in public, along with legal disincentives such as progressive taxing, minimum unit pricing, promotion of alcohol-free social gathering spaces as alternatives to the saloon, possibly a state monopoly at some point. The framing should never be moralistic or take the shape of a crusade against sin, but rather always come from a scientific and public health perspective and argue for the physical protection of bystanders as well as the promotion of personal health and ability. Alcohol is considered ok in rituals where someone is truly successful and has cause to celebrate and relax, however its consumption is looked down on when someone hasn't really achieved much. It is never glamorized or viewed as a rite of passage or linked to romance or status and it comes with clear health labels that disclose long term health risks and present the consumer with an informed choice.
> The homelands should have their own regulated on-reservation sales with earmarked tax revenue funding healthcare and infrastructure while eliminating the incentives for border town predation. Chronic poverty and idleness should be avoided in all areas including the homelands. Tribal governance and norms surrounding alcohol should be respected rather than stripping the tribes of legal jurisdiction over outsiders selling high-proof liquor or using it as trade currency.

## A. The principle (APPROVED, Q-1; A6 from the author's answer to Q-16)

**A1. Not a sin, a measurable harm.** Pacifica never argues against drink as sin. It argues from two measured facts: a person in drink is less able than an hour before, and the harm falls on people who did not drink (the family at home, the man on the next scaffold, the passengers behind the horses or the engine). The first fact is the drinker's own business until it becomes the second.

**A2. The ethos's own test.** *On the Public Arts* asks of every work whether it stands. The same question is asked of an evening: does the worker come to the work able? The Republic does not ask whether anyone is good.

**A3. Not prohibition.** Wine, beer and spirits stay lawful, made and sold at home. California's wine is an industry the Republic is proud of. What is regulated is strength, price, hour, place and information.

**A4. Never false.** The state may not lie (CR-049, CR-051). Its case is made only with true figures, and its label says only what the figures show. This sets it apart from the Union's later "scientific temperance" teaching, which taught schoolchildren things that were not true (Part I).

**A5. The cup belongs to achievement.** Drink marks a thing done, poured by others for the one who did it. Drinking alone, by day or without cause is what people do who have nothing to show; the custom does most of the work, and the law backs it at a few points.

**A6. Human capital, not the world's salvation.** The rule exists to keep Pacifica's own people able. It covers every intoxicant sold at home (Part K). What Pacifican merchants sell abroad, drink or opium, stays under the ordinary law of trade (CR-029). The Republic says so openly.

## B. Where the rule comes from ("how does he know?") (APPROVED, Q-2; B1's link between Reuben and Turley and the morning on the Arkansas are story assumptions)

**B1. Taos and the Arkansas, 1834–1837.** Simeon Turley, a Kentuckian (born about 1806; sources give 1806 or 1809) whose brothers Stephen and Jesse trade from Arrow Rock in the Boonslick, runs his mill and distillery at Arroyo Hondo, ten miles north of Taos, from about 1830. Trappers come to the mill to trade furs for his wheat whiskey, Taos Lightning, and traders carry it north in kegs to the Arkansas posts, where it buys the Plains nations' robes. Turley is killed at his mill in the Taos rising of January 1847. Reuben, a Boonslick man on the Taos road, deals at Turley's like every trader; one spring on the way home along the Arkansas the boy sees a camp the morning after a keg was traded there. Reuben is not made a drunkard, and his drowning stays his own choice of crossing (CR-066).

**B2. The diggings, 1848–1851.** The camps and Sacramento's saloons give the second picture: killings over cards, claims lost drunk, and volunteer companies that muster in saloons to go after Native villages (the expeditions Vallejo's government refuses in 1851). Thomas Muldrup Logan, a Charleston-trained physician in Sacramento from 1850 who keeps the town's medical and weather records, compiles the table Elias lays before the council: where and when the diggings' killings happen.

**B3. Lahaina, September 1859.** Historically, after two days of drinking (aboard the *Maria* on Saturday 10 September and at the governor's house on Sunday), Alexander Liholiho, Kamehameha IV, went to Henry Neilson's house at Lahaina late on Sunday 11 September and shot his secretary and friend in the chest. Neilson was moved to Honolulu when it was safe, lived as an invalid confined to his room in Alakea Street and died on 12 February 1862; the king never forgave himself. In this timeline Victoria, married ten months, is her brother's Kuhina Nui. Lahaina's settled ship's surgeon, the father of the girl Kaʻanoe born there the next year, dresses the wound (story assumption; K10).

**B4. The fellows bring the science.** The travelling fellowships run from 1854 (CR-049). A Carquinez physician brings home Magnus Huss's *Alcoholismus chronicus* (Stockholm 1849; German 1852), the first clinical account of chronic alcohol disease, and later the Gothenburg company of 1865 (D10). A Pacifican mutual life office copies the British practice of keeping abstainers and other lives in separate sections. The British figures compare a self-selected abstainers' section with a general section of non-abstainers (the United Kingdom Temperance and General Provident Institution's claims ran at about 70% of expectation in its temperance section against about 96% in its general section); by the 1870s the Republic has its own figures of the same kind.

**B5. Deseret's city store.** Historically, Salt Lake City ran a municipal liquor store in the 1860s, renting Brigham Young's distillery from 1861 to 1867, and in 1863 the city council told Young that the liquor business brought in more than all city taxes. A grand jury later found the city buying from Young and Wells at four dollars a gallon when better liquor could be had at a dollar and a quarter. Pacifica learns both lessons when Deseret accedes: a public store keeps the trade in hand, and an official who supplies it corrupts it (D10).

**B6. Hawaii's law and its insult.** Historically the kingdom licensed sales from 1838, and its penal code forbade any sale of spirits to a Native Hawaiian, a racial rule upheld in 1863 (*Rex v. Booth*) and repealed only in 1882. The kingdom's Act of 1860 licensed the importation and sale of opium. A rule that trusts the foreigner and not the Hawaiian cannot last in a Confederation built on Victoria's kingdom; Pacifica replaces it with a rule for everyone (G1).

## C. The customs: the unwritten rules (APPROVED, Q-2/Q-3)

These are carried by families, the halls, the Party's lodges, the Companies, the estates and the Kaʻahumanu house. They apply to women as to men. The law backs a few of them.

**C1. Never alone.** Drink is taken in company, at a table, with food. Someone who drinks alone is understood to have something wrong, and friends notice first.

**C2. Never by day.** No drink before the day's work is done; spirits are an evening thing only. One narrow exception survives: in the Californio and Hispano counties, wine at the family's midday table on a feast day or a Sunday, never spirits and never in a public house. It becomes rarer with each generation as the halls, the schools and the works' hours set the day's pattern, and by the 1920s it is a grandparents' custom (Q-3).

**C3. Never out of hand in public.** To be drunk where others must deal with you (on the scaffold, on the road behind a team, in the street, in the hall) puts them at risk, and it is a public failure, worse than the drinking. It costs a person standing in lodge or Company, and an officer or examined official the post (D7).

**C4. The earned cup; no one pours their own.** At the proving of a work, a master's leave-taking (CR-049), a Company's return, a race won, an examination passed, the opening of a dam, the others fill the cup of the one who did the thing. At Carquinez the cox pours for the stroke; at the Kaʻahumanu house the women pour for the surveyor who rowed in the winning crew. Pouring for yourself is a small, recognised vulgarity. Japanese and Korean partners arrive with the same habit of filling one another's cups (*oshaku*), and in Hawaiian ceremony ʻawa is served by another hand, so the custom feels old to every part of the Realm. Someone who has done nothing that week can drink with friends, but nobody toasts them, and they know it.

**C5. Never glamour, rite of passage, romance or rank.** A boy becomes a man by his first proving, his journeyman's piece, his first season in a Company. Courting is done sober, in the hall, on the river, at the county showing; a man who plies a woman with drink is despised as one who cannot win her otherwise. Rank is never shown by what one can afford to drink or how much one can hold, and the man who boasts of his capacity is laughed at. The prestige media keep the same rule: under Victoria and Kalākaua in their pictures, under their successors in films (Part H).

**C6. What is admired instead.** The person who rows, climbs, surveys, wins the county contest and goes home clear-headed. Fitness belongs to the warrior-scientist ethos from the start (Lot's love of horses and rough country, Elias's stamina; CR-049's contests, CR-071's arenas for settler virtues).

## D. The law (APPROVED, Q-4 to Q-8; bands, rates and the floor's level MODEL)

**D1. The treaties' article (1851–52).** Every treaty of 1851–52, and every later compact with a Native nation, carries a spirits article: no spirits enter a homeland except as the nation's council decides; no annuity, price, wage or debt is paid in spirits; a trader who breaks the article loses licence and bond and answers in the Republic's court on the nation's complaint, under the nation's ordinance (Part F). This is the Republic's own design; the American Trade and Intercourse Acts' ban on liquor in Indian country (1832–34) decided for the nations instead of with them.

**D2. No spirit ration.** The regular army and navy organised in 1852 (§8) have no daily spirit ration (the United States Navy ended its grog only in 1862; the Royal Navy kept its rum until 1970). The one issue is ordered after a victory or a deed of the ship, as the Royal Navy spliced the mainbrace. The ration is coffee (Part L). The Companies Act of 1888 writes the same rule into every company's code (CR-074).

**D3. The iron road and the engine.** From the Republic's first railways (§13), a man on duty with drink in him is dismissed without appeal (the rule American railways later called Rule G). The same rule passes to the mines' hoists, the dams' switchboards and the ships' engine rooms. California, the most motorized place on earth (CR-052), adds the motor law in the 1910s: a driver in drink loses the licence, and the Office measures by breath or blood once the method exists (Widmark's method dates from 1922).

**D4. The ladder duty: California's Spirits Law of 1852.** Vallejo's government taxes drink by strength, not by volume: each band of strength pays more per degree than the band below, so beer and table wine pay little and spirits a great deal. The Assay Office tests the strength as it tests gold. Until the London pledge is released in 1859, the part collected on imported spirits at San Francisco is customs revenue pledged to the bondholders, whose agent welcomes the heavier duty (H3). *(Bands and rates MODEL.)*

**D5. 1866–67: who passes what (M1).** The common institutions hold customs, not police or excise at home (§8, §12). So the rule becomes common in two layers:
- **The Confederation's Measures Act of 1866** covers only what is confederal: the customs duty on imported spirits and opium, licensed importers, and the assay standard by which strength (and, for opium, morphine content) is measured.
- **The members' own acts, 1866–67.** California's legislature extends its 1852 law; Hawaii's legislature under Lot extends its law of 1860; each member that accedes in 1866–67 takes the same terms in its charter of accession. The members' acts set the ladder at home, the floor, the label, the hours, the sobering room and the county option. The duty collected in Hawaii stays in Hawaii's agreed revenues (the 1865 bargain).
- The German brewers of San Francisco and the wine counties fight the ladder in California's legislature in 1866 and lose; the bands are softened at the bottom for beer and table wine.

**D6. The floor (1866–67).** No measure of pure spirit may be sold below a published floor price, and no seller may give a discount for quantity. Cheap drink is what empties the robe camp and the mining town. The floor is printed on the bottle.

**D7. The label (1866–67, extended with the science).** Every bottle and barrel carries the Assay Office's strength, the floor and a plain statement of what the figures show, and the label says no more than they show:
- 1866–67: strength; floor; the life offices' figures, deaths among insured abstainers set beside deaths among other insured men. From the 1870s the Republic's own figures are added.
- c. 1888 (the Office of Public Measures, CR-071): the liver, the nerves and the memory (Huss; Wernicke 1881; Korsakoff from 1887); deaths by drink in the county tables.
- c. 1900: drink in pregnancy (Sullivan's Liverpool study of 1899).
- 1910s: the cancers of the mouth and throat as the clinics find them.
No picture, slogan or exhortation; the reader is presumed able to decide.

**D8. Drunkenness excuses nothing.** A crime done in drink is punished as if sober, and more heavily where drink made it likely. A seller who serves a man already drunk shares liability for the harm he does that night. An officer, Commissioner, judge or examined official seen out of hand in public answers to board or bond; repeated, it ends the career. (The Groveland machinist, CR-051, fits this.)

**D9. The sobering room and the roll.** No one is jailed for being drunk. The police take him to the sobering room (first at Sacramento under Thomas Logan's health board of 1862; at the county surgeries; from the 1890s at the back of the county hall), he sleeps it off and pays a fine scaled to a day's wage. After the third time in a year a court may put him on the county's roll, and no store or house may sell to him for a term. The roll is the same for everyone, citizen or guest, of every nation and faith: a court order on a person, never a rule about a people.

**D10. Hours, credit and truck.** Spirits are served in a public house only after the evening hour and never on credit; a debt for drink cannot be sued for. No employer may pay wages in drink or in tokens good at his own bar (consistent with CR-071's labour article).

**D11. The Spirit Stores (county option from 1867; universal by the Spirits Act of 1888; name APPROVED, Q-6).** From 1867, under the members' acts, a county board where county boards exist may take the retail of spirits into a public store on the Gothenburg pattern: salaried managers paid no commission on sales, a fixed modest return on the capital, the rest of the surplus to the county's surgery and later its hall. The store buys at public auction from licensed distillers, and no official may own or supply a distillery that sells to it (B5). In 1888, the year of the labour law, the Realm's chambers pass the Spirits Act: the Spirit Stores become universal for spirits and smoking opium sold to take away; beer and wine stay in private hands under licence and the ladder.
- **Hawaii, 1888–1907 (S2):** the Territorial Council has no hand in the stores. Victoria, as Queen, gives Hawaii's Spirit Stores to the Hawaiian Board of Public Health of 1886 (Noe's board), and the duty to the board's work. Hawaii's restored legislature takes them over in 1907.

**D12. Age.** No sale to anyone under eighteen (Q-7).

**D13. Where the money goes.** The duty and the stores' surplus are earmarked to the county surgeries and the health boards, and from the 1890s to the county halls (Part E). The Senate prints the account every year beside the county tables.

## E. The dry rooms: what replaces the saloon (APPROVED with Q-2/Q-8)

**E1. Before the halls.** Honolulu's Bethel reading room and the Cold Water Army of the 1840s are the first models. In San Francisco and Sacramento the 1850s bring coffee rooms on the Honolulu pattern, serving Kona coffee, with newspapers in four languages, chess and the shipping lists. The Party's lodges meet dry from 1866: David Broderick, who closed his New York saloon, emptied its casks into the street and swore off drink before he came west in 1849 (*hist.*), keeps them dry because votes bought with drink are votes the Party cannot count. Chinese tea houses and, later, Japanese bathhouses add rooms where people meet without drink.

**E2. The county halls (1890s, CR-038).** Every county hall is dry by its charter on ordinary nights. It is open late, with the reading room, night classes, notices of work, sick and burial funds, the gymnasium or boat shed, the coffee counter and the sobering room at the back. A county may vote the earned cup into its hall on its own festival nights, in keeping with the hall's own language, festivals and character (K7). The saloon is not abolished; in most counties it loses its customers to the hall, and the Office's tables show the counties where it does not.

**E3. Arenas.** Rowing, climbing, shooting, survey races, the county contests with declared terms (CR-049) and, from the 1890s, football and baseball leagues of the halls and Companies.

## F. The homelands (APPROVED, Q-9/Q-10)

**F1. The nation decides.** Whether drink enters a homeland at all, and on what terms, is the nation's decision under the spirits article (D1). Some nations keep their country dry; some license; some change their minds. The Republic enforces the nation's choice and never makes it for them.

**F2. Jurisdiction over outsiders.** An outsider who sells or gives spirits on a homeland in breach of the nation's ordinance answers in the Republic's court on the nation's complaint, under the nation's ordinance as written. Once a nation is chartered (CR-071's ladder), its own court of first instance and its own police try him, county citizen or not, and the Realm's courts hear only his appeal. *(This reverses the American path, where tribal courts lost criminal jurisdiction over non-members; R9.)*

**F3. The nation's own store.** A nation that licenses may open its own store on its land on the pattern of D11, selling at the floor and collecting the ladder duty. As a stated exception to the trustee rule, every nation keeps its store's duty itself, chartered or not; the treaty earmarks it, half to the nation's clinic and nurses and half to its roads, wells and water, printed in the Office's two columns. A nation may move money between the two halves, but not out of them.

**F4. No border-town predation.**
- The floor and the ladder apply everywhere in the Republic, so liquor is never cheaper across the line.
- No county may license a public house or store within ten miles of a homeland line without the nation's consent (Q-9).
- On leased ground inside a homeland (the leased mining districts and towns, CR-071), the lease sets the terms of drink, and the nation consents to them with every renewal.

**F5. Never currency.** No spirits are paid as annuity, price, wage, bounty or debt anywhere in the Republic (D1, D10). A contract that pays in drink is void, and a merchant who takes annuity claims for drink debts loses the claims.

**F6. Against idleness and poverty.** Royalties and lease fees go into the nation's account, held in trust by the Republic until the nation is chartered and then in its own patrimony, never as cash shares to every member. A chartered nation may charter companies (CR-074). Work is close at hand: leases worked by the nation's own men where it chooses, the Companies abroad, the county works beside the homeland, the Polytechnic's scholarships.

**F7. What the tables still show.** The Office's tables show drink deaths higher on some homelands than in the counties beside them and lower on others, and the nations argue about the difference in their councils. They also argue about the ten-mile rule: where a nation's store is the only outlet inside the band, county drinkers ride in to it, and some councils want the store and some do not. Pacifica's claim is only that its figures are true.

## G. Hawaii and the Philippines (APPROVED, Q-14; the Hayama label standard PROPOSED)

**G1. Victoria and the kingdom's law of 1860.** After Lahaina Victoria asks Elias for the Sacramento table, and he sets Thomas Logan's figures in front of her without comment. She knows by then that he drinks only when others pour. She stops drinking that winter, apart from the cup others pour her on a day that has earned it, and does not preach it. In 1860 she carries the kingdom's new law through the legislature with Lot's support: the clause forbidding any sale to a Hawaiian goes, one rule of strength, licence and hour applies to Hawaiian and foreigner alike, and opium is licensed under the same rule. The missionaries' party votes against the repeal and the Hawaiian members carry it.

**G2. ʻAwa.** ʻAwa (kava) is not spirits. It stays under Hawaiian custom and the kingdom's own law, never under the ladder.

**G3. The Philippines.** The tobacco monopoly ends in 1876 (CR-029), and no spirits monopoly replaces it: the Ilocos remember the Basi revolt of 1807 against Spain's wine monopoly. The islands get no Spirit Stores. The Philippine Council ends the Spanish opium farm (the *estanco de anfión*, which since 1843–44 had sold opium to the Chinese alone) and applies K3's licence to everyone. The 1883 Municipal Ordinance adds the label, the floor and the sobering room, and the towns may close their own tuba and lambanog shops by vote.

**G4. Japan.** Japan's liquor tax is a pillar of the Meiji treasury and stays Japan's business. The Concord shares only the label standard (Hayama, 1928; PROPOSED).

## I. The Union beside it (APPROVED, Q-13)

**I1.** The Union's temperance movement keeps its American shape: a moral crusade, the Woman's Christian Temperance Union (1874), compulsory "scientific temperance instruction" in its schools from the 1880s (which taught among other untruths that a drop of alcohol was a poison), the Anti-Saloon League (1893). The Office's press prints the Union's school texts beside its own label without comment.

**I2.** The Union adopts national Prohibition as in history (1920–1933). The Spirit Stores sell nothing for export over the line, and rum-running comes from the Gulf and Canada, not from Pacifica (CR-058).

## J. Left for later

- **J1.** The guest workers' bunkhouses and the gambling halls of the Chinatowns: the same rules apply.
- **J2.** Medicine: spirits as medicine are dispensed by the county surgery or a licensed druggist on a physician's order; from 1866–67 every patent medicine containing alcohol or opium prints its strength.

## K. Opium and the other intoxicants at home (APPROVED, Q-16 to Q-21)

**K1. The same rule.** Opium, morphine and later cocaine are treated at home as drink is: lawful, assayed, taxed by strength, sold only at a floor, labelled with what the figures show, and kept out of the working day (A6). Pacifican merchants still carry opium to China, and the Republic says so.

**K2. No law against a people.** Historically, San Francisco's supervisors passed an ordinance in November 1875 banning opium dens, the first anti-drug law in the United States, with fines of $50 to $500 and up to six months in jail, aimed at Chinese-run dens while patent medicines and morphine injections were left alone. In this timeline the supervisors pass the same ordinance in November 1875, and in 1876 California's supreme court strikes it: a room licensed under the state's law cannot be closed by a county for the people who keep it. The rule is the same for the Chinese smoker, the Californio with laudanum and the veteran with a syringe.

**K3. Smoking opium.** Under the Measures Act of 1866 opium enters only through licensed importers and is assayed for its morphine; the members' acts tax it on a ladder of its own by that content, with a floor and a label. From 1888 it is sold to take away only at the Spirit Stores. A smoking room is licensed like a public house: evenings only, never on credit, never within sight of a school, its keeper liable as a publican is. No contractor may pay or advance wages in opium, as railway and cannery contractors historically did with Chinese crews (D10).

**K4. Enforcement.** The county police and the licensing inspectors enforce the licence. The Chinese district associations are consulted, and the county hall's Chinese interpreters work with them; the Office prints the Chinese districts' tables beside every other's. The dens of the 1870s shrink as licensed rooms and the community's own pressure replace them.

**K5. Medicine.** From 1888 laudanum, morphine and, after 1898, heroin are dispensed only on a physician's order through the county surgery or a licensed druggist. The surgeons of 1861–65 learn the soldier's dependence on morphine, and the Republic's wounded are the county surgeries' first patients for withdrawal. Cocaine, sold freely in tonics from the 1880s, comes under the same rule.

**K6. The roll and the cure.** The court's roll (D9) bars a person from the Stores for opium as for spirits. Someone who asks for help is treated at the county surgery and keeps a place in lodge, Company or estate while treated; someone who hides it and fails at the work loses it.

**K7. Hawaii.** Historically the kingdom's Act of 1874 restricted opium to medicine, the Opium Act of 1886 licensed its sale, the licence led to a bribery case around Kalākaua in 1887, and the legislature repealed the act. In this timeline Hawaii's opium stays under the 1860 law and the Measures Act, then the Spirit Stores under the Board of Public Health; no licence is sold, and the bribery case never happens.

**K8. Abroad.** Pacifica's position at the Shanghai opium commission of 1909 and the Hague convention of 1912 is OPEN for a later CR.

## L. A coffee country (APPROVED, Q-22 to Q-28; figures MODEL)

**L1. Why coffee.** The dry culture needs a drink for the day and for company. Coffee is the drink of the working day, the hall, the survey camp, the ship's watch and the examination room; the earned cup is for the evening. Pacifica's crowns hold coffee land (Kona; the Philippines), Pacifica sits on the route between the growers of Asia and Latin America, and after the Gold Rush San Francisco became the western centre of coffee importing and roasting, home of the Pioneer Steam Coffee and Spice Mills (1850, later Folger's), Hills Bros. and MJB.

**L2. The cup in daily life.**
- The coffee rooms of the 1850s (E1) become a Pacifican institution: every county hall has its coffee counter, and a town's coffee room is its newspaper, labour exchange and debating club.
- The ration: the army, navy and Companies issue coffee in place of a spirit ration (D2), on the Union soldier's historical scale of about 36 lb a year.
- Coffee is offered to every visitor, at every board meeting and every compact council.
- Japanese students meet in San Francisco's and Honolulu's coffee rooms and take the habit home, where it becomes the *kissaten*; Japanese buyers take Pacifican roasts with it.

**L3. The science and the champions.**
- **Roasting and packing:** the San Francisco houses compete on the published-licence terms of CR-057; Hills Bros. introduces vacuum packing in 1900 as in history.
- **Soluble coffee:** Satori Kato (born 1847), the Japanese chemist who historically worked in Chicago and showed soluble coffee at the Pan-American Exposition of 1901 (patent 1903), works in San Francisco on a Polytechnic fellowship and shows it there in 1901. His powder goes into the Companies' kits and the 1914–19 war rations.
- **Plant pathology:** the leaf rust that wrecked Ceylon from 1869 reaches Batangas in 1889 and historically destroyed the Philippine industry, which in the late 1880s had been among the world's largest exporters. Here the Philippine Council's government and the Polytechnic's plant pathologists, practised at quarantine since the cholera of 1882, impose seedling quarantine and copper spraying and replant with the more resistant Liberica and Excelsa and with highland Arabica in Benguet and later Bukidnon. Batangas loses most of its old trees; the industry moves and survives. *(The degree of success is a story assumption; R13.)*
- **Hawaii:** Kona's smallholders (historically mostly Japanese tenant families by 1930; over a thousand farms in 1932) get Development Fund credit for a co-operative mill and the Polytechnic's grading; Hāmākua, Puna and Kauaʻi are replanted where sugar does not need the land.

**L5. The slump.** When Brazil burns its surplus from 1931, the roasters buy cheap Brazilian and Colombian stock, which fits the Reserve Bank's view that cheaper inputs are a gain (CR-057). The growers of Kona and the Philippines get nothing beyond the Development Fund's ordinary credit (Q-27).
