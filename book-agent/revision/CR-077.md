## 0. What canon already fixes

- **1855:** Sylvester proposes the Calculation Office and brings Babbage's notation and Lovelace's notes (APPROVED, CR-049).
- **1856–1860:** a Scheutz-type printing calculator. It computes particular tables well and nothing else (APPROVED).
- **1863:** Peirce's relay demonstrator. Relays in series and parallel are Boole's "and" and "or", so circuits are designed on paper first (APPROVED).
- **1868–1870:** relay arithmetic in service; **1872:** preset sequences; **1876–1879:** a programmed electromechanical calculator; **1878–1882:** a common procedural notation from Babbage and Lovelace (APPROVED).
- **1888:** Hollerith's cards tabulate Noe's registers at the Office of Public Measures. Peirce loses the work to them, and the Office borrows the method that beat it (PROPOSED, CR-049).
- **About 1895:** Tesla's estate and the Works build valve amplifiers for the navy's wireless and the counters. The valves of the 1890s are soft and erratic (APPROVED, CR-049, CR-050).
- **About 1908:** Langmuir makes the first hard-vacuum valves (APPROVED, CR-050).
- **1932:** "patrols, pickets, direction finding and early radio location"; "radio and ciphers protect the links"; "its confidence in its own information remains a weakness" (storyline l. 1609).

Between 1888 and 1932 canon has no computing at all. That gap is what this CR fills.

## A. How far it can get: the answer in one paragraph

Pacifica's lead was largest at the start. Its programmed relay calculator of 1876–1879 does what Zuse and Aiken did in 1941–1944, about sixty-five years early. That was possible because relays are telegraph parts and the Office supplied the idea and the demand. Electronic machines are different: they need valves that last, memory and a factory that makes both by the thousand. Canon puts the hard valve only five years early. History needed thirty-five years from the hard valve (1913–1915) to the stored-program machine (1948), mostly because nobody had both the idea and the need. Pacifica has had both since 1863. **Recommendation (level B):** a general electronic calculator in 1921 and the first stored-program machine in 1928. By 1932 Pacifica and Japan have about fifteen stored-program machines (MODEL), one of them in Tokyo, thousands of card and relay installations and a programming profession. That is roughly the world of 1951 in 1932, about twenty years ahead of history at the frontier. Two things stay in the laboratory: magnetic cores and a solid amplifier.

### A1. The three levels

| | A: cautious | **B: recommended** | C: maximal |
|---|---|---|---|
| First electronic arithmetic | 1915 | **1912** | 1910 |
| General electronic calculator (plugged, ENIAC-class) | 1926 | **1921** | 1918 |
| First stored-program machine | 1932 | **1928** | 1924 |
| Stored-program machines in service, 1932 | 1 | **about 15 (about 14 in the Realm, 1 in Japan)** | about 60, with business machines on drums |
| Main memory in 1932 | valve registers | **acoustic delay lines and drums; storage tubes in trial** | magnetic cores in service |
| Equivalent in history | 1946 | **1950–1952** | 1955–1957 |
| What it costs in credibility | nothing | the pairing of idea and demand must be shown at each step (Parts B–F do this) | cores need square-loop magnetic materials that history found only in 1949–1952, and solid-state physics that is barely born in 1932 |

Level C fails on materials, not on ideas. The fastest honest path runs through the bottlenecks below.

### A2. The bottlenecks, in order

1. **A switch with no moving parts** (relay to valve). Canon has the soft valve by 1895 and the hard valve by 1908. The soft valve's fault, gas that makes it latch on and stay on, is exactly what a counter needs (*Hist.*: Wynn-Williams built his 1931–1932 counters from gas-filled thyratrons). Pacifica can start electronic counting with the bad valves of the 1890s.
2. **Valves that last.** ENIAC's 17,468 tubes ran only once their makers kept the filaments on and ran them below rating (*Hist.*). That needs oxide cathodes (*Hist.*: Wehnelt, 1904) made by the hundred thousand, and only a broadcasting industry makes valves in those numbers. Pacifica's broadcasting begins in San Jose in 1909 (*Hist.*: Herrold).
3. **Memory.** A plugged machine holds its program in wires. A machine that holds its own orders needs a cheap store of a thousand numbers. The candidates are acoustic delay lines (quartz: *Hist.* Langevin 1917; mercury: 1944), magnetic drums (from Poulsen's wire, 1898), storage tubes (from the cathode-ray tube) and cores (1949–1952). Pacifica can have the first three by 1932 and not the fourth.
4. **People.** The Office has trained computers since 1855 and taught Peirce's algebra of circuits since 1886 (Part B). This is Pacifica's largest advantage, and it explains why the lead narrows only from sixty-five years to twenty.
5. **Money** is not a bottleneck. In 1929 research is about 2% of output, about $870m at 1913 prices (MODEL, `finance/CR-057-growth-workbook.md`). The whole programme of Parts B–F costs well under a tenth of one year's research (Part J).

## B. The exchange and the card (1886–1900) — PROPOSED

- **1886: the algebra published.** Peirce publishes the Office's circuit algebra as a teachable method. It covers relays in series and parallel, and the fact that one operation, "neither–nor", builds all the others. *Hist.*: Peirce described switching circuits for logic in a letter to Marquand in 1886. He found the single-operation result in 1880–1881 and never published it. In Pacifica both become the first course in the Office's school. In 1887 he writes on whether a logical machine reasons (*Hist.*: "Logical Machines", 1887).
- **1888–1893: the Office borrows the card** (gives form to CR-049's PROPOSED line). Peirce's answer to losing the registers is to make the card the relay machine's input and output. The Office and the Office of Public Measures agree one card in 1890, the Realm card. By 1893 the Office's relay calculators read their numbers, and then their orders, from cards. This is the card-programmed calculator that IBM sold in 1949 (*Hist.*). The registers stay Hollerith's; the cards now feed Peirce's machines too.
- **1891–1893: the automatic exchange.** The Realm's telephone exchanges are designed with Peirce's algebra, taught in the Office's school (*Hist.*: Strowger's patent, 1891). Who owns the telephone is left to the telecommunications CR (D-7). San Francisco dials in 1893, and by about 1910 most of the Realm's telephones dial. The exchanges become the largest machines in the Realm. They also become the relay industry and the school of relay engineers, as Bell's exchanges were for Stibitz in 1937 (*Hist.*).
- **1896: Hollerith's company.** Hollerith leaves the Office of Public Measures to found a tabulating company in San Francisco (*Hist.*: the Tabulating Machine Company, 1896, in Washington). Its customers are the railways, the insurers, the Reserve Bank and the county halls. The Genealogical Society of Utah (*Hist.*: founded 1894) puts its records on the Office of Public Measures' cards from 1894 and becomes the company's customer in 1896. The Saints become the Realm's second-largest keepers of records.
- **1897–1898: Michelson's analyser.** At Ralston College Michelson builds his harmonic analyser, first with 20 and then with 80 elements, for spectra and tides (*Hist.*: 1897–1898). Davidson's tide tables run on it. This is the analogue line, and it stays small, because the Office has learned to trust what it can check digit by digit.
- **1899–1901: the latch.** Michelson lends his valve-amplified counter (canon) from Ralston College to the Laboratory's counting room at Carquinez, and there it sticks: a pulse sets a soft valve conducting, and it stays on until the plate current is cut. In Kate Brandegee's counting room Peirce, past sixty, sees a relay with no moving parts. Two such valves cross-coupled hold one of two states. Within two years the counting room counts in pairs, a scale-of-two counter thirty years before Cambridge's (*Hist.*: Wynn-Williams, 1931–1932).

## C. The valve relay (1901–1914) — PROPOSED

- **1901–1907: counters.** Trigger counters count the neutron's recoils and the Works' activities at thousands a second. They are unreliable, and the counting room keeps a log of every false count. Kate's habit of independent checking (CR-049) now has a machine to check.
- **1908–1911: the hard pair.** Langmuir's hard valves (canon) make the two-state pair stable. His laboratory at Tesla's institute publishes it in 1910 (*Hist.*: Eccles and Jordan, 1918). Pacifica calls it the **pair**.
- **1909: broadcasting.** San Jose broadcasts from 1909 (*Hist.*: Herrold), to listeners on crystal sets. From about 1914 the valve receiver becomes a consumer good, and by 1916 the Realm's valve works make oxide-cathode valves by the hundred thousand. This needs only a line in the storyline; broadcasting itself belongs to the media CR (D-8).
- **1912: electronic arithmetic.** The Office demonstrates a valve adder and a decade ring, ten pairs holding one digit, that add in thousandths of a second. The relay machines take tenths.
- **1913: Peirce's last challenge evening.** In the autumn Peirce, seventy-four, is the respondent. He asks how anyone will know the fast machine is right. The answer the room accepts is Kate Brandegee's: every calculation is run twice by different routes, and the discrepancies are kept. The machine is held to the standard the register set in 1867.
- **1914: Peirce dies** at Carquinez in April (*Hist.*: 19 April 1914, at Milford, Pennsylvania, in poverty). The adder of 1912 is in the room's inventory under his name. His death was OPEN in canon; D-10 sets it.

## D. The war machines (1914–1921) — PROPOSED

- **The cipher room.** From August 1914 Pacifica is at war with Germany at sea (CR-074, the letters of marque). The General Staff's cipher room at Thalassia sorts and indexes code groups on cards and strips additives with relay machines. *Hist.*: punched cards were used against additive codes in the 1930s and 1940s. In 1916–1917 Leonard Fuller, born at Portland in 1890 and the engineer who builds the navy's transmitters at Tesla's power estate (which holds the navy's wireless in canon), builds the room an electronic coincidence counter for statistical attacks with the counting room's people. *Hist.*: Fuller was chief engineer of the Federal Telegraph Company in San Francisco from 1913 and built arc transmitters for the Army and the Navy. The room reads the Zimmermann offer of January 1917 itself. Canon (PROPOSED) already has it "intercepted and published through Thalassia".
- **Hebern.** Edward Hebern of Madera and Oakland draws an electric rotor cipher in 1917 (*Hist.*). The Office breaks his single rotor in trials in 1918. He adds rotors, and in 1921 the navy buys the five-rotor service design and its patent rights outright. Hebern keeps a commercial machine without the service wiring and sells it abroad. In Pacifica he is told that the Office can read the commercial machine, and he goes on selling it. The cipher room never reads a partner's traffic (D-6). *Hist.*: Friedman broke Hebern's machines for the US Army; Hebern patented in 1918, sold five-rotor machines for trial in 1925 and was never told why his sales failed.
- **The Russian war.** The railway brigade's movement tables for the line in one week (26 May 1918, CR-041) are worked on the Office's card machines at Thalassia before the line moves and at Omsk once it is held. The cipher room reads Red and White telegrams from the summer of 1918 (D-6). Britain's traffic of 1927 is not read in this CR; that waits on a check against the Petrograd account (CR-046).
- **1919–1921: the Carquinez machine.** After the peace the navy's range tables, the Weather Office's and the Office's own tables justify a general electronic calculator. The halls' ion orbits never go to it: the Works computes them on its own relay machines inside the wall, built from 1913. Fuller builds it at Carquinez with the Office's workshop, about 8,000 valves (MODEL), decimal rings, plugboards and card input and output. It runs from the spring of 1921. A new program is a new pattern of wires and takes days to set up.
  - Its first months are a fight with valves. A team of examined women, the Office's computers, change failed valves at night. The two-route check finds the machine's errors before anyone outside does.
  - Sophia Levy is the Office's first order-writer. *Hist.*: born at Alameda in 1888 to parents born in the gold country; a Berkeley PhD of 1920, an orbit computer and later the author of the University of California's anti-aircraft mathematics. She writes its first orbit and ballistic programs and keeps the first library of tested set-ups.

## E. The machine that holds its orders (1921–1928) — PROPOSED

- **The problem.** Every new problem means days of plugging. The Works, the navy and the weather men each want dozens of programs a year, and the Carquinez machine spends more time being set up than running.
- **1922–1925: memory.** Fuller's group turns the quartz plates of Langevin's echo-ranging (*Hist.*: 1917, published after the war) into acoustic delay lines. Pulses circulate through a column, a few dozen numbers in each tube and a thousand in a battery of them, and are read as they come round. From 1925 Hollerith's company builds a magnetic drum for the Office, a rotating store descended from Poulsen's wire (*Hist.*: the telegraphone, 1898; Tauschek's drum patent, 1932).
- **1926–1927: von Neumann.** John von Neumann arrives about 1926 (canon). In 1927 his report on a machine whose store holds its orders and its numbers alike is read at a challenge evening with Levy as respondent. *Hist.*: the First Draft on the EDVAC, 1945, which drew on Eckert's and Mauchly's engineering. Levy's objection is that orders which can change themselves can also corrupt themselves. The room's answer is the Office's rule: a program is tested by a second route before its results are published. A second respondent, from Fuller's group, points out that their delay-line memorandum of 1925 already stored orders beside numbers. The corrections column prints the report with the group's memorandum as prior work, and von Neumann signs the correction.
- **1928: the first stored-program machine.** It runs at Carquinez with about 3,000 valves, a store of 512 numbers in delay lines and a drum behind it (MODEL; *Hist.*: Manchester, 1948; EDSAC, 1949). Its first published result is an orbit of Levy's. A minor planet's orbit is the right problem, because the answer can be checked against the sky.
- **Comrie.** Leslie Comrie, born at Pukekohe in 1893, comes to Carquinez after the war instead of Cambridge and London (*Hist.*: he served in France with the New Zealand Expeditionary Force, then joined Britain's Nautical Almanac Office in 1925, ran it from 1930 and first used punched cards for scientific tables in 1928). From 1925 he runs the Office's table service. It sells tables and machine time to every navy and almanac in the world, Britain's included.

- **Production.** The machines of 1928–1930 are built by the Office's workshop at Carquinez. From 1930 Hollerith's company (Hollerith dies in 1929, *Hist.*) builds the Realm machine under licence at the published royalty (CR-057). By 1932 about fifteen stored-program machines run (MODEL):

| The Office of Public Measures (its city OPEN) | 1930 | the census of 1930, the county tables, the registers |
| The Reserve Bank and the clearing house, San Francisco | 1931 | clearing and the cover, daily |
| The General Staff at Thalassia; the fleet | 1930, 1931 | ciphers, gunnery, logistics |

| Tokyo: the Central Meteorological Observatory and the Imperial University | 1931 | typhoons; Part G10 |
| Others (3–4) | 1931–1932 | the Patrimony and the railways (MODEL) |

- **Programming.** By 1931 Levy's library holds several hundred tested routines (*Hist.*: Wheeler's subroutines, 1949). The orders are written in symbols and translated by the machine itself, the first symbolic order code. It descends from the notation of 1878–1882 (*Hist.*: assemblers 1949–1950; autocode 1952).
- **In the laboratory, not in service:**
  - Philo Farnsworth, born at Beaver in Deseret in 1906, makes his image dissector in San Francisco in 1927 (*Hist.*). The same electron optics give the Office a storage tube in trial in 1931 (*Hist.*: the Williams tube, 1947). His television belongs to the media CR.
  - Magnetic cores are tried from 1930 in two forms: metal tape (*Hist.*: Permalloy, 1914) at Carquinez and the new Tokyo ferrite (*Hist.*: Kato and Takei, Tokyo Institute of Technology, 1930). Neither stores reliably by 1932 (OPEN for later).
  - Crystal amplifiers are tried after Losev's and Lilienfeld's work (*Hist.*: 1922, 1925). Nothing amplifies usefully before 1932.

## G. What it does to society — PROPOSED

**G1. The computers become order-writers.** The Office's human computers, mostly examined women since the 1860s, become the first programmers, as ENIAC's computers did in 1946 (*Hist.*). The profession inherits the customs:
- The corrections column names the author of a faulty routine and the person who found the fault, as it named Kate Curran in 1872.
- Each tested routine carries its maker's name in the library.

Because Pacifica's honours go to achievement over rank (CR-049 I2), the order-writers rise by tested work, not seniority. Levy heads the Office's order library from 1929.

**G2. The measured realm.** The Office of Public Measures holds "the Realm's numbers" (CR-027), and the machines make measuring cheap, frequent and fast.
- The four county standards are published monthly from 1931 instead of yearly.
- The census of 1930 is counted in weeks.
- The tax rolls of the War Revenue Act (1915), the Previsión's accounts (1919) and the tongue votes all run on cards.
- The Party, which "runs on the Office's tables" (CR-072), gets them sooner.

The regency's case for its rule, measurably better lives, is now tested every month in print. The weakness canon names in 1932, the state's confidence in its own information, grows with it. In 1931 a valley county meets the four standards in every monthly table, while the guest workers it houses are counted in the county that hired them, and the infant deaths in their camps appear in neither (PROPOSED). Noe's registers are tabulated (canon), and nothing here touches the question of her death.

**G3. Weather.**
- Rossby comes in 1926 and builds his airway weather service between Oakland and Los Angeles in 1927–1928 (*Hist.*: for the Guggenheim Fund and Western Air Express).
- From 1929 the Weather Office tries Richardson's forecast by numbers on Carquinez's machine (*Hist.*: Richardson, *Weather Prediction by Numerical Process*, 1922). The forecasts fail as his did, with impossible pressure changes, and the time steps are too long for the grid (*Hist.*: Courant, Friedrichs and Lewy's stability condition, Göttingen, 1928). Levy answers the Weather Office's complaint with the Office's old rule, that a calculation that runs is not an account that is true.
- In 1931–1932 Rossby's simplified equations make the trial forecasts hold, run on the upper-air soundings his airway pilots and the navy's aircraft have flown daily since 1928 (*Hist.*: radiosondes from 1929–1930; the filtered equations 1939–1948). Daily machine forecasts are OPEN beyond 1932 (D-9).
- The weather network, the teleprinter and the typhoon warnings for Hawaii, the Philippines and Japan improve with or without the machines.

**G4. Money.**
- The Reserve Bank watches its cover, never a price index (CR-057). From 1931 it knows the cover daily.
- The clearing house settles overnight.
- The insurers price life and fire risk from the Office's tables.
- The Patrimony publishes its accounts quarterly.
- Hollerith's card company licenses abroad at one royalty. Every bank in the Union that wants clearing on cards pays San Francisco.

**G5. The wire.** Dial telephones, teleprinter networks and the cables' traffic all run through relay exchanges designed by Peirce's algebra; who owns them is the telecommunications CR's question (D-7). Japan's telephone works build theirs with Pacifican partners. *Hist.*: NEC was founded in 1899 as a Western Electric joint venture; in 1934–1936 Nakashima Akira of NEC worked out switching algebra independently of Shannon.

**G6. War and secrecy.**
- The cipher room, the Russian war's movement tables and the navy's range and gunnery tables (Part D).
- After 1927, radio location with machine plotting, the "early radio location" of 1932 (canon).
- The cipher room's machines are never patented or sold; they are listed by name, with their makers, on a sealed roll of the General Staff.
- The Works' machines (the relay machines from 1913, the stored-program machine of 1929) are described only in the sealed record's second part, to be opened in 1947. This follows the atomic rule: silence with a written end, never a false paper, and no lost credit.
- The export restriction on the halls' goods (canon) covers their high-power, high-vacuum and regulating valves and the condensation pumps, not receiving or computing valves.
- Everything else is licensed openly.

**G7. Science.**
- Ralston College and Carquinez compute orbits, crystal structures and atomic wave functions (*Hist.*: Hartree's self-consistent field, 1928, by hand).
- The Works computes neutron histories inside the wall (Part F).
- The atomic dates do not move (Canon tensions, T3).

**G8. The imagination.**
- Hearst's press makes "the Office's brains" a word. Romances of thinking machines are thirty years older than in Europe, as atomic romances are (canon).
- Peirce's essay of 1887 asks whether a machine reasons, and the challenge evening keeps asking.
- In Honolulu the question whether a machine's work has *mana*, or only its makers do, joins the *mana* debate (CR-049 E3).
- The Saints' genealogists put the dead on cards.

**G9. Work.** The dial exchanges put thousands of telephone operators, most of them women, out of work between 1893 and about 1910. In 1898 the trades congress (1895) carries their complaint to the Senate's committee. The congress never hires, trains or pays (CR-038), so the answer comes from the Office: its school examines former operators for the computing rooms, and the exchanges pay a year's wage to those who leave. In 1930 the census machine does the same to the hand tabulators, and the same school takes them. The eight-hour day (CR-071) holds, because the machines raise output per hour and the machine rooms work shifts.

**G10. Japan.** The Tokyo machine of 1931 is bought from Pacifica, as the Hayama annex expects (CR-054), and computes typhoon tracks for Japan's coast. It is a second emblem of the friendship, beside the atomic station that lights Tokyo in 1932 (canon). Nishina's colleagues and the Tokyo Institute of Technology's ferrite chemists work with the Office.

## H. The neighbours — PROPOSED

- **The Union** bets on analogue machines: Bush's differential analyser at MIT (*Hist.*: 1931). Its Census Bureau refuses to pay a Pacifican royalty and builds its own machines, and its card industry forms around them (*Hist.*: James Powers built the Bureau's machines for the 1910 census and founded the Powers Accounting Machine Company in 1911). Watson builds the champion of that card industry (*Hist.*: CTR 1911–1914, IBM 1924); its name is OPEN. The Union's banks, which care about cost, license from San Francisco, and the revanchists resent it.
- **Britain** loses Comrie. The Nautical Almanac buys Pacifican tables. Cambridge's counter (*Hist.*: 1931–1932) is thirty years behind the counting room.
- **Europe** has Torres Quevedo's essays on automatics (*Hist.*: 1914, with floating-point numbers) and Ludgate's design (*Hist.*: 1909). Neither has a factory behind it.
- **The lead after 1932** rests on the same footing as the atom's: people, the valve and delay-line works, and the habits of the trade. Secrecy plays no part outside the cipher room and the Works.
