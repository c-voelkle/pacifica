## A. Who owns the wire (E-1, E-2: APPROVED)

**The split.** The rule of CR-057 applied to the telephone: rents, not equity, and a monopoly only where the network is one network.
- **The telegraph estate** (the Polytechnic's) owns three things as monopolies it owns:
  - the long lines;
  - the cables;
  - the Realm's switching and signalling standard.
- **Local exchanges** are county concessions, auctioned for twenty years. Every concession carries three articles:
  - connection to every other exchange and to the long lines at a published rate;
  - service figures in the Office's tables (calls completed, faults, hours to repair, the waiting list);
  - the Realm's standard.
- **The homelands.** A homeland that wants a line lets the concession itself, under its compact.
- **The equipment makers compete:**
  - the relay works that grew from the Office's machines;
  - Hollerith's company;
  - after 1899, Japan's partner works.

**Bell's rights (E-2).**
- **1876:** Bell files in Pacifica as well as at home. *Hist.:* Bell's partners offered Western Union all the patents for $100,000 in the autumn of 1876 and were refused.
- **1877:** the telegraph estate buys the Realm rights outright, for about $25,000 (MODEL). Soberanes is thirty and has no part in it. The purchase is the estate's board's, on Elias's instruction that the Republic should own any wire that may become a network.
- The estate licenses the patents at the one royalty (CR-057), so the first county exchanges, from 1878, pay the same rate as the instrument makers.

## C. The cables (E-4, E-5, E-6, E-14: APPROVED)

**The southern wire** (approved in the planning note's §9):
- The trunk from Oʻahu by Midway to Guam (Apra) is laid in 1889–90.
- Guam to Manila follows in 1891 (E-14; this replaces CR-015's "by 1900").
- Guam to Tokyo Bay, by Chichijima in the Bonins, is laid in 1892 on Japanese landing grants under the 1887 compact with Tokyo. The landing place is to be checked; CR-070 G8 suggests Yokosuka.
- It carries traffic from the autumn of 1892.
- About 6,800 nautical miles; about $8m (MODEL).
- Victoria orders it in 1888 as the regency's first great work. Soberanes builds it.

**The Wake cable (E-5).** About 1910 a second Pacific trunk runs from Midway by Wake to Guam, so that Guam has two routes. The claim of 1880 pays. About 2,400 nautical miles; about $3m (MODEL).

**China (E-6, in principle).** Pacifica lays Manila to the treaty ports of the Customs Inspectorate in the 1890s, on Chinese landing grants. The routes, grants and dates belong to the partners' CR. *Hist.:* Hong Kong was joined to Manila by a British company's cable in May 1880, and the cable to Canton followed in March 1882.

**Insulation and the cable works (E-4).** *Hist.:* gutta-percha came from Malaya and Borneo, and before 1914 British cable makers dominated both its trade and cable making.
- From the 1880s the Pacifican Borneo company holds a gutta-percha concession in its interior. It holds it under compact with the interior's peoples (CR-074), who tap and sell the latex for a share.
- The Realm's cable works stands on the Carquinez Strait (PROPOSED), beside the Polytechnic. It makes the core, armours it and loads the cable ships.
- From the 1920s acetylene rubber (CR-055) is tried as insulation and fails at sea depth by 1932. Canon's "insulation industry" of 1932 is the Carquinez works and its Bornean supply.

**The cable ships.** The first Pacifican cable ship lays the Oʻahu cable of 1879. A second ship follows after 1888, and by 1932 there are four (MODEL). They are built in Pacifica and crewed by the navy's reserve.

## D. Teleprinter and fax (E-7: APPROVED; R1)

- **Pictures by wire from 1908–10.**
  - The press seat's newspapers send photographs by wire within the Realm.
  - The Concord's newspapers, orders and contracts in kanji, Chinese and Hawaiian travel as pictures, because telegraph codes cannot carry kanji.
  - *Hist.:* Korn's telephotography, 1907; NEC's NE-type phototelegraph, 1928.
- **Teleprinters from about 1910** for the press, the railways and the Office. A six-language news wire runs on them.
- **Who makes the fax machines** belongs to the partners' CR (the Hayama annex question).

- **The Wireless Act of 1911.** Wavelengths are concessions of the telegraph estate, auctioned. Each licence carries a public-service article: the Office's daily tables read unaltered, and air time for the examination lessons.
- **The Pacific wireless chain (E-9).** Long-wave stations at Oʻahu, Guam, Manila, Sitka and Tutuila, built in 1910–12 by the power estate under Fuller and run by the telegraph estate. The fleet and trade can talk across the ocean without a cable, and the cable is no longer the only route. About $2m (MODEL). The chain talks to every maker's sets. *Hist.:* Marconi's company refused to talk to stations of other makers until the Berlin convention of 1906.
- **The radio standard for Asia,** as with the electricity standard: Pacifican wavelengths, sets and valves are sold in the partners' markets. The partners' CR settles Japan's share of making them.
- **Broadcasting's content** (what is on the air, Hearst, the Party) belongs to CR-079.

## F. Navigation and radio location (E-10: APPROVED)

- **Direction finding.** From the 1910s the fleet and coastal stations take bearings on transmitters. This is canon's 1932 "direction finding".
- **Radio beacons and a radio range** for the all-metal air arm and the airway from Oakland to Los Angeles (CR-077's weather service), about 1920. *Hist.:* the radio range came in 1929.
- **Yagi's antenna** (*Hist.*: Tohoku, 1926) is adopted at once for the fleet's direction finding and for radio location. In history it was little used in Japan until the war.
- **Radio location:**
  - **1924–26:** pulse experiments on metre waves at the navy's radio laboratory at San Diego (PROPOSED). The counting room's pulse methods time the echo, and a cathode-ray tube shows it.
  - **By 1932:** a few sets at Oʻahu and on the fleet's largest ships, with machine plotting at Oʻahu (CR-077). This is canon's "early radio location".
  - **The magnetron:** Okabe's split-anode magnetron (*Hist.*: Tohoku, 1927) is in trial with the navy. The cavity magnetron comes after 1932.
  - *Hist.:* metre-wave radar arrived in 1934–39, the cavity magnetron in 1940.

## G. The ocean (E-11, E-12: APPROVED; R3, R8)

- **Echo sounding.**
  - From about 1915 the cable ships sound routes and the Pacific's depths by echo. By 1932 the Office publishes depth charts of the Realm's waters.
  - From about 1925 fishing boats use echo sounders to find fish (R8, option approved): tuna off San Diego and Hawaiʻi, and the salmon runs at sea.
  - *Hist.:* Fessenden's oscillator, 1914; the US Navy's sonic depth finder, 1922; fish-finding echo sounders, 1930s.
- **The warnings compact, 1897.**
  - *Hist.:* the Sanriku tsunami of 15 June 1896 killed about 22,000 people in Japan, and its waves reached Hawaii.
  - In 1897 the Realm and Japan agree to exchange warnings of earthquakes at sea and of typhoons over the southern wire, which has joined Tokyo to Honolulu since 1892.
  - Ōmori's seismographs in Japan and the Realm's at Berkeley and Honolulu report to both sides. From the 1910s the warnings go by radio as well.
  - The ledger records the compact. Japan's side is detailed in the partners' CR.
- **Jaggar at Kīlauea (set piece).**
  - Thomas Jaggar founds the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory on Kīlauea's rim in 1912 (*Hist.*). He puts seismographs at Hilo and Kona under the compact's network.
  - Late in 1922 he privately predicts the wave from a Chilean earthquake, and it arrives on time (*Hist.*).
  - On 3 February 1923 he reads the Kamchatka earthquake and warns Hilo that the wave will arrive about noon.
  - *Hist.:* the harbourmaster did not take the warning seriously, and one fisherman was killed. In Pacifica the harbourmaster clears the harbour, because the compact obliges him to act on the Observatory's warning, and no one dies.
  - After the next warning, in October 1927, the whole fishing fleet goes out to deep water (*Hist.*, as happened). In Pacifica this is the second proof of the warnings, not the first.

## H. Tesla's wireless power (E-13: APPROVED; R4)

- **About 1902** Tesla proposes to send power through the earth without wires, from a tower. *Hist.:* Wardenclyffe, 1901–1906, was abandoned unfinished.
- **The challenge evening.** Ryan, thirty-six and head of the Polytechnic's high-voltage laboratory, is the respondent. He asks what fraction of the power reaches a receiver a hundred miles away, and how it could be measured. Tesla answers with the resonance of the earth and offers no figure.
- **The outcome.**
  - The room names a test: a receiver at Sacramento, and the measured power on the Office's register.
  - The test receives less than a thousandth of what is sent.
  - The estate does not build the tower. The power seat goes on lending Tesla's name to the grid and his laboratories to the young.
  - Tesla loses prestige, not money or his seat.
  - The result is printed in the corrections column under his name and Ryan's. It is the reason Ryan, not Tesla's own students, is trusted with the halls' supply after 1917.

## I. What it does to society (E-15: APPROVED, all)

**I1. The telephone as a county good.**
- **Bids.** The county concessions are bid for every twenty years. A county's service figures (calls completed, hours to repair, the waiting list) appear in the Office's tables beside the four standards.
- **Party lines.** In the interior a party line shares one wire among a valley's farms, with a ring for each. Everyone hears everyone, and the valley's news travels faster than the newspaper.
- **The homelands.** A homeland's own concession hires its own operators. In Diné country the first switchboard is run in Navajo (PROPOSED).
- **The Party's caucuses** meet by telephone between elections, and the opposition clubs complain of it.

**I2. Operators, then computers** (CR-077 G9, already canon). The dial puts the operators out of work from 1893. The Office's school takes them, and some become the first order-writers' assistants after 1921.

**I3. The air school.**
- From 1915 the examination lessons are broadcast at fixed hours in English and Spanish, and from 1916 in Hawaiian and Japanese.
- A farm family in the interior studies for the Realm's examination by radio. The settler bargain of CR-071, that the examination is a floor and not a ladder, gains its most visible instrument.
- The county halls lend receivers to families who have none.

**I4. Islands joined.**
- Sitka, a Hawaiian county and Manila hear the same news on the same evening.
- From 1922 Honolulu telephones Thalassia's offices across the Koʻolau, and Thalassia telephones San Francisco.
- From 1928 Thalassia telephones Tokyo. Julian speaks to the Emperor's household by radiotelephone in the year of the Concord's first Council (PROPOSED).

**I5. The empire's nerves.**
- **The two-route rule** grows out of Port Hamilton's lesson (1885) and becomes doctrine after 1892:
  - every vital link has a second route on Pacifican or allied ground;
  - from 1912 radio is the third route.
- **1932:** the 1932 crisis depends on these links, and canon's last line holds. The state trusts its information, and the information now arrives faster than it can be checked.

**I6. Warnings.**
- The 1897 compact is the Concord's oldest working institution after the southern wire itself.
- Hilo in 1923 is the first time the Realm's people see a warning save lives on the day. Hearst's press prints the Observatory's timetable of the wave beside the harbourmaster's order.

**I7. The Union.**
- Bell's private monopoly holds the Union's telephone, and AT&T's long lines reach San Francisco's border only in 1915 (*Hist.*).
- The Union buys Pacifican sets and valves, starts its own broadcasting commercially in 1920 and has no public-service article.
- Its revanchist press calls the air school Thalassia's voice in every farmhouse.
