## A. Who owns the wire (E-1, E-2)

**1876–1878.**
- In 1876 Bell files in the Confederation as well as at home. *Hist.:* Bell's partners offered Western Union all the patents for $100,000 in the autumn of 1876 and were refused.
- In 1877 the Confederation's state telegraph buys the Confederation's rights outright for about $25,000 (MODEL), on Elias's instruction that the Republic should own any wire that may become a network. It licenses them at one published royalty.
- The transmitter is Hughes's unpatented microphone (*Hist.*: 1878), so no second patent holds the field.
- In February 1878 the city and county of San Francisco lets the first exchange concession under California's law, with connection to every other line at a published rate. *Hist.:* Western Union's San Francisco exchange, 17 February 1878.

**From 1887.** The Polytechnic's telegraph estate owns three things as monopolies the state owns (T6):
- the long lines;
- the cables;
- the Realm's switching and signalling standard.

Exchanges stay county concessions, auctioned for twenty years. When the concessions are relet after 1888, each carries:
- connection at the published rate;
- service figures in the tables of the Office of Public Measures (calls completed, faults, hours to repair, the waiting list);
- the Realm's standard;
- a transfer rule: a losing incumbent's plant passes to the winner at a published valuation, as Spring Valley's works passed to the Republic in 1875.

**The homelands.** A homeland that wants a line lets its own concession under its compact.

**The makers compete:** the relay works grown from the Calculation Office's machines, Hollerith's company and, after 1899, Japan's partner works.

## C. The cables (E-4, E-5, E-6, E-14; T6)

**The southern wire.**
- **1888:** after Port Hamilton, Victoria asks for a wire to Tokyo that touches no foreign soil. Port Hamilton showed that the alliance's telegrams crossed British wires, by the Hong Kong–Manila cable and by Europe.
  - The Senate fights the $8m vote (MODEL). The inland command's senators say a wire to Japan is a wire for the coast's merchants.
  - Soberanes answers with the crisis's delays, telegram by telegram, and the vote passes.
- **1889–90:** Oʻahu by Midway to Guam (Apra). Midway's cable station stands on a lease from the Hawaiian Crown, whose land Midway is.
- **1891:** Guam to Manila (E-14).
- **1892:** Guam by Chichijima to Tokyo Bay, on Japanese landing grants under the 1887 compact with Tokyo (the landing place is to be checked; CR-070 G8 suggests Yokosuka). The wire carries traffic from the autumn of 1892.
- About 6,800 nautical miles.

**Hong Kong–Manila.** *Hist.:* Hong Kong was joined to Manila (Bolinao) by the Eastern Extension company in May 1880. In this timeline the company lands at Bolinao on a Pacifican landing grant of 1880. The cable stays British and is the Philippines' first link to Asia until 1891. Manila's own wire to the treaty ports belongs to the partners' CR (E-6).

**Wake, about 1910 (E-5).** A second span from Midway by Wake to Guam doubles the trunk's longest stretch. The claim of 1880 is used at last. About 2,400 nautical miles; about $3m (MODEL).

**Insulation (E-4).** *Hist.:* gutta-percha came from Malaya and Borneo, and British cable makers dominated its trade before 1914.
- From the 1880s the Pacifican Borneo company buys the latex from the interior's peoples under its compacts and sells it to the Carquinez cable works under a supply contract.
- Tapping fells the trees. By the 1890s the tappers go deeper into the hills for them, into the country of Mat Salleh's people (CR-074, 1895–98), and the company's compacts have to hold against its own buyers.
- The Carquinez cable works has existed since the Oʻahu cable of 1879. It stands beside the Polytechnic, makes the core and armours the cable.
- Acetylene rubber (CR-055) is tried as insulation in the 1920s and fails at depth by 1932.

**The cable ships.** The first lays the Oʻahu cable of 1879, and a second follows after 1888. By 1932 there are four (MODEL), built in the Realm and crewed by the navy's reserve.

## D. Teleprinter and fax (E-7; R1)

- **Teleprinters, about 1910,** for the press, the railways and the Office of Public Measures. A six-language news wire runs on them.
- **Who makes the fax machines:** the partners' CR.

- **The Wireless Act of 1911.** Wavelengths are concessions of the telegraph estate, auctioned. Each licence carries a public-service article: the Office of Public Measures' daily tables are read unaltered, and air time goes to the examination lessons. San Jose's station, broadcasting since 1909, has to bid for its own wavelength, and keeps it.
- **The Pacific wireless chain (E-9).** The power estate builds long-wave arc stations in 1910–12 at Oʻahu, Guam, Manila, Sitka and Tutuila (about $2m, MODEL), and the telegraph estate runs them. They talk to every maker's sets. From 1913 Fuller builds the chain's high-power valve transmitters and later its shortwave stations. *Hist.:* Marconi's stations would not talk to other makers' until the Berlin convention of 1906 obliged coast stations to intercommunicate (in force 1908).
- **The radio standard for Asia,** like the electricity standard. Japan's share of the making belongs to the partners' CR.
- **Radiotelephone and secrecy.** State business goes by cable. From 1928 the cipher room scrambles the Tokyo voice link, and it is still assumed to be overheard.
- **Broadcasting's content** belongs to CR-079.

## F. Navigation and radio location (E-10; T7)

- **Direction finding** from the 1910s, on the fleet and at the coast stations.
- **Radio beacons** for the air arm and the Oakland–Los Angeles airway, about 1920; Rossby's airway weather service follows in 1927–28 (CR-077). *Hist.:* the radio range was demonstrated in 1928.
- **Yagi's antenna** (*Hist.*: Tohoku, 1926) is adopted at once for direction finding and radio location.
- **Radio location:**
  - **1924–26:** pulse experiments on metre waves at the navy's radio laboratory at San Diego (PROPOSED). The counting room's pulse methods time the echo, and a cathode-ray tube shows it. *Hist.:* Breit and Tuve's pulse-echo soundings of the ionosphere, 1925.
  - **By 1932:** a few sets at Oʻahu and on the fleet's largest ships, plotted by machine (CR-077).
  - **The magnetron:** Okabe's split-anode magnetron (*Hist.*: Tohoku, 1927) in trial; the cavity magnetron after 1932.
  - **Secrecy (T7, APPROVED).** Radio location is never patented or sold. It is listed on the General Staff's sealed roll with its makers, as the cipher room's machines are, for as long as the secret holds. Its makers keep their credit on the roll.

## G. The ocean (E-11, E-12)

- **Echo sounding.**
  - From about 1915 the cable ships and the navy sound routes and the Pacific's depths, and the coast survey publishes the charts.
  - From about 1925 the fishing fleets find sardine and salmon at sea by echo; tuna come later.
  - *Hist.:* Fessenden, 1914; the US Navy's sonic depth finder, 1922; fish-finding in the 1930s.
- **Sanriku, 15 June 1896.**
  - The earthquake's news reaches Honolulu over the southern wire before the waves do, and no one there knows what it means.
  - The waves strike Hawaii's coasts that night. *Hist.:* about 22,000 people died in Japan, and waves of several metres struck Hawaii.
- **The warnings compact of 1897.** In 1897 the Realm and Japan agree to exchange warnings: reports of earthquakes at sea and warnings of typhoons, by the southern wire and from the 1910s by radio. Ōmori's seismographs in Japan and the Realm's at Berkeley and Honolulu report to both sides. From 1897 typhoon warnings reach Guam, Manila and the Visayas a day ahead.
- **Jaggar, 1912–1923.**
  - Thomas Jaggar founds the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory on Kīlauea's rim in 1912 (*Hist.*) and puts seismographs at Hilo and Kona.
  - In 1913 the Hawaiian legislature's harbour act carries the compact into Hawaiian law: a harbourmaster must act on the Observatory's warning.
  - Late in 1922 Jaggar privately forecasts the arrival of a wave from a Chilean earthquake, and it arrives on time (*Hist.*).
- **Hilo, 3 February 1923.**
  - The Kamchatka earthquake is reported over the wire from Petropavlovsk, in Japan's protectorate, beside Jaggar's own record.
  - He forecasts the wave for about noon.
  - The harbour pilot who must take the boats out is Keawe's father. He does not believe a scientist's timetable, and he takes the boats out because the act obliges him to. The waterfront mocks him all morning. At noon the wave comes, and no one in Hilo dies.
  - *Hist.:* the warning was not taken seriously, and one fisherman was killed.
  - This is the first time a sea-wave forecast saves lives.

## H. Tesla's wireless power (E-13; R4)

- **About 1902** Tesla, head of the power estate, proposes to send power through the earth from a tower. *Hist.:* Wardenclyffe, 1901–1906, abandoned unfinished.
- **The challenge evening.** Ryan, thirty-six and head of the Polytechnic's high-voltage laboratory, is the respondent. He asks what fraction of the power reaches a receiver ninety miles away, and how it would be measured. Tesla offers the resonance of the earth and no figure.
- **The test.** Tesla accepts the room's test: a transmitter on the estate's ground near San Francisco (PROPOSED) and a receiver at Sacramento, read on the Office of Public Measures' register. The receiver takes in less than a thousandth of what is sent.
- **The outcome.** Tesla withdraws the tower himself. The result is printed in the corrections column under his name and Ryan's. He keeps the power seat until 1924. When the halls need their supply after 1917, Ryan is trusted with it.

## I. What it does to society (E-15)

**I1. The telephone as a county good.**
- Every twenty years the counties relet their concessions. The service figures appear in the Office of Public Measures' tables beside the four standards. A losing incumbent's men find themselves working for the winner at the published valuation, or out of work.
- In the interior a party line serves a valley's farms on one wire, a ring for each. The valley hears its news on the line before the newspaper comes.
- A homeland's concession hires its own operators; in Diné country the first switchboard is run in Navajo (PROPOSED).
- The Party's caucuses meet by telephone between elections, and the opposition clubs complain of it.

**I2. Who loses.**
- From the 1880s the telephone takes the town messages, and the Morse telegraphers and messenger boys lose them a decade before the dial puts the operators out of work (CR-077).
- The Office's school takes some of each.
- Hearst's editors find the press seat's own radio news read out before their papers are printed.

**I3. The air school.**
- From 1915 the examination lessons are broadcast at fixed hours:
  - in English and Spanish from 1915;
  - in Hawaiian and Japanese from 1916;
  - in Tagalog from Manila from 1917;
  - in Cantonese from San Francisco from 1918.
- A farm family in the interior studies for the Realm's examination by radio. It is the most visible instrument of CR-071's settler bargain, in which the examination is a floor for every citizen, not a ladder to power.
- The county halls lend receivers to families who have none.

**I4. Islands joined.**
- Sitka, a Hawaiian county and Manila hear the same news on the same evening.
- From 1922 Thalassia and Honolulu telephone San Francisco by radio.
- From 1928 Thalassia telephones Tokyo. In the year of the first Council of the Two Crowns, Julian speaks to the Emperor's household by radiotelephone, a call chosen for the newspapers rather than for business (PROPOSED).

**I5. The empire's nerves.**
- After Port Hamilton (1885) and the southern wire (1892) the two-route rule becomes doctrine: every vital link has a second route on Pacifican or allied ground. From 1912 radio is a third.
- The 1932 crisis runs on these links.

**I6. Warnings.**
- From 1897 the typhoon warnings save lives every season in Guam, Manila and the Visayas.
- Hilo in 1923 is the first time a forecast of a sea wave does. Hearst's press prints the Observatory's timetable of the wave beside the harbour act.

**I7. The Union.**
- Bell's private monopoly holds the Union's telephone.
- AT&T's long lines buy Pacifican repeater valves and reach Denver in 1911 (PROPOSED). The first New York–San Francisco call runs over the Realm's long lines from the Front Range. *Hist.:* 25 January 1915.
- The Union starts broadcasting commercially in 1920 with no public-service article. Its revanchist press calls the air school Thalassia's voice in every farmhouse.
