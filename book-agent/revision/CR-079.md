# CR-079 — Picture and sound: recording, film, television and the Realm's music (1887–1932)

## 0. What canon already fixes

- **1887:** Hearst, San Francisco-born, takes over the *Examiner* and makes the six-language weekly the Realm's press. His seat among the Five is PROPOSED (CR-027).
- **1887 onward:** "Hearst wants spectacle and she wants honest numbers with the weak spots shown; he may dramatize the Union's failures, but he prints the Office's tables unaltered" (APPROVED, CR-027). The bible adds: "Measurement belongs to the Office and Polytechnic; publication belongs to the press seat."
- **1888:** the league tables reach the public through "the six-language weekly, images, film and study tours" (§25). Tongue votes are uncapped.
- **1891:** Kalākaua, the Queen's minister of the image, dies in San Francisco, and the press seat inherits his image work: the hula revived, the chants printed, the legends retold.
- **1894:** the Bread March's armed men are tried "fairly, on film" (§25).
- **From 1895:** W. K. L. Dickson, recruited from Edison, gives the press seat moving pictures (PROPOSED, CR-027).
- **Late 1870s:** after the accident Victoria stops singing. Elias keeps a phonograph cylinder of her voice and asks for it on his deathbed in 1887 (PROPOSED, CR-027).
- **Julian** studies phonographic recordings of Elias and learns his cadence.
- **1910:** Los Angeles becomes a Crown city for oil and film. "Los Angeles film and sound" is among the Realm's products, and its film money goes to Guaymas to drink.
- **About 1919:** Hearst spikes his reporter's story on the halls' power, and it costs him with his own editors (CR-050).
- **Newsreels:** gifts of state (CR-050); the Office's newsreels in every town before the Tomsk vote of 1921 (CR-041); Kolyma; the Five's newsreel cameras at the demonstration of 1927 (CR-049); the Office films both banks (1927).
- **"Where the camera points is a choice"** (CR-048). Victoria's rule that seeing is believing works against Moscow (CR-040).
- **CR-078:** "Hearst's editors find the press seat's own radio news read out before their papers are printed." The Union's broadcasting starts commercially in 1920, and its revanchist press calls the air school "Thalassia's voice in every farmhouse".
- **1932:** Victoria is alive in Hawaii, out of power. "The successors' choice matters in 1932" (bible, the Five).

## A. The press seat (P-1 to P-3)

**P-1. Hearst holds the seat, 1887–1931.** *Recommendation:* approve the seat CR-027 proposed. Hearst is born in San Francisco on 29 April 1863 (*Hist.*) and is twenty-three when he takes the *Examiner*. This CR needs a holder, and every canon line about the press seat already assumes him.

**P-2. What the seat holds.** *Recommendation:* the press estate is chartered in 1887 like the others, and the state holds shares in it (CR-057). It owns:
- the *Examiner* and the six-language weekly;
- the picture wire, from 1908–10 (CR-078's fax);
- the film unit, from 1895 (Dickson);
- the San Francisco station, from 1912, with its radio news;
- the newsreel;
- from 1927, Farnsworth's television (Part D).

The estate is the largest publisher in the Realm, not the only one. Private papers, studios, labels and stations compete with it, and the estate's laboratories license everything they make at the one royalty. "Hearst's editors" (CR-050, CR-078) are the estate's newspaper editors. Their quarrel with the estate's own radio news is a quarrel inside one house, between print and air.

**P-3. The succession of 1931.** Hearst reaches sixty-eight on 29 April 1931 and leaves the Five under the retirement rule (CR-038). The press seat is the only one of the five to change hands in the last two years before 1932.
- *(a), recommended:* **Walter Lippmann** (*Hist.*: born New York, 23 September 1889). Hearst hires him from Harvard in 1913 to run the station's news room. He reports the war on the air from 1914 and writes *Public Opinion* at San Francisco (*Hist.*: 1922), on the pictures in people's heads and on the expert intelligence bureaus a modern public depends on, a book the Office of Public Measures could have commissioned. He takes the seat at forty-one. A holder who thinks the public knows the world only through the pictures the press makes of it sits among the Five in 1932, when Julian's information is canon's weakness (§36).
- *(b):* **Irving Thalberg** (*Hist.*: born Brooklyn, 1899), the Los Angeles producer: the seat becomes film's.
- *(c):* the successor stays OPEN.

## B. Sound: loudspeaker, record and wire (P-4 to P-6)

**P-4. The loudspeaker and public address, 1911–1914.**
- Peter L. Jensen, born on Falster in 1886, comes to California in 1909 to set up Poulsen's arc transmitters (*Hist.*: for the Federal Telegraph Company at Palo Alto). Here he comes for the power estate's arc stations (CR-078).
- With Edwin Pridham at Napa he builds a moving-coil loudspeaker. *Hist.:* their patent was filed in 1911 and the working speaker came in 1915. Here regular broadcasting from 1912 gives it a market at once: a working speaker in 1912 and a public-address set in 1913.
- **August 1914:** Julian's broadcast at the outbreak of war is heard by the crowd in Union Square and at Honolulu through Jensen's horns. *Hist.:* the first public address in the world amplified a Christmas Eve concert and the mayor at San Francisco City Hall on 24 December 1915, for a crowd of about 100,000.
- From then on every rally, ceremony and stadium has amplified sound, the Party's rallies first (C6). The planning note's *Hist.* date for C6 is corrected (T4).
- Jensen's works at Napa stay private and license at the royalty.

**P-5. Electrical recording, about 1912.**
- The telegraph estate's telephone microphones, a valve amplifier and a moving-coil cutter make records in the press estate's San Francisco studio from about 1912. The condenser microphone follows about 1916 (*Hist.*: Wente, 1916). *Hist.:* Western Electric's electrical recording was licensed to Victor and Columbia in 1925.
- Records are pressed in phenolic resin under Baekeland's licence. *Hist.:* Edison's Diamond Disc of 1912 used a phenolic resin like Bakelite.
- From about 1915 the valve phonograph shares the radio set's amplifier and loudspeaker, and the radio-phonograph is a household good a decade early (*Hist.*: late 1920s).

**P-6. Magnetic recording: wire about 1910, tape about 1928.**
- Jensen knows Poulsen's magnetic wire from Copenhagen (*Hist.*: Poulsen's telegraphone, 1898). Valve amplifiers make it usable about 1910. It records dictation, court and Senate proceedings, the Office's hearings and the cipher room's intercepts, and the stations record evening programmes for Honolulu's later hour.
- **About 1921** the navy's radio laboratory at San Diego finds that a high-frequency bias clears the wire's noise (*Hist.*: Carlson and Carpenter at the US Naval Research Laboratory, 1921, then ignored).
- **About 1928** Baekeland's works coat a plastic film with iron oxide for the press estate (*Hist.*: Pfleumer's coated paper, 1928; AEG's Magnetophon, 1935; the bias added, 1941). By 1932 the Realm's stations record and edit programmes on biased tape. This is the boldest date in the CR; the parts all exist by 1921–28, and Pacifica's stations, spread over five time zones, need it more than anyone.

## C. Moving pictures and sound film (P-7 to P-9)

**Dickson (canon, 1895).** *Hist.:* Dickson built Edison's Kinetograph and in 1894–95 made the first film with recorded sound for Edison's Kinetophone. He left Edison in 1895 and co-founded the American Mutoscope Company. Here he comes to San Francisco in 1895 with a portable camera and his interest in sound.

**P-7. Lauste and sound on film, 1906–1920.**
- Eugène Lauste, born in Montmartre in 1857, worked beside Dickson at Edison's laboratory until 1892 and at Biograph in 1896–1900 (*Hist.*). Here he follows Dickson to San Francisco in 1896.
- In 1906 he records sound photographically beside the picture on one film (*Hist.*: British patent with Haines and Pletts, 1906, working by about 1911). *Hist.:* he never found the money for amplification. Here the press estate has valve amplifiers, and photocells come about 1910 (D3).
- **1911:** a demonstration film at San Francisco with sound read by photocell and played through a valve amplifier.
- **1913:** Jensen's speaker in the theatre.
- **1916–18:** sound newsreels of the war.
- **1919–20:** the first talking features, from Los Angeles. *Hist.:* *The Jazz Singer*, 1927.

**P-8. No patents trust.** *Hist.:* Edison's Motion Picture Patents Company of 1908 drove independent producers toward Los Angeles. Here Dickson's camera, the Latham loop (which Dickson credited to Lauste) and Lauste's track are licensed at one royalty from the start, so no trust forms. Studios move from San Francisco to Los Angeles from about 1908 for light and land, and the Crown city of 1910 follows them. Sound film is licensed abroad at the same royalty, like Hollerith's cards.

**P-9. Talking pictures in six tongues.**
- Sound comes to the Realm's cinemas a decade before the world's, in a country of six languages.
- Los Angeles shoots features in English and Spanish versions. Honolulu's studios make films in Japanese and Hawaiian, and Manila's in Tagalog from the 1920s. Exports to Japan and China carry titles in kanji and Chinese.
- The Office makes its own films; the press estate distributes them, in keeping with "publication belongs to the press seat" (T7).
- The sound newsreel at the 1927 demonstration records the explosion's report reaching the ships twenty miles off a minute and a half after the flash.

## D. Television (P-10, P-11)

**Parts.** The cathode-ray tube (*Hist.*: Braun, 1897), valves, photocells and the counting room's pulse timing, which gives television its synchronizing pulses (bias: pulse first). Television is electronic from the start, with no mechanical era (planning note §3).

**P-10. The laboratory of 1922–25.**
- *(a), recommended:* **Vladimir Zworykin** (*Hist.*: born at Murom in 1888; Rosing's assistant at St Petersburg in 1910–12, when Rosing showed a picture on a cathode-ray tube in 1911). He is at Omsk in 1918–19 buying radio for Kolchak's government. *Hist.:* he went to Westinghouse in 1920. Here the Realm's people at Omsk recruit him in 1919 for the power estate's laboratory. In 1922–25 his tube shows moving pictures scanned from film, and in 1923 he describes a storage camera. *Hist.:* Takayanagi showed the katakana イ on a cathode-ray tube at Hamamatsu on 25 December 1926; Zworykin's storage camera worked in 1931–33. The partners' CR inherits him (T6).
- *(b):* the power estate's laboratory, unnamed.

**P-11. Farnsworth and public television, 1927–1932.**
- **1927:** Farnsworth makes his image dissector at 202 Green Street, San Francisco (canon, CR-077). Here the press estate backs him from that year, because Hearst wants the spectacle. The dissector and Zworykin's camera are licensed side by side at the one royalty, so there is no patent war. *Hist.:* RCA fought Farnsworth over the patents through the 1930s.
- **1930:** the press estate's San Francisco transmitter starts a public service on metre waves, an hour or two each evening.
- **1931:** Honolulu, for Thalassia and the city.
- **1932:** Los Angeles. *Hist.:* Don Lee's W6XAO at Los Angeles went on the air on a daily schedule in December 1931; the BBC's service began in 1936.
- **Cameras:** Farnsworth's dissector scans film; Zworykin's storage camera works in the studio from 1931–32. Pictures of about 240 lines by 1932 (MODEL).
- **Sets:** a few thousand homes in San Francisco and Honolulu by 1932, and viewing rooms in county halls and cinemas (MODEL).
- **Programmes:** newsreels, the Office's tables drawn as charts (picture over text), the air school's science demonstrations and Hawaiian music.
- The Office's storage tube of 1931 (CR-077) comes from the same electron optics.

## E. The Realm's music (P-12 to P-14)

**P-12. The steel guitar and Hawaiian music.**
- Joseph Kekuku, born at Lāʻie about 1874, finds the steel-guitar sound as a schoolboy (*Hist.*: about 1885–89, at the Kamehameha School). *Hist.:* he left for the mainland in 1904 and toured the United States and Europe.
- Here the mainland is the Realm. The press estate, which inherits Kalākaua's revival in 1891, records chant and steel guitar from the 1890s, and Kekuku plays and teaches at San Francisco from 1904 and at Los Angeles in the 1920s.
- From 1913 the Honolulu station broadcasts chant, hula songs and steel guitar, and the air school's Hawaiian lessons follow in 1916 (CR-078). *Hist.:* the Hawaiian craze began at San Francisco's exposition of 1915, and Victor then sold more Hawaiian records than any other kind. Here radio carries the craze from 1913, and Hawaiian music becomes the Realm's popular music, with Spanish and Japanese music beside it on the air.
- Kekuku dies in January 1932 (*Hist.*: 16 January 1932), here at Los Angeles.

**P-13. The electric steel guitar, about 1925.**
- *(a), recommended:* **George Beauchamp** (*Hist.*: born in Coleman County, Texas, 1899), a vaudeville steel guitarist in Los Angeles, makes a magnetic pickup about 1925 with Honda's KS magnet steel (*Hist.*: Tohoku, 1917) and a Jensen amplifier. *Hist.:* Beauchamp's "Frying Pan" of 1931 was the first electric lap steel, made at Los Angeles. The amplified Hawaiian guitar, then an electric "Spanish" guitar about 1928, gives the Realm an electric popular music by 1930. Jazz comes from the Union by record and over the air and meets it in Los Angeles.
- *(b):* the pickup is made in the press estate's sound-film shops, unnamed.

**P-14. Music on the wire (R7).** *Recommendation:* background only. From about 1912 the county exchanges sell music lines to hotels, restaurants and factories, made workable by valve amplifiers and loudspeakers. *Hist.:* Cahill's Telharmonium, New York, 1906–08; Muzak, 1934. Valve instruments appear in the 1910s and stay curiosities. Termen (*Hist.*: the theremin, 1920) belongs to the partners' CR.

## F. What is on the air (P-15 to P-17)

CR-078 left broadcasting's content to this CR.

**The day.** The public-service article fixes the Office's daily tables, read unaltered, and the air school's hours. The rest of the day belongs to the concession holder: news, music, drama, sport, church services and the sovereign's broadcasts. Hearst's papers answer the radio news with pictures. From the picture wire of 1908–10 the *Examiner* becomes a picture paper, and the radio is read about in it the next morning.

**P-15. Sponsors.** *Recommendation:* sponsored programmes are allowed outside the public-service hours. The tables and the lessons are never sponsored, and each sponsor is named on the air. *Hist.:* the Union's commercial model; the BBC's licence fee.

**P-16. Parties and the air.**
- *Recommendation:* no party may hold a wavelength, as no faction may capture an estate. In election months every ticket buys air time at one published rate, and the Office prints each ticket's minutes beside its votes. The Prosperity Party buys the most, and the table says so.
- Whether the press seat inherits the Party's wire stays as CR-072 left it (PROPOSED).

**P-17. The air school and the tongue vote.**
- Each certified language beyond English adds a tongue vote, without a cap (1888). The air school's language lessons, broadcast from 1915–18 (CR-078), prepare listeners for the language examinations.
- *Recommendation:* the Office prints the new certifications each year by language and county. Hawaiian certifications among haole and Japanese settlers on the islands rise fastest (MODEL: several times the 1915 number by 1930). The language Kalākaua revived becomes a second tongue for thousands who were never taught it as children, and a vote for each of them.
- County boards and the Party argue over whether the air school is teaching or campaigning. The Union's press says Thalassia sells votes by radio.

## G. Victoria, Julian and the image (P-18, P-19)

**Victoria's medium.** She governs by tables and pictures. She lets the cameras into the Bread March trial and to the grave on the Koptyaki road, and the weak spots go into the newsreels beside the gains. She is Regent until 1907, before regular broadcasting, and renounces her last executive role in 1921.

**P-18. Victoria's voice.**
- *(a), recommended:* she never sings again and never speaks into a microphone. The newsreels show her face, and the Realm never hears her. Elias's cylinder is the only recording of her voice. It stays with her and is never copied, though the press estate asks.
- *(b):* she speaks once on the air, in 1928, in Hawaiian and English, for the Concord's lawful consent.

**P-19. Elias's voice and Julian's cadence.**
- *Recommendation:* in 1913 the press estate copies Elias's cylinders electrically, and from 31 July 1913 the founder's recorded voice is broadcast each Confederation Day.
- In August 1914 the Realm hears Julian for the first time, and older listeners hear Elias's cadence in him. From then on his broadcasts are part of his command.

## H. The Union and abroad (P-20)

*Recommendation:*
- **Film.** Los Angeles is Pacifica's, so the Union's studios stay in New York and Chicago. *Hist.:* Selig and Essanay began in Chicago. The Union's studios license Lauste's track and make talkies from about 1922. In 1923 the Union sets a quota on Pacifican films in its cinemas. *Hist.:* Britain's Cinematograph Films Act, 1927.
- **Radio and television.** The Union's radio is commercial from 1920 (canon). Its television waits for Pacifican licences, since Zworykin and Farnsworth are both in the Realm (P-10 (a)).
- **The partners' CR:** Takayanagi and Japan's television; the benshi and the coming of sound to Japan's cinemas; Japan's records and radio; Shanghai's films; Manila's own studios; Termen.

## I. Society by 1932 (P-21)

- **Radio:** about one home in five by 1920 and most by 1928 (planning note §9, MODEL). Families listen together through the loudspeaker from 1913.
- **Cinema:** most cinemas are wired for sound by about 1922, and the Realm's talking pictures fill the screens of the Pacific and Latin America.
- **Records:** electrical, pressed in resin and sold everywhere the radio is.
- **Television:** a few thousand sets and the viewing rooms.
- **The press:** the picture paper, the newsreel, the radio news and Lippmann's seat (P-3 (a)).
- **Language:** six tongues on the air and on the screen, and the tongue vote growing with the air school.
- **Music:** the electric Hawaiian sound, with Spanish and Japanese music beside it.
- **Politics:** amplified rallies, the Party's purchased minutes in the Office's tables, and Julian's voice in Elias's cadence.
- **Abroad:** the Union's quota and its revanchist air; the Realm's films as the Pacific's.
