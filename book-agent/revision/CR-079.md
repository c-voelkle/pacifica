## A. The press seat (P-1 to P-3)

**P-1. Hearst holds the seat, 1887–1931.** The seat CR-027 proposed is approved. Hearst is born in San Francisco on 29 April 1863 (*Hist.*) and is twenty-three when he takes the *Examiner*. This CR needs a holder, and every canon line about the press seat already assumes him.

**P-2. What the seat holds.** The press estate is chartered on 1 August 1887 like the others, and the state holds shares in it (CR-057; T9). It owns:
- the *Examiner* and the six-language weekly;
- the picture wire, from 1908–10 (CR-078's fax);
- the film unit, from 1887–89 (Le Prince's camera and projector works; Dickson's production, newsreel and sound from 1895);
- the San Francisco station, from 1912, with its radio news;
- the newsreel;
- from 1927, Farnsworth's television (Part D).

The estate is the largest publisher in the Realm, not the only one. Private papers, studios, labels and stations compete with it, and the estate's laboratories license everything they make at the one royalty. "Hearst's editors" (CR-050, CR-078) are the estate's newspaper editors. They quarrel with the estate's own radio news, which beats their papers to the story.

**P-3. The succession of 1931: Lippmann.** Hearst reaches sixty-eight on 29 April 1931 and leaves the Five under the retirement rule (CR-038). The sovereign appoints his successor with the Senate's consent (§22: "the Regent appoints with Senate consent"), and in 1931 that is Julian.
- **Walter Lippmann** (*Hist.*: born New York, 23 September 1889; Harvard, 1909; *A Preface to Politics*, 1913). Hearst hires him from New York in 1913, at twenty-four, to run the station's news room. He reports the war on the air from 1914 and writes *Public Opinion* at San Francisco (*Hist.*: 1922), on the pictures of the world that people carry in their heads and on the expert intelligence bureaus a modern public depends on.
- **Julian's reason.** He appoints Lippmann in May 1931, at forty-one, because *Public Opinion* says that experts must give the public its picture of the world. That suits Julian's government by command. In 1932 Lippmann is the critic of the same confidence: canon's 1932 weakness is Pacifica's "confidence in its own information" (§36), and the new holder of the press seat is the man who has written about it.

## B. Sound: loudspeaker, record and wire (P-4 to P-6)

**P-4. The loudspeaker and public address, 1912–1914.**
- Peter L. Jensen, born on Falster in 1886, comes to California in 1909 to set up Poulsen's arc transmitters (*Hist.*: for the Poulsen Wireless company, from 1911 the Federal Telegraph Company, at Palo Alto). Here he comes for the power estate's arc stations (CR-078).
- With Edwin Pridham at Napa he builds a moving-coil loudspeaker. *Hist.:* their company was founded at Napa in 1911, the moving-coil patent was filed on 20 August 1913, and the working speaker came in 1915. Here regular broadcasting from 1912 gives it a market at once: a working speaker in 1912 and a public-address set in 1913.
- **August 1914:** Julian's broadcast at the outbreak of war is heard by the crowd in Union Square and at Honolulu through Jensen's horns. *Hist.:* the first public address in the world amplified a Christmas Eve concert and the mayor at San Francisco City Hall on 24 December 1915, for a crowd of 75,000–100,000.
- From then on every rally, ceremony and stadium has amplified sound, the Party's rallies first (C6). The planning note's C6 is corrected (T4).
- Jensen's works at Napa stay private and license at the royalty.

**P-5. Electrical recording, about 1912.**
- The telegraph estate's telephone microphones, a valve amplifier and a moving-coil cutter make records in the press estate's San Francisco studio from about 1912. The condenser microphone follows about 1916 (*Hist.*: Wente, 1916). *Hist.:* Western Electric's electrical recording was licensed to Victor and Columbia in 1925.
- Records are pressed in phenolic resin under Baekeland's licence. *Hist.:* Edison's Diamond Disc of 1912 used a phenolic resin like Bakelite.
- From about 1915 the valve phonograph shares the radio set's amplifier and loudspeaker, and the radio-phonograph is a household good a decade early (*Hist.*: late 1920s).

**P-6. Magnetic recording: wire about 1910, tape about 1928.**
- Jensen knows Poulsen's magnetic wire from Copenhagen (*Hist.*: Poulsen's telegraphone, 1898). Valve amplifiers make it usable about 1910. It records dictation, court and Senate proceedings, the Office's hearings and the cipher room's intercepts, and the stations record evening programmes for Honolulu's later hour.
- **About 1921** the navy's radio laboratory at San Diego, which exists by then, finds that a high-frequency bias clears the wire's noise while recording radio telegraph for the cipher room. *Hist.:* Carlson and Carpenter's patent, filed at Washington on 26 March 1921, used high-frequency bias to record radio telegraph on a telegraphone, and was then ignored.
- **About 1928** the press estate coats cellulose acetate film from its own film-stock works with iron oxide (*Hist.*: Pfleumer's coated paper, 1928; BASF's acetate tape and AEG's Magnetophon, 1935; the bias rediscovered by Weber in Germany in 1940). By 1932 the Realm's stations record and edit programmes on biased tape. This is the boldest date in the CR. The parts all exist by 1921–28, and Pacifica's stations, spread over five time zones, need recorded programmes.

## C. Moving pictures and sound film (P-7 to P-9)

**Projected film, 1878–1895 (T1 case C).**
- **1878:** Muybridge (*Hist.*: born at Kingston upon Thames, 9 April 1830) photographs a horse in motion with a battery of cameras on a Palo Alto stud farm. *Hist.:* he did it for Leland Stanford, who is not in canon; here the owner of the farm is left unnamed. Kalākaua asks for it and the Queen's government pays. From 1879 Muybridge projects his sequences with the zoopraxiscope (*Hist.*), and the payments continue through the 1880s. *Hist.:* in 1874 he shot his wife's lover, Harry Larkyns, and was acquitted in 1875.
- **1887:** Louis Le Prince (*Hist.*: born at Metz, 28 August 1841) has spent 1881–87 in New York building a multi-lens camera; in May 1887 he went back to Leeds. Here Kalākaua's office brings him to San Francisco instead, and the press estate takes over his contract on 1 August 1887.
- **1888:** his single-lens camera films on a paper roll (*Hist.*: Roundhay, Leeds, October 1888).
- **1889:** celluloid roll film (*Hist.*: Eastman, 1889; Goodwin's patent application, 1887), perforated strips (*Hist.*: Reynaud's patent, December 1888) and an arc lamp on the Realm's cheap current.
- **1889–90:** the first projected film at San Francisco, short open-air subjects. *Hist.:* Le Prince disappeared from the Dijon–Paris train in September 1890 and was never found. Here he does not take that train.
- **1890s:** the press estate's newsreels in music halls abroad, from Chicago to Berlin (power timeline); the Bread March trial of 1894 is filmed. In the Union, Edison's peephole Kinetoscope of 1894 is a parlour novelty beside Pacifican projectors.
- **1895:** Dickson leaves Edison (*Hist.*) for the press estate. *Hist.:* he built Edison's Kinetograph and in 1894–95 made the first film with recorded sound for Edison's Kinetophone. Here he runs production, the newsreel and sound, and Le Prince keeps the camera and projector works until he retires in 1909, at sixty-eight. Muybridge dies in 1904.

**P-7. Lauste and sound on film, 1906–1920.**
- Eugène Lauste, born in Montmartre in 1857, worked beside Dickson at Edison's laboratory until 1892 and at Biograph in 1896–1900 (*Hist.*). Here he follows Dickson to San Francisco in 1896.
- In 1906 he records sound photographically beside the picture on one film (*Hist.*: British patent with Haines and Pletts, 1906, working by about 1911). *Hist.:* he never found the money for amplification. Here the press estate has valve amplifiers, and photocells come about 1910 (D3).
- **1911:** a demonstration film at San Francisco with sound read by photocell and played through a valve amplifier.
- **1913:** Jensen's speaker in the theatre.
- **1916–18:** sound newsreels of the war.
- **1919–20:** the first talking features, from Los Angeles. *Hist.:* *The Jazz Singer*, 1927.

**P-8. No patents trust.** *Hist.:* Edison's Motion Picture Patents Company of 1908 drove independent producers toward Los Angeles. Here Le Prince's camera and projector, Dickson's camera, the Latham loop (which Dickson credited to Lauste) and Lauste's track are licensed at one royalty from the start, so no trust forms. Studios move from San Francisco to Los Angeles from about 1908 for light and land, and the Crown city of 1910 follows them. Sound film is licensed abroad at the same royalty, like Hollerith's cards.

**P-9. Talking pictures in six tongues.**
- Sound comes to the Realm's cinemas about a decade before it did in history, in a country whose air carries six languages (English, Spanish, Hawaiian, Japanese, Tagalog and Cantonese; CR-078).
- Los Angeles shoots features in English and Spanish versions. Honolulu's studios make films in Japanese and Hawaiian, San Francisco's in Cantonese, and Manila's in Tagalog from the 1920s. Exports to Japan and China carry Japanese and Chinese titles.
- The Office makes its own films; the press estate distributes them unaltered, in keeping with "publication belongs to the press seat" (T7). The rule is Victoria's, made in 1913 (P-18).
- The sound newsreel at the 1927 demonstration records the explosion's report reaching the ships twenty miles off a minute and a half after the flash.

## D. Television (P-10, P-11)

**Parts.** The cathode-ray tube (*Hist.*: Braun, 1897), valves, photocells and the counting room's pulse timing, which gives television its synchronizing pulses (bias: pulse first). Television is electronic from the start, with no mechanical era (planning note §3).

**P-10. The laboratory of 1922–25.**
- **Vladimir Zworykin** (*Hist.*: born at Murom in 1888; Rosing's assistant at St Petersburg in 1910–12, when Rosing showed a picture on a cathode-ray tube in 1911). *Hist.:* in 1918–19 he bought radio at Omsk for Kolchak's government, and he went to Westinghouse in 1920. Here he buys radio for the Directory's war ministry at Omsk, where Kolchak is minister (CR-041), and Kashevarov's Reserve Bank office at Omsk recruits him in 1919 for the power estate's laboratory. In 1922–25 his tube shows moving pictures scanned from film, and in 1923 he describes a storage camera. *Hist.:* Takayanagi showed the katakana イ on a cathode-ray tube at Hamamatsu on 25 December 1926; Zworykin's storage camera worked in 1931–33. Takayanagi belongs to the partners' CR; Zworykin's move is T6.

**P-11. Farnsworth and public television, 1927–1932.**
- **1927:** Farnsworth makes his image dissector at 202 Green Street, San Francisco (canon, CR-077). Here the press estate backs him from that year, because Hearst wants the spectacle. The dissector and Zworykin's camera are licensed side by side at the one royalty, so there is no patent war. *Hist.:* RCA fought Farnsworth over the patents through the 1930s.
- **1930:** the press estate's San Francisco transmitter starts a public service on metre waves, an hour or two each evening.
- **November 1931:** Honolulu, for Thalassia and the city.
- **1932:** Los Angeles. *Hist.:* Don Lee's W6XAO at Los Angeles went on the air on a daily schedule in December 1931; the BBC's service began in 1936.
- **Cameras:** Farnsworth's dissector scans film; Zworykin's storage camera works in the studio from 1931–32. Pictures of about 240 lines by 1932 (MODEL).
- **Sets:** a few thousand homes in San Francisco and Honolulu by 1932, and viewing rooms in county halls and cinemas (MODEL).
- **Programmes:** newsreels, the Office's tables drawn as charts (picture over text), the air school's science demonstrations and Hawaiian music.
- The Office's storage tube of 1931 (CR-077) comes from the same electron optics.

## E. The Realm's music (P-12 to P-14)

**P-12. The steel guitar and Hawaiian music.**
- Joseph Kekuku, born at Lāʻie about 1874, finds the steel-guitar sound as a schoolboy (*Hist.*: about 1889, at the Kamehameha School for Boys, opened in 1887). *Hist.:* he left for the mainland in 1904 and toured the United States and Europe.
- Here he goes to the Realm's mainland. The press estate, which inherits Kalākaua's revival in 1891, records chant and steel guitar from the 1890s, and Kekuku plays and teaches at San Francisco from 1904 and at Los Angeles in the 1920s.
- From 1913 the Honolulu station broadcasts chant, hula songs and steel guitar, and from 1916 the air school teaches Hawaiian at fixed hours (P-17). *Hist.:* the Hawaiian craze began at San Francisco's exposition of 1915, and Victor then sold more Hawaiian records than any other kind. Here radio carries the craze from 1913, and Hawaiian music becomes the Realm's popular music, with Spanish and Japanese music beside it on the air.
- Kekuku dies in January 1932 (*Hist.*: 16 January 1932), here at Los Angeles.

**P-13. The electric steel guitar, about 1925.**
- **George Beauchamp** (*Hist.*: born in Coleman County, Texas, 1899; here a Gulf Republic citizen by birth), a vaudeville steel guitarist in Los Angeles, makes a magnetic pickup about 1925 with Honda's KS magnet steel (*Hist.*: Tohoku, 1917) and a Jensen amplifier. *Hist.:* Beauchamp's "Frying Pan" of 1931 was the first electric lap steel, made at Los Angeles. The amplified Hawaiian guitar, then an electric "Spanish" guitar about 1928, gives the Realm an electric popular music by 1930. Jazz comes from New Orleans in the Gulf Republic and from the Union's cities, by record and over the air, and meets it in Los Angeles.

**The new music (author, P-21).** What the Realm's musicians make in the 1920s draws on Hawaiian chant and song, the wider Polynesian music of the islands' visitors and settlers, Japanese music, and Western music, the concert hall's as well as the dance hall's. It is not a variety of any of them. It stands to its sources as jazz stood to its own: nobody predicted it, and to listeners raised on any one of the traditions it sounds foreign. The electric steel guitar, the loudspeaker and the record carry it; the air school's and the stations' six languages give it its audiences.

**P-14. Music on the wire (R7).** APPROVED as background, which a scene may mention in passing for texture. From about 1912 the county exchanges sell music lines to hotels, restaurants and factories, made workable by valve amplifiers and loudspeakers. *Hist.:* Cahill's Telharmonium, New York, 1906–08; Muzak, 1934. Valve instruments appear in the 1910s and stay curiosities. Termen (*Hist.*: the theremin, 1920) belongs to the partners' CR.

## F. What is on the air (P-15 to P-17)

CR-078 left broadcasting's content to this CR.

**The day.** The public-service article fixes the Office's daily tables, read unaltered, and the air school's hours. The rest of the day belongs to the concession holder: news, music, drama, sport, church services and the sovereign's broadcasts. Hearst's papers answer the radio news with pictures. From the picture wire of 1908–10 the *Examiner* becomes a picture paper, and the radio is read about in it the next morning.

**P-15. Sponsors.** Sponsored programmes are allowed outside the public-service hours. The tables and the lessons are never sponsored, and each sponsor is named on the air. *Hist.:* the Union's commercial model; the BBC's licence fee.

**P-16. Parties and the air.**
- No party may hold a wavelength, as no faction may capture an estate. An amending act of 1913 to the Wireless Act sets the rule: in election months every ticket buys air time at one published rate, and the Office prints each ticket's minutes beside its votes. The Prosperity Party buys the most air time, and the Office's table shows it.
- Whether the press seat inherits the Party's wire stays as CR-072 left it (PROPOSED).

**P-17. The air school and the tongue vote.**
- Each certified language beyond English adds a tongue vote, without a cap (1888). CR-078's air school gives the examination lessons in each of its languages. From 1916 it also teaches the listed languages themselves at fixed hours, for the language examinations (new canon).
- The Office prints the new certifications each year by language and county. Hawaiian certifications among haole and Japanese settlers on the islands rise fastest (MODEL: several times the 1915 number by 1930). The language of the Kaʻahumanu Society's schools becomes a second tongue for thousands who were never taught it as children, and a vote for each of them.
- County boards and the Party argue over whether the air school is teaching or campaigning. The Union's press says Thalassia sells votes by radio.

## G. Victoria, Julian and the image (P-18, P-19)

**Victoria's medium.** She governs by tables and pictures. She lets the cameras into the Bread March trial and to the grave on the Koptyaki road, and the weak spots go into the newsreels beside the gains. She is Regent until 1907, before regular broadcasting, and renounces her last executive role in 1921.

**P-18. Victoria's voice: never recorded again.**
- She never sings again and never speaks into a microphone. Her reason: her voice belonged to Elias and to the chant, and she will not let the press estate cut and arrange it. The newsreels show her face; she is never heard on the air.
- Elias's cylinder is the only recording of her voice. It stays with her and is never copied, though the press estate asks. *Hist.:* tinfoil records of 1877–78 could not be kept or replayed reliably; here the Laboratory of Light records on wax by the late 1870s (*Hist.*: Bell's Volta Laboratory, wax, 1881–86).

**P-19. Elias's voice and Julian's cadence.**
- Victoria holds Elias's cylinders. In 1913 Julian asks for electrical copies, and she agrees. From 1 August 1913, the anniversary of the Constitution, the press estate broadcasts the founder's recorded voice each year, and the first time she hears it on the air she switches the set off.
- **Her decision (author, 12:28).** In 1913, while she still holds her last executive role, Victoria settles the rule for the Office's films: the Office makes them, and the press estate distributes them unaltered, as it prints the tables (T7).
- In August 1914 the Realm hears Julian for the first time, and older listeners hear Elias's cadence in him. From then on his broadcasts are part of his command.

## H. The Union and abroad (P-20)

- **Film.** Los Angeles is Pacifica's, so the Union's studios stay in New York and Chicago. *Hist.:* Selig and Essanay began in Chicago. The Union's studios license Lauste's track and make talkies from about 1922. In 1923 the Union sets a quota on Pacifican films in its cinemas. *Hist.:* Britain's Cinematograph Films Act, 1927.
- **Radio and television.** The Union's radio is commercial from 1920 (canon). Its television runs on Pacifican licences from 1930–31, beside its own mechanical sets (*Hist.*: Jenkins, 1928); the analogue road is the Union's (planning note §3).
- **The partners' CR:** Takayanagi and Japan's television; the benshi and the coming of sound to Japan's cinemas; Japan's records and radio; Shanghai's films; Manila's own studios; Termen.

## I. Society by 1932 (P-21)

- **Radio:** about one home in five by 1920 and most by 1928 (planning note §9, MODEL). Families listen together through the loudspeaker from about 1915; before that the loudspeaker is heard in halls, theatres and squares.
- **Cinema:** most cinemas are wired for sound by about 1922, and the Realm's talking pictures fill the screens of the Pacific and Latin America.
- **Records:** electrical, pressed in resin and sold everywhere the radio is.
- **Television:** a few thousand sets and the viewing rooms.
- **The press:** the picture paper, the newsreel, the radio news and Lippmann's seat (P-3 (a)).
- **Language:** the air's six languages on the air and on the screen, and the tongue vote growing with the air school.
- **Music:** the new electric music of Part E.
- **Politics:** amplified rallies, the Party's purchased minutes in the Office's tables, and Julian's voice in Elias's cadence.
- **Abroad:** the Union's quota and its revanchist air; the Realm's films as the Pacific's.

## Canon tensions

- **T1. Projected film by 1889–90.** The storyline says the Bread March of 1894 is tried "on film" and that Dickson gives the press seat moving pictures "from 1895". The power timeline (`power_timeline_1865_1900.md`, "Kaiulani's measure of nations", APPROVED under CR-015) goes further: Muybridge's work is funded until projected film comes around 1888–90, with newsreels in music halls from Chicago to Berlin in the 1890s. The outline missed that line.
  - *Case A:* keep 1895. The 1894 trial is photographed and its testimony taken on phonograph cylinders.
  - *Case B:* Dickson comes in 1894.
  - *Case C, the power timeline's plan:*
    - Muybridge's sequences from 1878, paid for by the Queen's government at Kalākaua's request; Le Prince brought to San Francisco by Kalākaua's office in 1887, his contract taken over by the press estate on 1 August 1887; projected film in 1889–90; newsreels abroad in the 1890s; Dickson from 1895 for production, the newsreel and sound. Part C sets it out.
    - The storyline's "from 1895 W. K. L. Dickson, recruited from Edison, gives it moving pictures" becomes "from 1889–90 Louis Le Prince's camera gives it projected film, and from 1895 W. K. L. Dickson, recruited from Edison, runs its film unit".
  - **The test.** By 1888–89 most parts existed in different places: Le Prince's camera, the celluloid base, perforated strips and projection of motion drawn from photographs (the zoopraxiscope). *Hist.:* Le Prince's projections never reached an audience outside his circle; Edison disdained projection as unprofitable and chose the peephole; Reynaud's projected drawn strips ran in Paris from 1892; Lumière's paid screenings began in December 1895. No one in 1889 had the money, the electric light and a reason to show pictures to crowds. The press estate has all three. The risks are, first, an intermittent projector that moves celluloid through the gate steadily and safely; then short films (the Latham loop, which lets long films run, comes in 1895), nitrate fires under an arc lamp and slow emulsions indoors. They limit 1889–90 to short open-air subjects rather than a standing cinema, which suits the newsreel. The lead is five or six years, the size of CR-077's, and it meets the rule of parts, need and people with two historical people.
  - **Cost:** two new people, Muybridge and Le Prince; the planning note's C1 moves from 1895 to 1889–90.
  - **Decided C** (author, 11:43).
- **T2. Hearst's seat is PROPOSED** (CR-027). P-1 decides it.
- **T3. Hearst reaches sixty-eight in 1931** (CR-038), so the press seat changes hands before 1932. P-3 decides who takes it.
- **T4. The planning note's C6** gives the historical date of public address as "1919–20s". It was 24 December 1915, at San Francisco. The Pacifican date also moves from about 1915 to 1913 (P-4). The note's table is corrected on integration.
- **T5. "Hearst's editors"** (CR-050, CR-078) are the estate's newspaper editors under P-2.
- **T6. Zworykin.** The partners' brief (§10, Russia) names him among the émigrés who left for the Union. He goes to the Realm instead (P-10), and the brief is amended.
- **T7. "The Office's newsreels"** (CR-041) and "publication belongs to the press seat" (bible). Decided: the Office films, and the press estate distributes; the rule is Victoria's, of 1913.
- **T9. Film firms and the state's stake.** Storyline §25 (CR-057): "Competitive steel, motor, chemical, electrical, film, aluminium and aircraft firms carry no state stake." The press estate, in which the state holds shares, owns a film unit and television.
  - *Case A:* the estate's film and television are estate businesses beside private studios, and the line reads "film firms other than the press estate's".
  - *Case B:* the film unit is spun off as a private firm, and the estate keeps only the newsreel.
  - **Decided A** (author, 12:13).
- **T8. Dickson in the Union.** *Hist.:* he co-founded Biograph and went to England in 1897. Here Biograph is founded without him in the Union, or not at all; nothing on the page depends on it.
