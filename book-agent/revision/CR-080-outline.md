## 0. What canon already fixes

**Japan**
- **1858–1871:** the treaty of 1858 and the alliance of 1870–71: "full Japanese tariff autonomy and no consular courts, dockyard rights at Yokosuka, a coaling lease at Hakodate, Takashima coal and foreign credit" (§14). *Hist.:* Japan regained tariff autonomy only in 1911.
- **From 1856:** fellows go both ways. Nagaoka is at Carquinez from 1887, and Kitasato's medicine learns from Pacifica.
- **The construction industry:** "By the later nineteenth century Japan has a construction industry whose speed and efficient use of labour become an example to its partner" (CR-031-R1). Japan and Pacifica are reciprocal innovators and competitors.
- **Wars:**
  - 1894: Korea becomes independent and neutral under guarantee.
  - 1898–99: Japan gains the Ussuri, Vladivostok (Urajio) and all Sakhalin.
  - 1904–05: Russia's eastern squadron is defeated, but its fleets survive.
  - 1914–18: a boom on Entente credit, after which Japan is a creditor nation.
  - 1923: the earthquake.
  - 1927: the bank rescue, and the Concord.
- **1928, the Hayama annex** (CR-054). Pacifica supplies "aluminium, magnesium, oil products, electrical equipment and heavy machinery". Japan supplies "textiles, its own nitrogen and the silk and consumer trades, builds no aluminium or heavy electrical industry against Pacifica's before 1937, and buys Pacifican equipment for its hydro". The Restoration officers call this dependence.
- **Machines and atoms:** Japan buys the Tokyo machine from Pacifica in 1931 "as the Hayama annex expects" (CR-077). Japan's telephone works build their exchanges with Pacifican partners (CR-077 G5). The atomic station lights Tokyo on 29 April 1932, on leased fuel.
- **The wire and the air:**
  - the southern wire, 1892, and the Aleutian cable, 1903 (CR-078);
  - fax carries "the Concord's newspapers, orders and contracts in kanji, Chinese and Hawaiian" from 1908–10;
  - radiotelephone to Tokyo, 1928;
  - Yagi's antenna (1926) and Okabe's magnetron (1927) are adopted at once.
- **1931–32:** the Manchurian plot is stopped, and the May 1932 coup fails. Japan is "the Pacific's second industrial power".

**China**
- **From 1868:** the Educational Mission studies in San Francisco, and Yung Wing's machinery orders go to Carquinez (PROPOSED). The Kaiping mines and Chinese steamships draw on Pacifican engineering and credit.
- **1885–1929:** the Customs Inspectorate is under Pacifican leadership (Edmund Fong) by China's appointment. China recovers it in 1928–29 under the Treaty of Nanjing.
- **From 1895:** Formosa stays Chinese under the Formosan Revenue Convention, with a Pacifican administration of the camphor monopoly, the Keelung mines and the customs. The Polytechnic of Formosa opens at Taipei in 1906.
- **Huludao:** the harbour trust (from 1910) and Zhang Zuolin's industrial district (1923).
- **Politics:**
  - 1911–12: Julian backs Sun Yat-sen.
  - 1925: Sun dies and Chiang succeeds him.
  - 1928: reunification, after Zhang Zuolin's assassination in June.
  - 1928: the Treaty of Nanjing with the Concord.
  - 1919: Shandong is returned to China at The Hague.
- **Canon's summary line:** "China, Korea and other trading regions gain more slowly through rail, power, schools and markets" (§33). See T1.

**Korea**
- **Treaties and guarantees:**
  - about 1878–80: the first Western treaty is with Pacifica;
  - 1885–87: the Port Hamilton settlement makes Pacifica a guarantor;
  - 1888: a Pacific Company drills the king's guard at Seoul.
- **1894:** Korea becomes independent and neutral under Chinese, Japanese and Pacifican guarantee. Queen Min survives, and Gojong does not flee.
- **1897:** the Unsan gold concession.
- **After 1905:** "an independent guaranteed state with Japanese economic predominance and agreed military and transit rights; coordinated foreign policy does not make it a protectorate."

**Russia and Siberia**
- **1918–1921:** the coalition wins the Russian war. Federal Russia under Wrangel follows Harbin, and Siberia votes itself into it at Tomsk in 1921.
- **Russia's science:** Russia keeps its scientists. "Its electrical people stay in Russia or come to Pacifica instead of leaving for the Union" (planning note). Zworykin comes to the Realm from Omsk in 1919 (CR-079).
- **The settlement:** Kamchatka is a republic under Japanese protection. Japan holds a Transbaikal sphere under a civilian Resident (PROPOSED). Kolyma's gold is worked from 1929 with Pacifican dredges.
- **Credit:** reconstruction credit reaches Wrangel's government "in stages, against results".

**Licensing and its limits**
- **Open licensing:** Polytechnic patents are licensed openly at one published royalty (CR-057). Hollerith's company licenses abroad on the same terms (CR-077).
- **What is never licensed:** the cipher room's machines, the Works and radio location (sealed rolls). The export restriction covers the halls' high-power valves and pumps, not receiving or computing valves (CR-077).
- **Standards:** the AC standard "spreads with Pacifican equipment and credit through the Pacific, East Asia"; the radio standard for Asia (CR-078).
- **The cipher room** never reads a partner (CR-077 D-6).

## 1. The question, and what makes it explosive

Pacifica has spent seventy years handing its partners tools, money and schooling. This CR asks what the partners did with them. The planning note's rule 1, as amended in §8, says that a partner's invention can come early in its own country, and that a Pacifican invention can find a larger market abroad than at home.

**Three forces push the partners ahead, some of them past Pacifica:**
1. **Script.** Morse and the teleprinter suit alphabets. Kanji and Chinese characters travel better as pictures (fax) or as numbers. *Hist.:* the Chinese telegraph code of 1871 gave each character a four-digit number. A character that is a number can be punched on a card, sorted and tabulated. The partners whose scripts the telegraph handled worst may be the ones the card and the fax serve best. Hangul is an alphabet of twenty-four letters, which suits keyboards and teleprinters.
2. **Density and scale.** The partners have crowded cities, large populations and few clerks. The machines save the most labour where the registers are largest: China's customs and census, Japan's railways and posts, Korea's land survey.
3. **A late start.** The partners have no sunk plant in old technology. Japan can build its telephone system dialled from the start, and China can make its newspapers' wire a fax wire.

**Three forces hold them back, and canon fixes them:**
1. **The Hayama annex** keeps Japan out of heavy electrical work and aluminium before 1937.
2. **Japanese predominance** in Korea and on the Manchurian railway, and Japan's army.
3. **War and politics:** China's revolution and warlords to 1928, Russia's war to 1920, the slump after 1929.

**The power question** (planning note §10.5): each partner's boom enlarges the Concord's market and pool, and it also builds independence. For every proposal this CR asks which path brings Pacifica the most power and prosperity, and what it costs Pacifica in control.

## A. The frame (decisions X-1 to X-6)

- **A1. What Pacifica gives.**
  - Licences at one royalty abroad, as at home.
  - Credit.
  - Fellowships, and places at Carquinez, Ralston College and the Polytechnic of Formosa.
  - Standards: AC at 60 cycles, the radio standard for Asia, the Realm card and the fax standard.
  - The Concord's institutions.
- **A2. What Pacifica withholds:** the sealed rolls (ciphers, the Works and radio location) and the halls' high-power valves. *Recommendation:* nothing else, so no technology is withheld from a partner for commercial reasons. This is what makes the explosion possible.
- **A3. Whose inventions?** The partners' institutions own their inventions (planning note §2 rule 3: no Pacifican estates abroad). Credit stays national, as Nagaoka keeps his Japanese. A Japanese who invents in Pacifica, as Satori Kato does in 1901, is a Pacifican or a fellow by his own choice.
- **A4. The annex line.** This is the decision on which Part B turns. The annex reserves "electrical equipment and heavy machinery" to Pacifica and "the silk and consumer trades" to Japan.
  - *Recommendation:* read the line as **heavy current against light current at the terminal**.
    - **Pacifica keeps:** generators, transmission, exchanges, transmitters, cables, machines and the halls.
    - **Japan takes:** the things people buy and use at the end of the wire. These are receivers, gramophones and records, telephone sets, fax terminals, typewriters, cameras and film stock, sewing machines and looms, and from the 1920s receiving valves.
  - Before 1928 this is practice; at Hayama it is written down.
  - The effect is that Japan becomes the Concord's maker of consumer electronics in the 1920s, as it became the world's in the 1950s–70s. The Restoration officers can call that a servant's trade, and the engineers can call it the future.
  - *Alternative:* read "electrical equipment" broadly, so that Japan makes only what Pacifica does not want. Its electronics then come only after 1937.
- **A5. Canon's summary line on China and Korea** (T1). *Recommendation:* amend it. Rail, power and schools arrive slowly, and information technology quickly. The line would read: "China, Korea and other trading regions gain more slowly in rail, power and industry, and faster than anyone expected in the wire, the card and the air."
- **A6. Cast policy.** About twelve historical people across four countries, chosen because they are in canon's places already or carry a whole part. The rest are named in background only (§ Cast).

## B. Japan (questions for Part B)

- **B1. The base, 1858–1914.** Tariff autonomy from 1871, forty years before history, Pacifican credit and the alliance's market.
  - **The question:** does Japan industrialize faster, and how much? It could protect its infant industries from the start: cotton, silk machinery, shipbuilding and electrical goods.
  - *First recommendation:* yes. Japan's output per person about 1914 sits between its historical level and Italy's (MODEL), and its electrical firms start a decade early.
  - *Hist.:* Fujioka Ichisuke founded Hakunetsusha, later Tokyo Electric, in 1890; NEC was founded with Western Electric in 1899; Hitachi in 1910.
- **B2. The annex line in practice, 1900–1937** (A4). Japan's light-current industry grows on Pacifican licences:
  - receivers from about 1914;
  - gramophones and records, which ties to CR-079;
  - telephone sets, with Pacifican partners on the exchanges (CR-077 G5);
  - the fax terminal (B4);
  - receiving valves by the 1920s.

  *Hist.:* Matsushita (1918) and Hayakawa (1912; crystal sets 1925) are the consumer electrical firms. The heavy-electrical firms (Shibaura, Hitachi) stay inside the annex's limit and grow on hydro work bought from Pacifica.
- **B3. Japanese inventions: early, on time, or used at once.** Birth dates bind the people, not the inventions (planning note §10). The candidates:

|---|---|---|
| The automatic loom (Toyoda Sakichi, born 1867) | Type G, 1924; Platt bought the rights in 1929 | earlier, about 1910–15, on cheap electric drive; sold abroad at the royalty |
| Magnet steel KS (Honda Kōtarō, Tohoku, born 1870) | 1917 | on time; already in CR-079's pickup |
| Fax for kanji (Niwa Yasujirō, born 1893; NEC) | NE type, 1928, first used for the enthronement photographs | earlier: Japan's terminal maker for the Concord's fax wire from about 1915–20 (C2) |
| The directive antenna (Yagi, born 1886) | 1926 | on time, adopted at once (canon) |
| The split-anode magnetron (Okabe, born 1896) | 1927 | on time (canon) |

| The Japanese typewriter (Sugimoto Kyōta) | 1915 | on time; the basis for a kanji keyboard on the fax wire |
| Switching theory (Nakashima Akira, born 1908) | 1934–36 | cannot come much before 1932 (born 1908); a note only |
| Ferrite (Kato and Takei) | 1930 | on time (canon, CR-077) |

- **B4. Kanji and the wire.**
  - Japan's telegraph uses kana. *Hist.:* the wabun code.
  - Kanji go by fax. A kana teleprinter could come early; Japan's newspapers and ministries take to fax.
  - *Question:* does Japan put kanji on cards through a four-digit code like China's, for its railways, posts and census?
- **B5. Japan's air and screen.**
  - **Broadcasting:** *Hist.:* Tokyo's first station, JOAK, opened in 1925. Here the air school's Japanese lessons go out from Honolulu from 1916, so Japan's own state broadcasting probably comes about 1916–20.
  - **The coming of sound:** the benshi (film narrators) and the coming of sound to Japan's cinemas.
  - **Music:** Japanese records, and Japan's side of the new music (CR-079 P-21).
- **B6. Japan's machines.** Canon fixes the purchase of the Tokyo machine in 1931 (CR-077). Japan makes the parts: valves, ferrite and relays. It uses card machines on Hollerith's licence from the 1890s. *Question:* does a Japanese calculating industry start inside the annex's light-current side?
- **B7. Politics.** Japan's electronics boom gives the Restoration officers' complaint of dependence a counterweight in national pride, and it also gives Japan an industry it could take out of the Concord. *Question:* does this sharpen or soften the May 1932 coup? (Canon: the coup fails.)
- **B8. People** (choose three or four): Fujioka Ichisuke, Toyoda Sakichi, Niwa Yasujirō, Matsushita Kōnosuke, Takayanagi Kenjirō, and possibly Shibusawa Eiichi as the organizer of Japanese industry.

## C. The links and the scripts (questions for Part C)

- **C1. Manila to the Customs ports** (CR-078 E-6, approved in principle).
  - *First recommendation:* a cable from Manila by Formosa (Keelung) to Amoy, Foochow and Shanghai, about 1895–1900, laid after the Formosan Revenue Convention. It is run by the telegraph estate under China's landing grants and the Inspectorate's use.
  - It lands only on Pacifican, allied or Chinese soil, so it lets the Inspectorate's ports talk to Manila and Thalassia without British wires.
  - *Hist.:* Great Northern's Shanghai–Hong Kong cable of 1871; Eastern Extension's Hong Kong–Manila cable of 1880.
- **C2. Who makes the fax machines.** CR-078 left this open.
  - *Recommendation:* the telegraph estate sets the standard and builds the central stations. The terminals in the Concord's newspapers and offices are Japanese-made from about 1915, under A4, and Pacifican before that.
- **C3. Characters as numbers.**
  - The Chinese telegraph code (1871) and the Realm card (1890) can meet. A card column that holds a four-digit character code lets machines sort, count and print Chinese, and with a kana or kanji code Japanese.
  - *Question:* who does it first? *First recommendation:* the Customs' statistical office at Shanghai under the Pacifican Inspectorate, in the 1890s, for the trade returns. This would be the world's first machine processing of a non-alphabetic script.
- **C4. The Concord's standards.** AC at 60 cycles, the radio bands, the fax standard, the card and the character codes. Each partner adopts them or not. *Question:* does Japan keep its historical 50/60-cycle split, or take 60 throughout?
- **C5. Languages on the air.** The air school's six languages (CR-078) against the partners' own stations.

## D. China (questions for Part D)

- **D1. The Inspectorate's numbers, 1885–1929.** The Customs' statistics go on cards (C3). From 1928–29, when China recovers the Inspectorate, it inherits a statistical machine and the people trained to run it. *Question:* does Nationalist China build its census and land tax on it?
- **D2. Characters on the machine.**
  - **The telegraph:** *Hist.:* Sheng Xuanhuai ran the Imperial Telegraph Administration from 1881.
  - **The Chinese typewriter:** *Hist.:* Zhou Houkun, born 1889, MIT 1915, built one at Shanghai's Commercial Press in the mid-1910s.
  - **Indexing:** *Hist.:* Wang Yunwu's four-corner indexing, 1920s.
  - **Newspapers:** fax at Shanghai's newspapers.
  - *Question:* does Shanghai become the centre of character machines, with the Commercial Press as its maker?
- **D3. Engineers.**
  - The Educational Mission in San Francisco (canon, PROPOSED). *Hist.:* Zhan Tianyou, born 1861, a member of the historical mission, built the Beijing–Zhangjiakou railway in 1909.
  - The Polytechnic of Formosa (1906) and the Boxer-indemnity scholarships.
  - Fong's Customs staff.
- **D4. Radio and mass politics: the explosive case.**
  - **Sun Yat-sen:** Honolulu-educated and backed by Pacifica, he could speak to China on the air from Canton in the 1920s.
  - **The Nationalists' radio:** *Hist.:* their Central Broadcasting Station at Nanjing dates from 1928.
  - **Literacy:** *Hist.:* James Yen's Mass Education Movement (1923) taught about 1,000–1,300 basic characters, and his Ding Xian experiment ran from 1926. Literacy by radio, beside the air school's method, is the strongest proposal in this part.
  - *Questions:* who controls the transmitters, and what does cheap radio do to China's warlords, to the Nationalists and to Japanese officers in Manchuria? The Communists are not in canon. With Moscow beaten in 1920, does a Chinese communist movement arise from its own roots? This is OPEN, and a candidate to leave out.
- **D5. Industry.** The Huludao district of 1923 (canon) as an electrical district. Shanghai's light industry. China as a second maker of consumer goods beside Japan, outside the annex because China is not a party.
- **D6. Film and records.** Shanghai's studios, and the Cantonese films of San Francisco (CR-079).
- **D7. The control problem.** A China that reads, listens and computes faster than it industrializes. The Formosan grievance. Japan's army's resentment of Chinese customs at Huludao (canon). Pacifica's interest is a strong China that stays a friend.

## E. Korea (questions for Part E)

- **E1. Status.** Korea is independent and guaranteed, with Japanese economic predominance (canon). It is not a party to the Concord.
- **E2. Hangul and the machine.**
  - *Hist.:*
    - Korean Morse code, 1888 (Kim Hak-woo);
    - the *Tongnip Sinmun*, all in Hangul, from 1896, founded by Seo Jae-pil;
    - Ju Si-gyeong (1876–1914) and the standard language;
    - the first Hangul typewriter, 1914.
  - *First recommendation:* Korea adopts the teleprinter, the typewriter and the card faster than any partner, because Hangul is an alphabet. A Hangul teleprinter about 1910, cards for the land survey and census, and Hangul newspapers by wire.
- **E3. Korea's own reformers.**
  - *Hist.:* Seo Jae-pil, born 1864, fled after the Gapsin coup of 1884, trained as a physician in the United States and was exiled again in 1898. Canon has Pacifica "knowingly stand aside" from the Gapsin coup. *Question:* does he come to Pacifica instead?
  - *Hist.:* the Korean Empire's Gwangmu reforms of 1897–1904. Queen Min survives (canon).
- **E4. Japanese predominance.** Korea's railways, mines and banks lean on Japan. Its information industries could be Korea's own ground.

## F. Russia and Siberia (questions for Part F)

- **F1. Russia's science stays.**
  - **Radio:** Bonch-Bruevich's radio laboratory at Nizhny Novgorod (*Hist.:* 1918; it made valves from 1919) belongs here to federal Russia.
  - **Crystals:** Losev (born 1903) found amplification in a zincite crystal in 1922. This bears on CR-077, where crystal amplifiers are trials in Pacifica: Russia could be ahead.
  - **Others:** Ioffe's physical-technical institute; Termen's instrument (1920); Shukhov's towers.
- **F2. Electrification on Pacifican credit.** Federal Russia's electrification plan, the non-Bolshevik counterpart of history's GOELRO plan, financed against results (canon). The Volga's and the Urals' power.
- **F3. Siberia.**
  - Pacifican standards along the railway board's line.
  - *Hist.:* Tomsk's institutes; the Technological Institute opened in 1900.
  - Kolyma's dredges (canon).
- **F4. Russia as a rival.** Federal Russia is a partner and a competitor in radio and electronics, between the Concord and Germany's eastern clients.

## Canon tensions (early list; each part will state its own)

- **T1. "China, Korea and other trading regions gain more slowly through rail, power, schools and markets"** (§33). The strongest version of this CR has them gain fast in information. A5 recommends an amendment.
- **T2. The annex** (CR-054) reserves "electrical equipment" to Pacifica. A4's reading, which gives Japan light current at the terminal, has to be stated in the annex's text and the ledger (C-1928-HAYAMA-ANNEX).
- **T3. The Tokyo machine is bought from Pacifica in 1931** (CR-077). Any Japanese computing industry must start from parts and stay inside the annex.
- **T4. "Japan becomes the Pacific's second industrial power"** (§33) stands. B1's faster start must not make Japan the first.
- **T5. The planning note's rule 3** (no Pacifican estates abroad) holds. The C1 cable is the telegraph estate's on Chinese landing grants, like the southern wire on Japanese ones.
- **T6. The Educational Mission in San Francisco** is PROPOSED. Part D may depend on it.
- **T7. CR-077's crystal amplifiers** stay trials in Pacifica to 1932. F1 could put Russia ahead with Losev.
