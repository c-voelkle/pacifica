## 3. Coal and iron

**Kaiping**
- Founded 1877–78 to fuel the arsenals and the China Merchants' steamers (FS 2).
- Passed under foreign control, unlike the other official enterprises, which passed to Chinese private investors (CE 48). The transfer was to the Anglo-Belgian Chinese Engineering and Mining Company in 1900–01, with Herbert Hoover as the engineer who arranged it (section 10).
- Merged in 1912 with a rival Chinese firm to form the Kailan Mining Administration (R 309).
- At Kailan, 1920–36, wages came to 55% of cumulative profits for the whole enterprise (R 308).

**Hanyang and Hanyeping**
- Zhang Zhidong's Hanyang ironworks (CE 45 gives no date for the ironworks; its 1890 date is the Hanyang arsenal's). The Hanyang–Daye works began production in 1894 (CE 49), several years before Japan's Yawata works (R 68).
- Sheng took it over in 1896 with the Daye iron mines; the Pingxiang coal mines followed in 1898 (FS 9).
- Joined in 1908 as the Han-Yeh-Ping Coal and Iron Corporation, authorized capital $20 million (about 14 million taels); the China Merchants' company held 1,019,000 taels of its shares (FS 182).
- Unable to repay its Japanese loans, it fell deeper into debt to the lenders, which became the pretext for the Hanyeping clause of the Twenty-One Demands (CE 82).
- Cost: pig iron at Hanyang cost Ch.$48.50 a ton in 1919; Japan's Benxi works in Manchuria made it for Ch.$22.00 in 1915 (CE 147).
- Hanyang held the monopoly of rails for the Peking–Hankow and Canton–Hankow lines (FS 29).

**Coal output** (already in the outline from other sources): 8,987,862 tons in 1912, over 23 million in 1926, over 27 million in 1931. The modern mines' ownership is in the Table 32 row above.

## 8. The hinterland (Pomeranz)

Huang-Yun is the inland region of western Shandong and the adjacent parts of Zhili/Hebei and Henan along the Grand Canal, with more than fifteen million people (P 220).

- **What the state gave up:** in 1891 the Qing reformed Yellow River control and put most of its burden in this area on the localities; in 1901–02 it ended the grain tribute by the Grand Canal and left the canal too to local hands (P 13). Railways and German troops came to Shandong from 1897 (P 13).
- **Where the floods went:** before 1891 major Yellow River floods fell mostly on lower Shandong near the mouth; after it they shifted upstream into Huang-Yun (P 207, Table 3).
- **The cost:** a lower-bound annual loss in the 1920s–30s of about 33.7 million yuan in crops (canal lakes, canal floods, the widened Yellow River channel, dike breaks, the Wanfu River) (P 215, Table 4) and 7–9 million yuan in extra transport costs (P 219, Table 5). With minimal property damage, 48–51 million yuan a year, about a tenth of the region's farm output, and plausibly 100 million, a fifth (P 218–20).
- **Set against the world economy's gain:** American cotton varieties, the largest foreign boost, added perhaps 8–15 million yuan a year (P 220).
- **Fuel:** the canal had carried wood into a treeless region; when that trade ended the fuel shortage became a crisis; state reforestation went where it could be paid for in hard currency, not here; lack of pit props held back Shandong's coal (P 82, 123, 128).
- **Why:** under foreign competition and nationalism, the state put its flood control near the coast and near railway bridges, where revenue and the foreign threat were, and withdrew from Huang-Yun (P 210). In the 1920s a Yellow River contract went to an American bid of 1.5 million yuan against the river office's 3 million, and projects were refused at the mere possibility of Japanese involvement, among them a Grand Canal restoration (P 210).

## 9. What the series change in the outline

Revised on 20 November 2026 after the fresh-context review (`CR-081-review.md`). The outline's A8 carries the resulting recommendations.

1. **Railways.** History built 9,253 km in 1895–1911, 544 km a year (CE 151). The proposal of 11,000–12,000 km by 1911 is 1.2–1.3 times that pace and credible with early credit. History then slowed for political reasons (298 km a year in 1913–27). The revised path is about 14,000–15,000 km by 1927 and 16,000–17,000 km by 1932. In canon the Manchurian lines are Chinese-owned, which changes ownership rather than kilometres: Rawski's track figures already include them.
2. **Coal.** Modern mines were 93% foreign-controlled in 1913 and 78% in 1928 (CE 119). Kaiping/Kailan and Fushun were the two largest foreign-controlled producers. With both Chinese, the Chinese-owned share comes to roughly 70–85% by 1928. Keelung's coal is outside these series and is reported separately in canon's Formosan administration.
3. **Iron.** Hanyang started before Yawata but its iron cost more than twice Benxi's (Ch.$48.50 a ton in 1919 against Ch.$22.00 in 1915). Feuerwerker puts the gap down to slow progress on the Canton–Hankow line and the long river haul of coke, not to prices (CE 147). Coke made at Benxi cost Ch.$5.74 a ton; at Hanyang, brought from Pingxiang by river, it cost Ch.$24.54. That points Pacifican credit at coke and haulage.
4. **Cotton.** Chinese-owned mills made 71% of the yarn in 1933 but only 39% of the cloth.
5. **Electricity.** Foreign utilities held 77% of output in 1923 and 55% in 1936; output grew 14% a year. History's 4 kWh a head in 1936 implies about 2.4 kWh in 1932. Two to three times that is about 5–7 kWh a head.
6. **Output per person.** In 1933 prices the modern sector was about 4.7% of GDP (Liu and Yeh, CE 95). Doubling it adds about 5%. Pomeranz's losses in Huang-Yun, 48–100 million yuan a year, are 0.2–0.3% of China's GDP of 29.88 billion; the waterworks matter for floods and famine, not for national output. A gain of 5–10% above Rawski's path needs the modern sector at about twice history's and one to three points from agriculture. Rawski's path is itself about a quarter above Perkins's flat path over 1912–33.
7. **The state's money.** In the early 1890s the customs were a quarter of the centre's revenue, railways got half a million taels of its spending and the two fleets ten million (CE 76).
8. **Foreign capital.** Russia held 31% of foreign investment in 1902, mostly the Chinese Eastern Railway, and Japan 40% by 1936; Britain held 33–38% throughout (CE 174). In canon Russia's and Japan's shares change; Britain's position becomes a question.
9. **The China Merchants' company.** History's company stopped growing in 1877, had no Chinese officers in 1885 and gave up its Honolulu and San Francisco voyages of 1880–81 (FS 183–84).
10. **Telegraph.** About 54,000 km of line in 1902 (Administration and provincial) and 62,000 km in 1912; inland telegrams stayed at 2–4 million a year.

  - In 1868–1914 China took only 5–10% of the rice exports of Siam, Cochin-China, Indo-China and India, most of it into the southern ports (Amoy, Canton, Kowloon, Lappa, Swatow).
  - In 1901–1910 it imported about 6 million cwt a year (about 0.3 Mt), less than 7% of those exporters' trade; the yearly figures run from 1.15 million cwt (1902) to 15.19 million (1907).
  - In 1911–1920 its net imports were about 8% of Monsoon Asia's net rice exports.
  - In 1921–1935 its average imports almost tripled, to about 15% of Monsoon Asia's exports, which rose from 4.3 to 7.5 million tonnes between 1911–15 and 1931–35. That puts China's imports at about 0.6–1.1 million tonnes of rice a year in 1921–35 (arithmetic on Brandt's shares, MODEL).
- **The 1931 flood loan** (FRUS, verified): in September 1931 the Union's Grain Stabilization Corporation sold China 15 million bushels of wheat and flour, 450,000 tons, on credit for US$9,212,826.56 at 4%. It was serviced from the Customs' 5% flood-relief surtax and taken over by the Export-Import Bank in 1936.
- **Today** (China's General Administration of Customs, via Reuters, verified): China imported 99.41 million tonnes of soybeans in 2023, about 70% of them from Brazil. It is the world's largest soybean importer, which B11 assumed.
**Wheat flour and the crop, 1914–1930** (author's research note, 22 November 2026; figures as given there, still to read against the tables named)

- **Flour imports:**
  - Flour imports fell from 2,162,424 piculs in 1914 (about 0.13 Mt) to 157,652 piculs in 1915 (about 0.01 Mt), and Shanghai's and Wuxi's roller mills took the home market.
  - Imported flour was 1.8% of China's import value in 1920 (Yu-kwei Cheng, p. 34) and 2.06% in 1928–29.
  - Two surges: 1922–24 (Yangtze floods, cheap North American wheat) and 1928–29 (northern famine, rail disruption, falling world prices). In between, 1925–27, Shanghai mills took more foreign wheat and less flour.
  - The yearly quantities are in Hsiao Liang-lin, *China's Foreign Trade Statistics, 1864–1949*: Table 2.5 ("Wheat Flour", imports, 1,000 piculs and 1,000 Hk. Tls.), read with Table 3.11 (exports). A retrospective summary is in the Customs' *The Trade of China, 1933*, vol. 1, part 2.
- **Manchuria's soybean crop:**
  - About 3.0–3.5 Mt in the early 1920s and 5.3–5.4 Mt by 1929–30, roughly as much as the rest of China, and about 61% of the world's soybean output in 1929.
  - Some SMR and IIA estimates put its share of the world's commercial export supply at about 80%.
  - Standardised all-Manchuria crop surveys by the SMR Research Bureau begin with the 1924 harvest (1924–32). Figures before that are reconstructions from rail shipments.
  - Tables: Kungtu C. Sun, *The Economic Development of Manchuria in the First Half of the Twentieth Century* (ch. 2, around Table 23); the SMR's *Report on Progress in Manchuria, 1907–1928* (agriculture section); Prodöhl, *Globalizing the Soybean* (the 1924–32 series).
- **What this changes:** Manchuria's whole crop about 1930 is a little above provisioning.md's export figure of 5.0 Mt, so that figure is an upper bound for the export (outline G-1). China's flour imports peaked in the two famine years, which supports reading China as a buyer in bad years (B11).
