## Summary verdict

Part C is a sound frame and most of it should stand. Its main idea is right: Pacifica picks a Chinese leader twice (1911 and 1928) and in between deals with whoever holds Peking, the provinces and Manchuria through revenue, works and people. The *Hist.* lines are mostly accurate. I found one date wrong, three claims doubtful or overstated, and one historical premise (P-20's mixed courts) that history had already abolished.

Six problems need fixing before decision:

1. **P-20 goes backwards from history and touches canon.** The Shanghai Mixed Court was closed on 1 January 1927, and Chinese courts took over in the Settlement under the agreement of 17 February 1930. "Mixed courts with foreign assessors" for ten years would bring back what Nanjing had already recovered. A jurisdictional "joint-management zone" on the Manchurian line also moves toward the territorial corridor that canon has Zhang Xueliang refuse (SL 1483) and that guaranteed transit is meant to answer (SL 1485).
2. **"Works, not factions" does not hold as written.** Canon has Pacifica fund Sun in Canton from 1916–17 (SL 1290), support Whampoa with "Pacifican money" (SL 1483), and bring Peking into the Russian war in 1918 (SL 1352), and Peking was then Duan Qirui's. P-12 and P-14 admit that the works account frees Canton's revenue for its army. Part C should say openly that Pacifica funds factions indirectly, or decide how it funds them directly. At present it does neither. Canon tensions (line 569) lists P-14's reading as "none a conflict with canon". It is a real tension and should be presented as one, with both cases (R8).
3. **A key historical fact is missing from P-5 and P-12.** In March 1921 Peking pledged the customs surplus to the service of its internal loans, and Canton's 1923 claim ran into that pledge (FRUS 1923, vol. I, doc. 401). Whether Fong does the same decides whether Pacifica's Inspector-General finances Peking's factions in 1921–28. That bears directly on A8.4.
4. **P-22 gets canon's cause wrong.** Canon says Thalassia's map of Manchuria stopped with Mikhailov's death in January 1928, and that Zhang Zuolin's killers were Japanese officers (SL 1593). Not reading Chinese traffic does not explain that surprise. The recommendation itself is consistent with D-6. The reasoning given for it is not.
5. **Gaps that change recommendations.** Part C does not decide:
   - the Nishihara loans and Peking's 1918 bargain;
   - Zhang Zuolin 1916–28, including the 1924 coup that puts Puyi in Tianjin, as canon requires (SL 1655);
   - China's entry into the war in 1917, which it needs to be at The Hague;
   - Japan's presence in Shandong that leads to Jinan;
   - where the Nationalists get arms without Soviet shipments;
   - Weihaiwei, whose 1898 lease was tied to Russia holding Port Arthur, which canon denies;
   - Formosa's politics, opium, and the railways as the warlords' prize.
6. **The aim is served but not measured.** A8 has a 1932 material target. Part C has no political target for 1932: which provinces Nanjing really governs, how large and how loyal its army is, what Japan has lost and gained. One history-based offset to Japan's cotton loss is not used: Japan's tariff agreement with China of 6 May 1930.

Smaller issues: a wrong date in C4 (the Demands were accepted on 9 May, not 25 May); the 1911 cabinet's composition; "open ballot" used to mean contested elections, when Pacifica's 1911 reform was the *secret* ballot; several canon claims missing their PROPOSED labels; and about six balanced or epigrammatic sentences (section F).

### A2. Proposals that contradict canon or stretch it without saying so

1. **P-14's "indirect" money (line 459) against SL 1290 and SL 1483.** Canon says "Pacifica funds Sun in Canton" from the Yuan years and "Pacifican money supports Whampoa". Chinese Pacificans' private subscriptions are not Pacifica's money. The works account only starts in 1923, so it cannot cover 1917–23. A8.7 (decided) also says "officers train at Whampoa on Pacifican money". Canon tensions (line 569) calls this "none a conflict". It should be listed as a tension with (a) indirect money, with canon amended to "Chinese-Pacifican money", and (b) canon kept, with declared Pacifican funding and a stated reason it does not breach "Works, not factions". See D, P-14.
2. **C0's rule "Works, not factions" (line 363) against canon's 1918.** A8.4 bans lending to *Peking factions* in 1916–28. Canon's Russian war needs Peking's agreement and Chinese soldiers on the line (SL 1352, 1358). In 1918 Peking means Duan Qirui's Anhui clique, the borrower of the Nishihara loans. Someone pays the Chinese brigade. Part C should say who (E8).
3. **P-1 (line 374): "Victoria's friendship with Cixi belongs to 1898–99".** Canon has the regent broker the 1894 armistice, after which "the Qing retain solvency and an ally in the regent" (SL 1063). It also leaves "the scenes of Victoria's friendship with Cixi … for later edits" (SL 59, CR-042 integration). Dating the friendship to 1898–99 closes an item canon left open, and it sits awkwardly beside canon's 1894. Say instead that the friendship does not extend to the arsenals or to court politics.
4. **P-10 (line 430): "In canon the boycotts of 1915 and 1928 are short and those of 1919 and 1931 do not happen."** Canon says nothing about boycotts. This is a proposal labelled as canon.
5. **P-22 (line 494)** gives the not-reading of Chinese traffic as the reason Zhang Zuolin's killing surprises Thalassia. Canon (SL 1593, APPROVED CR-045) gives Mikhailov's death as the reason, and the killers were Japanese officers, whom D-6 never reads. Feng's 1924 coup is in Peking, not "there" (Manchuria), so it does not clash with "the first men there whom Thalassia does not see coming". The 1928 attribution should be corrected.
6. **P-20 (line 488): the "joint-management zone" with its own mixed courts.** Canon gives Japan "guaranteed transit and joint railway management" in answer to "the army's territorial demands" (SL 1485), and has Zhang Xueliang "hostile to Japan's demand for a territorial corridor" (SL 1483). A strip with its own jurisdiction is a step toward that corridor. This is a canon tension and is not listed as one.
7. **Weihaiwei (lines 480, 488).** The British lease of 1 July 1898 ran "for so long a period as Port Arthur shall remain in the occupation of Russia". In canon Russia never gets Port Arthur (SL 1071, APPROVED CR-042). Unless Part C gives Britain another reason for the lease, there is no Weihaiwei to return in 1930. Part C repeats history here without saying so.
8. **C1 Canon line (372): "and is not recalled".** Canon (SL 748) has the mission in San Francisco (PROPOSED) and says nothing on recall. D0.1 asks the question. Part C answers it inside a "Canon:" line. Move it to a proposal (P-1, or a new P-0).
9. **C8 Britain (line 499):** "the lien on the Mukden–Huludao line (canon)". The lien is PROPOSED CR-042 (SL 1263). "Its retreat after 1927 (canon)" is not in the excerpts and I could not find it. Give the source paragraph, or label it.
10. **P-12 and canon's Companies.** Canon has a Pacific Company in the Customs' preventive service under a treaty of service with the recognised government from 1888 (SL 991, APPROVED CR-074). In 1923 that is Peking. P-12 ignores this. Fong's own armed revenue service, not foreign warships, would be the first force facing Sun at Canton (see D, P-12).

**Important historical facts Part C omits that would change a recommendation**

- **The 1921 internal-loan pledge of the customs surplus** (FRUS 1923 doc. 401). This changes P-5, P-12 and the "Works, not factions" rule.
- **Nanjing's internal debt on the customs.** It floated 38 internal issues with a face value of Ch.$1,634 million in 1927–35. In January 1932 payments due were about Ch.$200 million, "a third of the revenue anticipated", which forced the 1932 reorganisation. The cause was the "political inability to 'break rice bowls' by reducing the swollen … armies" (CE 165). This changes P-19 and P-21, and it bears on A8.4's "first charge".
- **The Mixed Court** closed on 1 January 1927, and Chinese courts took over in the Settlement under the agreement of 17 February 1930 (FRUS 1930 vol. II ch. 9). This changes P-20.
- **The Sino-Japanese tariff agreement**, signed at Nanking on 6 May 1930 (FRUS 1930 doc. 653). In history it bought Japan's acceptance of China's tariff autonomy with fixed rates on certain Japanese goods, cotton goods among them, for a term. Verify the schedule before using it. It offers a direct offset to A8's "largest cost" to Japan.
- **Deng Yanda**, the historical non-communist left of the Nationalists. He was on Whampoa's preparatory committee, a Northern Expedition commander, broke with Chiang in 1927, founded the "Third Party" in 1930, and was arrested in the International Settlement in August 1931 and executed by Nanjing on 29 November 1931 (Wikipedia, *Deng Yanda*). This changes P-16 and P-18: it is history's evidence of how Nanjing treated an agrarian left without communists.
- **He Jian**, governor of Hunan 1929–37, whose subordinate led the 21 May 1927 attack on the peasant associations. This changes P-18.
- **Japan's Shandong expeditions of 1927–28** (Tanaka). Jinan was their result. This changes P-8, P-10 and the Shandong settlement.
- **The Weihaiwei lease condition.** See A2.7.

## D. Decision-by-decision assessment

Each entry gives the verdict on credibility and the aim (strong, aligned China; minimal cost to Japan), the second-order effects, and a better option where one exists.

**P-1. The partner before 1894 (Li and Sheng).** Credible and well grounded: historically the recalled students went into Li's and Sheng's telegraph, mining and naval offices. It serves the aim. The fix is the Cixi dating (A2.3). Second-order: tying Pacifica to Li makes Li's fall after 1895 Pacifica's loss too. Canon's milder 1895 keeps Li in office longer, and Part C should say so in C2.

**P-2. Fleet credit lent in money.** Credible, and it protects canon's 1894 result and Japan's trust. One cost: canon gives the mission "naval-academy places" (SL 748). Sending the naval graduates "mainly to the merchant line" spends that premise on almost nothing. In history several mission men died at Mawei (1884) and the Yalu (1894). A better option: a minority serve in the Beiyang fleet, as in history, and some die at the Yalu. That gives the mission's generation a stake in 1894 without Pacifican ships or officers.

**P-3. Reformers in exile.** Credible. The "no arms" rule is consistent with canon's 1897 steamer incident (SL 1122). Fix the 1905 overstatement (B #41). Second-order for the Chinese Pacificans: two rival Chinese parties raising money inside Pacifica touch the Prosperity Party's "Hui" among Chinese members (SL 614). Part C should say whether the Party tolerates them, competes with them or absorbs their local men. Prose flag on "protects the exiles' lives, not their plans" (F).

**P-4. Why the Qing still falls.** Credible and necessary. **Inconsistency with A1 and A8.4:** canon's PROPOSED 1896 credit is for "the Hankow–Canton trunk". If Pacifica has financed that line since 1896, the 1911 nationalisation and the Huguang loan concern mainly the Sichuan–Hankou line and the Sichuan shareholders. P-4 should say so. P-7 (Zhan on Canton–Hankow after 1912) and P-12 (the "southern section" still a works item in 1923) suggest the trunk is unfinished in 1923, 27 years after the credit. Give the trunk's completion date (proposal: by about 1915, inside A8.4's 11,000–12,000 km of 1911).

**P-5. The customs trusteeship.** The 1912 decision is credible and keeps faith with Pacifica's savers. It does not serve the aim unless Part C answers the 1921 question: does Fong, like Aglen, make the surplus the security for Peking's internal loans?
- *(a) Yes, as history:* Peking's factions borrow on Fong's surplus, and "Works, not factions" is false in substance.
- *(b) No:* the surplus is held for the A8.10 works and the provinces, and Peking's bankers and the factions blame Fong.

I recommend (b), stated openly. It is the stronger story and the cleaner rule, and its cost (Peking's anger, a weaker Peking) suits canon's wish to keep Peking at arm's length.

Second-order of the trusteeship in canon: a smaller Shimonoseki indemnity and a partly remitted Boxer indemnity mean a larger surplus than history's. In 1912–13 that money goes to Yuan, and it may fund him against the Second Revolution. Tradeoff 1 notes this for later years. P-6 should note it for 1913, because it weakens Pacifica's stated reason for refusing the Reorganisation Loan.

Also: recognising "the Republic on the abdication" on 12 February 1912 sits between two claimants. Sun's Nanjing government holds the provisional presidency, and the abdication edict commissions Yuan. Say which Pacifica recognises (proposal: the Nanjing provisional government, which then elects Yuan on 15 February). History's recognitions came in 1913.

**P-6. The Reorganisation Loan.** Credible and in character. Japan "not yet in the Concord" lending to Yuan while Sun sits in Honolulu works. Add that canon's ally is financing the man Pacifica's candidate fights, which strains the 1907 war council. Credit P-6 with one benefit: Britain's salt inspectorate gives Britain a stake that offsets the customs loss (A8 tradeoff 6).

**P-7. Tang Shaoyi.** A good choice: historical, already in D0.1, and one man across 1906, 1912, 1919 and 1929–31. History makes him stronger than P-7 does:
- he led the southern delegation at the 1919 Shanghai peace conference (fits P-9);
- he was a director of Sun's Canton government in 1918 and its finance minister in 1921;
- he sat on the Standing Committee of the 1931 Canton opposition government (B #34).

So the head of Nanjing's showcase county joins the government that challenges Nanjing in 1931. Use that in P-21. It is the most direct way to show tradeoff 5 ("Pacifica's money takes sides in the provinces"). The cast stays the same size.

**P-8. The Twenty-One Demands.** Credible in outline. Fix the Group V overstatement (B #17) and the Daye wording (C). **Gap:** Group I (Shandong) was the core demand of 1915. In canon Japan is a co-occupier at Tsingtao (SL 1276) and Pacifica runs the anchorage. P-8 should say that Japan's claim to the German rights is deferred to the peace, where canon denies it (SL 1339), and what Japan keeps instead (A8.13). That deferral is the in-canon origin of the Japanese interests that lead to Jinan in 1928.

**P-9. Who signs for China.** Credible. It needs China to be a belligerent in canon (E9). "Pacifica asks for that" (the joint delegation) is good: in history C. T. Wang represented the south at Paris. Add Tang's 1919 role (P-7). Fix "Xu, who chartered Huludao" (A1).

**P-10. No May Fourth outrage.** The students turning on the Anfu loans and the treaty system is credible. It quietly assumes the Nishihara loans happen in canon (E8). The risk to the aim is that "Fong's customs" becomes the students' target before Japan does (tradeoff 3). A better option that keeps P-10's logic: Fong publishes in 1919–20 a dated plan for Chinese commissioners and a Chinese successor. Fong is himself a Taishan family's son born in San Francisco (SL 786). The recovery of 1928–29 then becomes a promise kept, not a concession forced. The nationalists' attitude to an ethnic Chinese Pacifican at the head of the "foreign" customs is a better story than an anonymous foreign service. Fix the "In canon" mislabel (A2.4).

**P-11. The federalists.** Credible and honest about the cost. One wording problem: "a quarrel with Sun before 1922" is unclear. Say that rewarding Chen Jiongming's Guangdong strains Pacifica's relations with Sun, whose base Chen holds until June 1922.

**P-12. The Canton customs, 1923.** This is the decision most in need of revision.
- *Credibility.* Sun wanted the surplus for his army against Chen Jiongming. A works account he cannot spend on troops is something he could accept only if he expects to free his own revenue for the army, and P-14 says exactly that. So the account funds the army at one remove. P-12 should say so.
- *Authority.* Under P-5 and the 1912 trusteeship the surplus belongs to the recognised government. Fong regionalising it is a decision against Peking, the government that canon's alliance binds in 1922 (P-9).
- *Second-order effects:*
  - Every regional holder of a customs port can cite the formula: Tianjin and Hankou under Zhili, Newchwang and Antung under Zhang Zuolin.
  - In May 1931 the Canton opposition government will cite it against Nanjing (P-21).
  - Britain's resentment is credible.
  - The Company in Fong's preventive service is under treaty with Peking. If Sun moves on the customs house, Pacificans in China's service stand against Pacifica's candidate. That is better drama than foreign warships, and canon already supplies it (SL 991).
- *Better option.* Present P-12 as an extension of A8.10: Fong has spent surplus on works since 1891. Fix the shares in advance by a published percentage for each region, as the 1919–20 instalments did (FRUS 1923 doc. 401), paid to contractors. Decide the 1921 pledge (P-5) first, because the formula depends on it. Keep "no warships needed" only if the Company guards the customs house instead.

**P-13. Chiang to Pacifica in 1923.** Credible, and the best single change for the aim. Chiang was trained in Japan (Shinbu Gakkō, 1908–10, then the 13th Field Artillery at Takada). A route through Tokyo to Thalassia, with Japanese army acquaintances on the way, gives Japan a stake in Whampoa's founder at no cost to Pacifica, in line with A8.7 ("Japan keeps training China's lawyers and doctors"). Second-order: Japanese army ties to Chiang also complicate 1928 (Jinan) in a useful way. List this as an option in P-13. Canon tension: none. CR-080's watch item is fine.

**P-14. The Nationalists' model.**
- *Credible:* published accounts and examined cadres, with T. V. Soong's bank. History gives a hook Part C misses: Sun's Five-Power Constitution already had an Examination branch, which became the Examination Yuan in 1930. The Prosperity Party example reinforces an idea Sun already held. Say so; it makes the borrowing far more credible.
- *Error:* "It refuses the open ballot" (line 457) and tradeoff 4's "the open ballot that Pacifica made its own in 1911" (line 536). An open ballot is a non-secret vote. Pacifica's 1911 reform was "the secret, state-printed ballot" (SL 97, SL 1260). Write "contested elections", or "the secret ballot and contested elections".
- *Irony to use:* canon's Pacifica is in practice a one-party system ("real politics is fought inside the Party", SL 995). The KMT copies the one-party part and omits the ballot. That is the honest form of tradeoff 4.
- *Whampoa's instructors:* Chinese Pacificans from the Sacramento school serving Canton "under private contract" may breach the spirit of the Companies Act's "never … for a pretender" (SL 987). Canton was not a recognised government. Check the Act, or say the instructors leave the Companies first, as canon's sergeants are "discharged from the companies first" before joining Japan's army mission (SL 857). Better: most instructors are Chinese officers from Baoding and Japan's academies, as in history, with Pacifican-trained men as a minority. Fewer invented people and more stake for Japan.
- *Money:* see A2.1.
- *Arms:* not addressed (E11). Without Soviet rifles and artillery (from October 1924 in history) Whampoa and the Expedition need another supplier, and P-3's "no arms" rule is Pacifica's.

**P-15. Labour in 1925.** Credible. The exemption of the China Merchants' line and the labour-article firms is close to the strike committee's historical permit system for non-British ships that did not call at Hong Kong. Second-order: Britain will read the trade shift to Pacifican ships as collusion, and Part C should let London say so. Japan's mills: in history the Shanghai dispute with the Japanese mills was settled separately in 1925, and the Canton boycott targeted Britain. "Japan's mills pay some" is right.

**P-16. The 1927 split without communists.** The split over land and labour is credible. "No Shanghai massacre because there is no Communist party to purge" is too clean. In April 1927 Chiang, the Green Gang and the bankers moved against the armed pickets of the Shanghai General Labour Union as such. A non-communist labour movement with armed pickets would still meet force, on a smaller scale. A better option: a suppression of the pickets with tens rather than thousands of dead. Name the non-communist left's historical leader, Deng Yanda (B). Otherwise, beside Wang Jingwei, the left has no face. Also say what happens to Chen Duxiu, Li Dazhao and Zhou Enlai in a world with no Comintern, even as a single line in the historical notes. They are history's leaders of the left, and the reader will ask.

**P-17. Land.** Credible, consistent with S-6, and the right line between Komuch and China. Prose flag on its last sentence (F).

**P-18. Mao.** It meets X-8, and the arc is plausible to 1927. Two problems for 1928–32:
- Hunan from 1929 is under He Jian. His subordinate attacked the peasant associations in May 1927, and he would not let a Wuhan-side organiser run the rent limit in his province. The works districts along the Canton–Hankow line must then be under a national body (the Ministry of Railways or the conservancy board), with Mao as its employee. That needs saying, and it creates a useful conflict.
- Chiang executed Deng Yanda, the leader of the non-communist agrarian left, in November 1931. A Mao who becomes head of the peasant department in 1932 needs a reason to survive where Deng did not. History supplies one: Wang Jingwei's return as premier on 28 January 1932.

Link Mao's 1932 post explicitly to Wang's return. Without that, "head of the peasant department … against Nanjing's officers and bankers" in 1932 is not credible under Chiang. Age 38: correct (born 26 December 1893).

**P-19. Soldiers.** The direction is credible. The numbers and the mechanism need work.
- *Baseline:* FRUS (January 1929) implies about 1.6 million under arms. Rawski's table gives 1.62 million for 1929. P-19's "1.7–1.9 million in 1928" (MODEL) is therefore barely below history's 1929 figure, and 1.3–1.5 million in 1932 is a cut of about 10–20% from history's 1929, not the 30–35% from 2 million that the text implies.
- *Mechanism:* Rawski's point (R 34–36) is that finance, not men, limited armies. A Nanjing with the recovered customs can pay more soldiers, and in history it did, borrowing on the customs to pay "the swollen central and provincial armies" (CE 165).
- Fewer refugees does not cut armies by itself. Commanders' unwillingness to "break rice bowls" does. The works can absorb discharged men only if the commanders release them.
- *Better option:* make demobilisation a condition written into the works contracts, consistent with P-17's district-by-district method. Its first discharges are men of the provincial armies whose commanders accept the terms, in exchange for works in their provinces. This also gives the Central Plains war a sharper cause than "because it is about power" (F).
- *Second-order:* Nanjing's own central army probably grows while the provincial armies shrink. P-19 should give both numbers.

**P-20. Extraterritoriality.**
- *Credible:* the Concord's crowns renouncing together on Nanjing's date, and it serves the aim. Note that canon's Concord has a common foreign ministry, normally Pacifican (AGENTS.md, current canon). A joint renunciation is therefore partly a Thalassia decision taken over Japan's army's head. Japan's officers will say so, and Part C should let them.
- *Wrong:* the mixed courts with foreign assessors at Shanghai (B; FRUS 1930 ch. 9).
- *Canon tension:* the railway-zone jurisdiction (A2.6).
- *Better option:* consular jurisdiction ends on 1 January 1930. Concord nationals are tried in modern Chinese courts at designated cities, with foreign legal advisers or an appeal guarantee for a term. That follows history's 1930 Shanghai agreement and, if verified, the reserved cities of the 1931 Anglo-Chinese draft treaty, whose text Hansard confirms was completed (7 December 1931). There is no separate railway jurisdiction.
- *Japan's cost:* its residents in Shanghai, Tianjin, Hankou and Shandong lose consular protection. List it among A8's costs and pair it with an offset (the 1930 tariff schedule).
- "Without Mukden, Britain and the Union sign in 1932": canon still has a plot crisis in 1931 (SL 1649). Say whether that crisis delays the treaties. Delete Weihaiwei unless canon gives Britain the lease (A2.7).
- The alternative (Pacifica alone) is rightly rejected.

**P-21. Canton in 1931.** Credible, and the 1931 flood plus "Nanjing's finances" is well grounded: CE 165 gives January 1932's domestic-debt crisis as a third of the year's revenue. Add Tang Shaoyi (P-7). Also note that "the customs pay Nanjing" is China's own Inspectorate's decision after 1929, not Pacifica's. The question is whether the Inspectorate, still staffed by Fong's men, withholds Canton's surplus. P-12's 1923 formula is Canton's argument. Second-order with A8.4: if Pacifican railway loans are a first charge while Shanghai's bankers take a cut in 1932, Chinese bankers and Canton's opposition will both point at the foreign creditor.

**P-22. The cipher question.** The recommendation is consistent with D-6, which lists what is read and does not include China. Reading warlords would extend D-6, which is a canon change. Fix the 1928 cause (A2.5).

Give the rule a motive the actors would have (R4). D-6 is a rule; the General Staff needs a reason for keeping it in China. Credible reasons:
- Chinese traffic runs over lines in which Pacifica has a hand (the cable of CR-080 C1, the customs telegraph, the Mukden lines), so a discovered intercept would end Fong's and the railways' standing;
- Zhang Zuolin and Peking are coalition partners in 1918–20.

D-6 already allows reading Britain in 1927 under a check against CR-046. Say whether that includes Britain's China traffic in 1925–27 (the Canton–Hong Kong strike, Hankou). If it does, Pacifica's picture of China is not "open sources only", and the C9 table's wording should change.

The alternative (reading Manchurian factions in 1918–22) is rightly rejected. It would read a partner.

**C8 items.**
- *Party station:* consistent with S-12. Also say that a hollow rule is acceptable as preparation for later parties. "Equipped elsewhere" is credible; history's 1932 transmitter was German. Name no maker unless one is confirmed.
- *Women and the census:* consistent with S-11 and S-18. It depends on CR-080 C3. The "school law of 1932" needs a source; history has the primary and middle school laws of December 1932.
- *Britain:*
  - Hong Kong, the Yangtze trade and the salt are right.
  - Add Hart as British minister (canon PROPOSED, SL 786). It gives Britain a face in Peking from 1885 and fits A8 tradeoff 6.
  - Weihaiwei (A2.7).
  - The labels (A2.9).
- *Korea's rice:* credible. "A Sino-Korean matter for the guarantors of 1894" is unclear, because China is itself a guarantor of 1894 (SL 1063). Write "for Japan and Pacifica as co-guarantors". The Yalu recommendation is sound.
- *Mongolia and Tibet:* fine. Add Tuva (SL 1451).

## E. Gaps

Items left by Parts A and B (A7 line 145; B10 line 303), and others found in review. Each needs at least a one-line decision.

1. **Warlords' revenue and the railways as their prize** (A7). Not decided. One point is worth stating: because A8.4's loans are secured on the customs, not on line receipts, a warlord's seizure of a line's revenue does not default a Pacifican loan. Decide whether Pacifican-financed rolling stock carries any rule on military use, or whether, as is more credible, its commandeering is simply a cost Pacifica records and bills.
2. **Opium revenue** (A7). S-9 decided enforcement; Part C does not cover the politics. Warlord poppy revenue (B8's Liu Xiang figure), Nanjing's opium suppression bureaus as revenue after 1928, and the Formosan stores against Nanjing's policy.
3. **The 1913 salt revenue.** P-6 covers it. Add what happens to the salt inspectorate after 1928 (history kept it under Nanjing).
4. **Formosa's politics 1895–1932** (A8.12): the Chinese governor beside the Pacifican Director-General; the gentry; the province in the 1911 revolution; the Japanese deputy from 1928 as seen by Formosans. History's Formosan home-rule petitions (1921–34, under Japan) suggest the form a Formosan movement takes in canon: petitions for an elected provincial assembly with power over the administration's surplus.
5. **The Chinese brigade's later fate** (canon 1918). Does it become Zhang Zuolin's railway guard after 1921, a Chinese railway police on the Manchurian line, or the core of a Northeastern unit? History's 1929 Sino-Soviet railway war does not happen in canon. Say so in C7.
6. **Zhang Zuolin 1916–28.** Canon has Pacifica work with him for Siberian security (SL 1290), take his cooperation in 1918–20 (SL 1367), and have him charter the Huludao district (SL 1621) and patronise Harbin radio (B7). Part C does not cover:
   - his Peking regime of 1926–28 (Grand Marshal from June 1927), which is the Expedition's last enemy while Pacifica funds the Nationalists;
   - who persuades him to withdraw beyond the Wall in 1928.

   This is canon's "contradiction" in its sharpest form and deserves a decision.
7. **The 1924 Beijing coup and the second Zhili–Fengtian war** (September–November 1924). Feng's coup expelled Puyi from the Forbidden City (5 November 1924), and he moved to Tianjin in February 1925. Canon's "Puyi remains in Tianjin" (SL 1655) depends on it. The coup also brought Sun north to die in Peking. Decide it in C5 or C6, not only in P-22's aside.
8. **The Nishihara loans and Peking's 1918 bargain.** A8.4 cites them as a warning; C5 lists them; P-10 assumes them. In history part of them funded Duan's War Participation Army, raised in name for the war. In canon there *is* a real Chinese part in the Russian war. One credible reading: Japan lends to Duan for the Chinese brigade and the Khingan agreement, which is Japan's money with Pacifica's agreement. That would be canon's criticism of Julian's bargains in a concrete form. History's May 1918 student protest against the Sino-Japanese military agreements is the canon analogue of the coalition pact.
9. **China's entry into the war (14 August 1917) and the Labour Corps.** China needs to be a belligerent to sit at The Hague (P-9). It would also recover the German and Austrian concessions at Tianjin and Hankou in 1917.
10. **Japan in Shandong, 1919–28, and Jinan.** Connect A8.13's traffic manager and mining company, Japanese residents, and a Tanaka-style expedition in 1927–28 to canon's Jinan (SL 1483). Then say what Julian's intervention is.
11. **Arms for the Nationalists without the Soviets**, and the powers' 1919 arms embargo. Who arms Whampoa, the Expedition, Feng and the warlords: the Hanyang and Canton arsenals, German, Czech, Italian and Japanese sellers? This is the biggest credibility question about a Soviet-less Northern Expedition.
12. **Feng Yuxiang without Soviet aid.** His Guominjun took Soviet arms in 1925–26. In canon it is weaker, or armed by someone else, and that changes the Expedition's northern front in 1926–28.
13. **The Nanjing incident of 24 March 1927** (attacks on consulates; British and American ships shelled the city). Does it happen, and is Pacifica among the powers that fire?
14. **The internal-loan pledges of 1921 and Nanjing's internal debt.** See B and P-5.
15. **Britain's 1898 holdings in canon** (Weihaiwei; the New Territories lease, which is still plausible).
16. **Hart** as British minister from 1885 (canon PROPOSED). It is unused, and it would give Britain a historical face in Part C without adding a person to the cast.
17. **Fong's succession.** Fong is born 1859, so he is 70 in 1929. Who leads the Inspectorate in the 1920s, and is the 1928–29 recovery handled by him or a successor? A Chinese successor named in advance (P-10's better option) fits A8's "recovery and alignment".
18. **The Company in the preventive service**, in 1911–12, 1923 and 1925 (canon, SL 991).
19. **The Nationalist left beyond Wang:** Deng Yanda (P-16, P-18), and Liao Zhongkai, whose assassination in August 1925 is history's turning point inside the party.
20. **Japan's China policy 1927–29:** Tanaka's "positive policy" and the Eastern Conference of 1927, before canon's Shidehara restraint of 1931.
21. **Japan's tariff offset:** history's 1930 agreement (B).
22. **The Russo-Japanese war of 1904–05 on Chinese soil** (SL 1171: "the decisive land battle centers on Harbin and the Sungari"). It is fought on neutral China's ground, as in history. Part C's C3 should give its effect on the Qing and the Three Eastern Provinces, and when Japan's troops leave Manchuria.
23. **A 1932 political target** to match A8's material one. Proposal for a table:
   - which provinces Nanjing governs directly, and which by allegiance (Manchuria under Zhang Xueliang) or semi-autonomy (Guangdong, Guangxi, Shanxi, Sichuan, Yunnan);
   - central and provincial army strengths;
   - revenue and debt service;
   - Japan's gains and losses in China listed together;
   - Pacifica's holdings.
