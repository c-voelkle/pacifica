## Reference works

1. **The Cambridge History of China, vols. 10–13** (Late Ch'ing and Republican China), above all Albert Feuerwerker's two economic chapters, also reprinted together as *The Chinese Economy, 1870–1949* (1995). This is the standard frame for every layer.
2. **Thomas G. Rawski, *Economic Growth in Prewar China* (1989).** The quantitative case that China's modern sector grew fast before 1937. Its series are the baselines for Part A.
3. **Albert Feuerwerker, *China's Early Industrialization: Sheng Hsuan-huai (1844–1916) and Mandarin Enterprise* (1958).** The China Merchants' company, the telegraph, the cotton mills and Hanyeping, all through one man. These are the enterprises Pacifican credit would touch first.
4. **Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Making of a Hinterland* (1993).** Inland North China, 1853–1937: what happens to the regions the railways and the treaty ports pass by. It is the corrective to a coastal story.

**Useful for single parts:**
- Tim Wright, *Coal Mining in China's Economy and Society, 1895–1937*;
- Ying Jia Tan, *Recharging China in War and Revolution, 1882–1955* (2021, open access), on electricity;
- Thomas Mullaney, *The Chinese Typewriter* (2017), on characters and machines.

### A8. The 1932 target and the decisions measured against it (approved 20 November 2026)

**The author's aim (20 November 2026):** a strong, successful China aligned with Pacifica, at minimal cost to Japan.

**The target in 1932 (MODEL where numeric)**
- China is solvent and governed. It has recovered its customs and tariff (canon, 1928–29), owns most of its railways, mines and utilities, and services its debt.
- China is still poor beside Japan. In the mid-1930s Japan's output per person was about 31% of the United States level and China's about 10%. With China 5–10% above Rawski's path and Japan faster in canon, China stays between a quarter and a third of Japan.
- Pacifica lends, trains and sets the standards. China owns. Japan sells manufactures and buys coal, ore and food under long contracts.
- Alignment rests on interest: credit, the Chinese Pacificans, the scholars, the standards and the Concord's guarantee of China's frontiers (Treaty of Nanjing). Pacifican ownership is kept to what canon already holds: the Formosan administration (as credit security, a Concord affair with a Japanese deputy from 1928, PROPOSED), the Huludao trust until its bonds are paid, and the Tsingtao anchorage. China resents these, and canon says so.

**Japan's position in China in canon:**
- Manchuria, Dalian and Port Arthur stay Chinese (CR-042). Japan has running rights on the Manchurian line (§25) and, from 1928, its joint management (Treaty of Nanjing, CR-039).
- Shandong returns to China and Pacifica keeps the Tsingtao anchorage (§30).
- Japan receives a share of the Formosan receipts (§25) and, from 1928, the deputy (PROPOSED).
- Victoria blocks the worst of the Twenty-One Demands (CR-040, PROPOSED).
- Japan's other gains (Urajio, Enkaishū and Karafuto in 1899, the Kamchatka protectorate, the Transbaikal sphere, Korea's economic predominance) were taken from Russia, not given for China.

**Where this proposal still costs Japan:** its cotton goods and mills in China after the tariff of 1928, Hanyeping ore at a reviewed price instead of a captive one, and the San Francisco route. The recommendations below turn the rest of Japan's interests into contracts.

**Tradeoffs that cannot be avoided**
1. **Cotton.** China's tariff from 1928 hits Japan's cotton goods and its mills in China. This is Japan's largest cost. How far it is softened depends on what the partners' CR gives Japan (CR-080 X-3, undecided).
2. **Formosa.** Pacifican control remains China's grievance to 1932. Japan's deputy and its share of the receipts make it a shared asset, and Formosan sugar sold to Japan competes with Hawaiian and Philippine sugar.
3. **Recovery and alignment.** China will press on Formosa, Huludao and Tsingtao. Pacifica keeps China aligned by credit, contracts and the guarantee, not by those holdings.
4. **Speed and debt.** Faster building on loans secured on the customs loads the customs and invites a backlash against the lender, as the railway loans did in 1911. The revised railway path is slower after 1911 for this reason.
5. **Silver.** Loans in silver put the risk on Pacifica; loans in gold put it on China.
6. **Britain.** Kaiping kept Chinese, Fong in place of Hart and a large Pacifican creditor share all come at Britain's expense. Britain's answer belongs to Part C.
7. **Japan's army.** Its material interest is the Manchurian corridor and its transit, which canon secures. Canon already counts Huludao's customs among the officers' grievances and has the Manchurian plot stopped in 1931. A stronger China does not prevent the plot, and this CR leaves it as canon has it.
8. **Hawaii.** A Chinese Pacific line and a growing Chinese-Pacifican population touch the Hawaiian crown's concern for its own people. Hawaii keeps control of its entry rules, and the story does not become about one diaspora (CR-037).
9. **Coast and interior.** The waterworks earn no cash. Their money comes from the customs surplus and the indemnity share that could otherwise build railways.

**Tradeoffs that cannot be avoided**
1. **Rent and the Nationalists' base.** Enforcing the limit where the register exists takes income from landlords, who are much of the Nationalists' rural support. Not enforcing it leaves Mao his issue (Part C).
2. **The labour article and Japan's mills.** Japan's mills in China are not bound by it, and labour unrest starts there, as in 1925.
3. **Japan's receivers and China's tariff.** If CR-080 X-3 gives Japan consumer electronics, China's radio, record and telephone boom is Japan's largest new market and the cheapest offset to its cotton loss (A8, tradeoff 1). 4. **A party station and Pacifica's own rule.** Pacifica gives no wavelength to a party (CR-079). Nanjing's central station was a party organ; equipping it breaks the rule, and refusing offends Nanjing. S-12's state stations are the middle course and may not satisfy the party.

7. **Literacy, reach and nationalism.** More readers and listeners strengthen the demand to recover Formosa, Huludao and Tsingtao; reach grows faster than literacy.
8. **Radio in Manchuria.** Chinese broadcasting at Mukden and Huludao joins Huludao's customs among the Japanese officers' grievances (canon); the 1931 plot is still stopped.
9. **Relief and the warlords.** Rail and truck relief works only while the lines' owners let grain pass, as in 1920–21 and not in 1928–30.
10. **Women in offices.** Women in the card rooms, exchanges and stations come first from Chinese-Pacifican and mission-school families; their visibility is contested at home in the way it was in history's treaty ports, and it ties women's careers in China to Pacifican institutions.
11. **The Chinese Pacificans.** At about 13% of residents they are larger than any Chinese community abroad in history; the story must not become about one diaspora (CR-037), and Hawaii's concern for its own people stands.

### C1. 1860–1894: Self-Strengthening with a partner (D0.1, D0.2)

### C2. 1895–1901: a milder defeat, the reformers and the Boxers

- *Hist.:* the Hundred Days of reform (June–September 1898) ended in Cixi's coup. Kang Youwei founded the Baohuanghui (Chinese Empire Reform Association) at Victoria, British Columbia, on 20 July 1899, with Liang Qichao as vice-president; its chapters spread through the Americas, and at one time most of Honolulu's Chinese supported it. Sun had founded the Xingzhonghui at Honolulu on 24 November 1894, and in 1905 the Tongmenghui was founded in Tokyo among China's students in Japan. In 1900 the southern viceroys kept their provinces out of the court's war (the Southeast Mutual Protection).
- *Canon:* the milder 1895 keeps Li Hongzhang in office longer and the Qing solvent; Germany still takes Kiaochow, and Pacifica takes nothing (§25). Russia enters Manchuria during the Boxer rising (§26, PROPOSED).

**P-3. The reformers in exile.** *Proposal:* the Hundred Days fail as in history, because the court is unchanged. Pacifica gives Kang, Liang and Sun's men asylum and the right to organise among the Chinese Pacificans, but no arms, no credit and no ships for risings. Victoria asks Cixi to leave the exiles unharmed and promises nothing about their plans. The Baohuanghui and Sun's society both grow in San Francisco and Honolulu and compete for the same remittance money (S-3); the revolutionaries' league of 1905 is still founded in Tokyo, where the students are (B5). The Prosperity Party's Chinese members (the Hui) may belong to either Chinese party, which runs no ticket in Pacifica.

**The Boxers.** Canon's contingent is limited and withdraws early, and Pacifica's indemnity share goes to scholarships and, after 1901, to the conservancy (A8.10). No decision is needed here.

### C2a. Victoria and Cixi, and the end of the Qing (revision of P-4, 21 November 2026)

The author approved P-1 to P-3 on 21 November 2026 and asked for P-4 to be rethought around a peer friendship between Cixi and Victoria after the Boxers. Three ways for the dynasty to end were drafted, and the same day the author chose Option 1: Guangxu dies in November 1908 as in history, and the dynasty ends in 1911–12 as canon has it. Options 2 and 3 were rejected because their devices, Guangxu's survival and Yuan's usurpation of 1915, are not believable. Der Ling and Sarah Pike Conger are left out of the cast. Kaʻiulani speaks Chinese and is the Empress Dowager's confidante. What Julian does from November 1908 is open, and P-4e proposes it.

**The historical anchor.** *Hist.:* after the court returned to Peking in January 1902, Cixi began a series of palace receptions for the wives of the legations, on the assumption that women would be readier to answer an appeal of friendship. The American minister's wife, Sarah Conger, became her best-known guest; Der Ling, a lady-in-waiting, interpreted from 1903 to 1905; and the court photographer Xunling photographed Cixi holding Conger's hand. Neither woman is in canon's cast, and their place is Kaʻiulani's. Cixi's charm campaign of 1902–1908 was real; what it lacked was a counterpart of her own rank. In canon there is one. Victoria was born in 1838 and Cixi in 1835. Both are widowed regents who governed for adopted sons, Victoria for Julian and Cixi for Guangxu. They negotiated together in 1898–99 (CR-042, APPROVED), and CR-042 left the scenes of their friendship for a later edit.

**P-4a. The friendship (proposal).**
- *Its seed* is the Boxer year. Pacifica's limited contingent guards the legations, takes no loot and withdraws first (canon), and Victoria's indemnity share goes to scholarships and the conservancy. When the court comes home in January 1902, Victoria writes to Cixi as one regent to another.
- *Kaʻiulani.* Princess Kaʻiulani (born 1875, CR-043) is Victoria's envoy at the court from 1902 and becomes Cixi's confidante. Schooled in England from 1889, she speaks English, Hawaiian, French and German. She begins Chinese with private tutors in 1899, the year of the rheumatic fever she spends in bed, and by 1902 speaks the court's northern Mandarin well enough to talk with Cixi alone. Her tutors are unnamed. The air school's Chinese hours begin in 1915–16 (CR-078, CR-079), after the friendship, so they are where she keeps her Chinese up, not where she learns it. The court is her apprenticeship in the diplomacy she uses at The Hague in 1919. Her damaged heart valve limits her journeys, which she makes by sea and rail.
- *How she gets in, and why she is trusted.* She does not come as one of the legation wives. In the spring of 1902 she brings Victoria's letter as a princess of the Hawaiian royal house, and Cixi receives her as a royal guest. Two things make her the confidante. She needs no interpreter, so what Cixi says to her is not heard by the court's interpreters or reported through the Board of Foreign Affairs. And she carries Victoria's word on the one question Cixi cannot settle herself: in 1905 she brings the assurance that Japan's army will leave Manchuria under the settlement then being negotiated, and the settlement signed at Urajio keeps it (CR-039). After that, Cixi uses her as a private channel to Victoria for matters she does not want in the Board's files.
- *The court's gatekeepers.* Her audiences are arranged through Guangxu's empress, who is her sponsor among the court ladies, and Li Lianying's eunuchs keep the doors for her as for any guest of the Empress Dowager. The Grand Council under Prince Qing sees a channel to Victoria outside the Board of Foreign Affairs and tolerates it, because it brings the Revenue Council's credit and asks for no appointments. The Manchu princes who want the army and the revenue in Manchu hands, Tieliang among them, distrust her, and the photograph of 1903 is their exhibit.
- *The photograph.* Xunling photographs Cixi holding Kaʻiulani's hand at the Summer Palace (1903, MODEL date), and the court sends prints to the legations. The *Examiner* prints it and the London and New York papers copy it. In Pacifica it is the picture of the friendship; to the court's conservatives it shows a foreign princess at the Empress Dowager's side.
- *The meeting.* Victoria visits the Summer Palace in the spring of 1906, after the war of 1904–05 and before her own handover to Julian, while the constitutional missions are abroad. She meets Guangxu's empress, Cixi's niece, later the Empress Dowager Longyu.
- *What each wants.* Cixi wants a power that does not plot against her and a model for an old woman handing over a throne. Victoria wants a China that survives as a partner, and a friend who knows what a regency costs.
- *The limits.* The friendship lends money and advice, never troops or warships (P-2), and it never asks Cixi to change her court. Japan trains the New Army's officers, as in history, so Japan holds its own stake in the dynasty's army.

**P-4b. The New Policies with a friend (proposal).** Each change takes a historical measure and gives it what it lacked: money, time or a model. The five changes do not compete for the same money. The banner conversion pays for itself, because the stipends it ends cost the provinces more each year than the loan's service (P-5's table). The converted examination uses the old halls, fees and degrees. The constitution costs elections and an assembly. Their common limit is administrative: the same viceroys and provincial treasurers carry all of them, and the banner loan sets the conversion's pace by what the provinces can survey and settle.
1. **The examinations are converted, not abolished.** *Hist.:* the throne had agreed to cut the degree quotas over the next three examinations, and on 2 September 1905, on Yuan Shikai's and Zhang Zhidong's memorial, it abolished the examinations at once. Economists and historians link the abolition to the gentry's turn toward revolution after 1905 (Bai and Jia). A title incentive programme gave old degrees to graduates of the new schools until 1911 (Chu). *Proposal:* Cixi keeps the gradual plan of 1903–04, and from 1906 the old degrees are awarded by new-subject state examinations under a board of examinations. The board sits under the Ministry of Education created in 1905. Its examiners of record are Chinese degree-holders and new-school graduates appointed by the throne. The Polytechnic's examiners advise only on method, on Pacifica's model of examination for office (CR-037, CR-038): papers marked blind by two readers and published pass lists. The papers set the classics beside the new subjects, the halls are the old ones, and the degrees keep their names, so the gentry see their own examination reformed, not a foreign one. The gentry's sons keep their road into office, and the new schools are its entrance.
2. **The banner conversion is paid for.** *Hist.:* Cixi's edicts of September and October 1907, the first on Yuan's memorial, ordered the provincial banner garrisons gradually disbanded, their pensions capitalised for ten years and land granted in place of stipends, and declared Manchu and Han equal before the law. The money was never found. A proposal reported by Rockhill in September 1907 would have done away with the twenty-four banner corps two a year. *Proposal:* a Pacifican loan of 1908 funds the capitalisation, on the pattern of the Realm's Land Fund. Its service is paid to the Inspectorate from the stipend funds the provinces no longer pay the garrisons, as the loan of 1898 had seven salt and likin collectorates placed under the Inspector-General (CE 83), and the customs balance is its second security. The loan lets the garrisons convert in four or five years instead of twelve. By 1912 most provincial bannermen hold land or bonds instead of stipends, and the "Manchu question" that drove the revolutionaries loses much of its force.
3. **The constitution has a date the court keeps.** *Hist.:* the missions of 1905–06 (Dai Hongci and Duanfang to the Union first, Zaize to Japan and Europe) reported that constitutional government was the only way to make the state strong; the edict of 1 September 1906 promised a constitution, and the Outline of August 1908 set a nine-year timetable. On 4 November 1910, under the assemblies' petitions, the regency brought the parliament forward to 1913. *Proposal:* Dai and Duanfang's mission stops at Thalassia after Washington. The Outline of 1908 keeps Japan's model for the throne and sets a parliament for 1912. The regency keeps the date after 1908, so the edict of November 1910 is not needed, and the National Assembly of 1910 presses instead for a responsible cabinet. Three things hold the date. The regent's authority rests on Cixi's last edicts, and her valedictory edict confirms the Outline and its year (P-4c), so repudiating the date would repudiate the edicts that made him regent. The provincial assemblies of 1909 are full of degree-holders whom the converted examination kept inside the state, and they expect 1912. And from 1910 Pacifica's new credit waits on a voted budget (P-4e). Keeping the date does not stop the princes' cabinet; it brings it on. Because a parliament meets in 1912, the princes want the executive in the imperial clan's hands before it does, and the cabinet of May 1911 is their answer.
4. **The emperor in council.** *Proposal:* from 1906 Cixi brings Guangxu into the Grand Council's business, reading memorials and speaking at audiences, and means him to reign under the Outline when the constitution comes. When Victoria lays down the regency under the Act of Settlement in November 1907 (canon), Cixi has seen a regency end without a coup. She has named no one when Guangxu dies.
5. **Money and the customs.** The Revenue Council of 1906 with Tang Shaoyi (P-7) is the friendship's working office, Fong reporting to it (canon). The first national budget comes earlier than history's of 1910–11.

**P-4c. November 1908 (proposal).** *Hist.:* Cixi's seventy-third birthday was kept on 3 November 1908, and she fell ill soon after. Guangxu died on 14 November 1908, aged 37. By edicts of 13 to 15 November Cixi named Puyi, the son of Prince Chun (Zaifeng, aged 25), heir, and Prince Chun regent; she died on 15 November, and Puyi was enthroned on 2 December. A forensic study of 2008 found arsenic in Guangxu's remains far above normal; historians suspect Cixi, Yuan Shikai or the eunuch Li Lianying, and none is proved. *Proposal:* as history, for history's reasons. Zaifeng is Guangxu's brother and the husband Cixi chose for Ronglu's daughter, Puyi has been the likely heir since his birth in 1906, and the court's factions are those of history. Guangxu's years in council make the choice harder to contest, not different. One clause changes: her valedictory edict confirms the Outline of 1908 and its year for the parliament, the only concession she makes to the constitutionalists on her deathbed. Kaʻiulani has come to Peking for the birthday and is at the Lake Palace when both die. She has no part in the succession and witnesses no edict. When Victoria's cable arrives she carries its condolence to Guangxu's widow, now the Empress Dowager Longyu, in person. Canon keeps the poisoning unproved: no character establishes who gave the arsenic, and the narrator does not say. Because Guangxu was coming back into the government, the men who betrayed him in 1898 had more to fear from him than in history, and the suspicion of 1908 falls on them harder. Victoria never says whom she suspects. The cable reaches Thalassia in the month of the challenge evening on fission (CR-050).

**P-4d. How the Qing ends (Option 1, chosen 21 November 2026).** Prince Chun's regency keeps the assemblies, the budget and the constitution's date, and makes the three decisions that history's regency made: Yuan's dismissal (January 1909), the princes' cabinet (May 1911) and the nationalisation of the trunk railways (May 1911). Wuchang follows on 10 October 1911, and the dynasty ends with the abdication of 12 February 1912, as canon has it; P-4 in C3 gives the mechanics. China's Republic inherits a converted examination, a paid banner conversion and a working budget, so the warlord years start from stronger institutions. *Rejected on 21 November 2026:* Option 2 (Guangxu outlives Cixi and reigns, and the dynasty falls to Yuan's usurpation in 1915–16) and Option 3 (the Qing survives to 1932).

**P-4e. Julian after November 1908 (proposal; left open by the author on 21 November 2026).**

Canon's Julian is 22 in November 1908. In the spring of 1908 he sailed for Europe believing that war could be made too expensive to start (§27, PROPOSED); in July 1914 he refuses to break a contract for politics and breaks it only when it can no longer change anything (§28, APPROVED). In 1911 he supports Sun's movement (§27); in 1916 he leaves Yuan to his fate, and later he funds Sun while working with Zhang Zuolin, a contradiction that becomes "a criticism of Julian's strategic bargains" (§29). The proposal builds his China policy of 1908–12 from those traits. He inherits his mother's contracts with the court, not her friendship.

*Three stances.*
- **A. The heir to the friendship.** Julian keeps Victoria's policy: Pacifica lends to the regency, protests Yuan's dismissal and backs the throne until Wuchang, and turns to Sun only after the abdication. It is what Victoria wants. It is not canon, in which Julian supports Sun in 1911, and it makes the first years of his reign his mother's.
- **B. The constitution and the contracts, not the dynasty (recommended).** Julian keeps every contract the court has signed and makes new credit depend on the constitution: the published budget, and from 1910 the National Assembly's vote on it. He gives Sun's movement what P-3 allows, openly. When the revolution comes he lends to neither side, and he makes recognition depend on the Qing's debts and treaties being assumed and on the court's safety.
- **C. The early break.** After Yuan's dismissal Julian decides the regency is lost and backs Sun with money from 1909. It breaks P-3 (no credit for risings) and Julian's own rule on contracts, makes Pacifica a party to the Canton rising of April 1911, and hands a court with reason to repudiate them the Huludao commission and the banner loan.

*The rule's domain under Stance B.*
- **General loans to the central government** (budget support, currency, reorganisation): from the opening of the National Assembly on 3 October 1910, only on a budget the national legislature has voted. From 1912 the rule reads the Republic's parliament for the Assembly.
- **Contracts signed before then** run on: the banner loan of 1908, the railway contracts, the Revenue Council's office and the Huludao commission.
- **Works credit** (railways under A8.4, the conservancy, later the provincial works contracts of P-11 and P-12) has its own rule: specified works, a borrower that publishes its accounts, and service on the customs. It is not general credit and needs no vote.
- **The customs surplus** is not Pacifican credit. It is China's revenue, and Fong releases it to the recognised government under the trusteeship (P-5), whatever that government's relation to its parliament.
- **When Yuan dissolves the parliament in January 1914,** the rule closes general credit until a parliament sits again; it already barred the Reorganisation Loan of 1913, signed without the parliament's consent (P-6). Works credit goes on, and the surplus goes on reaching Peking. Julian could stop the surplus only by breaking the trusteeship, and he does not. That is what canon's "leaves him to his fate" (§29) means in money.

*Stance B, year by year.*
1. **Recognition and mourning (November–December 1908).** Julian recognises the Xuantong Emperor and the regency in the week of the deaths. Pacifica's contracts (the Revenue Council's office, the banner loan, the Huludao option) are with the state, and he says so. Victoria writes to Longyu. Julian tells his mother that China is now the Crown's business and no longer hers. It is the month the fission paper is challenged, so the drift that canon dates from November 1908 (CR-050) has a second strand from its first weeks.
2. **Tang Shaoyi's mission (November 1908 to January 1909).** *Hist.:* an edict of July 1908 named Tang special envoy to thank the Union for the indemnity remission. He left China in late September and reached Washington in December. Yuan and Xu Shichang had sent him to propose using the remitted funds in Manchuria and to study the currency question; Roosevelt's administration, which had exchanged the Root–Takahira notes with Japan on 30 November and preferred the remission's education scheme, rebuffed him, and he was recalled after Yuan's dismissal. *Proposal:* Tang crosses the Pacific by Thalassia (P-7) and asks Julian for a currency-reform loan and Manchurian credit. Julian takes up the Huludao option and offers the currency loan against the Revenue Council's published budget. Tang's recall ends the offer: Pacifica's credit offers are made to a named office-holder under the throne's commission and lapse when he leaves office, and Julian does not renew one to his successor unasked, so no offer passes to whoever holds the office next. In 1910–11 the regency's Board of Finance takes the currency loan to the four-power banks instead, as in history (signed 15 April 1911), because the princes prefer several lenders to the one that was Cixi's partner.
3. **Yuan's dismissal (2 January 1909).** *Hist.:* the regent sent Yuan home to cure a "foot ailment"; the British and American ministers considered uniting the legations in a protest and gave it up. *Proposal:* Julian makes no representation and says why in public: Pacifica does not choose China's ministers. Victoria does not ask him to. Xu Shichang leaves Manchuria on 8 February 1909 to become Minister of Posts and Communications, and the Huludao commission is issued from his ministry later in 1909 (the first reading in the watch item; CR-042's charter stands).
4. **Cixi's funeral (November 1909).** *Hist.:* the coffin left Peking for the Eastern Tombs in November 1909, a year after her death. Duanfang, then viceroy of Zhili, was censured and dismissed for letting photographs be taken of the cortège and for using trees in the mausoleum's enclosure as telegraph poles. *Proposal:* Julian sends Victoria, aged 71, as his envoy, with Kaʻiulani; it is Victoria's last journey to China. The cameraman Duanfang lets onto the route is the press estate's (CR-079), and his film is part of the case against Duanfang. Victoria did not know of the permit. She has the film kept out of Pacifica's theatres, and it stays in the press estate's archive. The grievance is one of the reasons the regency's Board of Finance prefers the four-power banks to Pacifica in 1910–11 (items 2 and 6).
5. **The regent's brothers (1909–10).** *Hist.:* the regent's brothers led the military reform. Zaixun, Navy Minister from December 1909, studied the navies of Europe in 1909 and the Union in 1910, and the court gave complimentary ship orders to the powers he visited. Zaitao, head of the army's consultative council (the Junzichu), toured eight countries in 1910. *Proposal:* both princes pass through Thalassia, and Julian receives them. He sells no warships (P-2) and offers places in the Polytechnic's engineering courses. The orders go to the other powers on their route. The princes understand the condition on new credit and reject it: a voted budget would give the Assembly the purse before the imperial clan holds the cabinet.
6. **The Assembly and the budget (1910–11).** *Hist.:* the National Assembly opened on 3 October 1910 and pressed for a responsible cabinet and for control of the budget. *Proposal:* from 1910 Pacifica lends to the Peking government only on a budget the Assembly has voted, and existing contracts run on. The rule makes Pacifica the constitutionalists' creditor and the regency's critic without a word against the dynasty. The regency borrows from the four-power group instead, without the Assembly's vote: the currency loan of 15 April 1911 and the Huguang loan of 20 May 1911.
7. **The railway crisis (1911).** *Proposal:* Victoria asks Julian to offer cash on Pacifican credit for the Sichuan shareholders. Julian refuses. The Huguang contract is signed, the regency has not asked, and Pacifica's credit in China rests on signed contracts being kept, other lenders' as well as its own. It is the rule he keeps with Germany's oil in July 1914. Sichuan rises as in history.
8. **Wuchang to the abdication (October 1911 to February 1912).**
   - *Hist.:* on 13 October the Hubei military government told the consuls at Hankow that treaties already concluded stood and that indemnities and loans would be paid, that treaties concluded later with the Qing would not be recognised, and that powers helping the Qing would be treated as enemies. Sun, who read of the rising at Denver on 12 October, appealed to the powers for neutrality, promised to observe the treaties, and went to London and Paris to keep the Qing from foreign money; the consortium's banks stopped lending to both sides. Yuan became premier on 1 November, and the regent resigned on 6 December.
   - *Proposal:* Sun tours the Chinese Pacificans (canon, PROPOSED) and comes to Thalassia, where Julian receives him openly. Julian states three terms in public. Pacifica lends to neither side while the fighting lasts, and the customs revenue is held for the loan service (P-5). It recognises the government the provinces accept once that government assumes the Qing's debts and treaties, the banner bonds among them. And the Emperor and the Empress Dowager are to be safe. Sun accepts all three the same day; none goes beyond what he had already promised. Sun had gone to London and Paris in history to keep foreign money from the Qing. In canon the consortium's banks have already stopped lending to both sides, and Julian's first term covers the one great creditor outside the consortium, so the journey's object is met. He sails from San Francisco by Honolulu instead of by Europe and reaches Shanghai at the end of December (MODEL), in time for his election on 29 December and his inauguration on 1 January 1912, as in history.
   - *Victoria's term.* The third term is Victoria's. She writes to Longyu, and Kaʻiulani carries the letter to Peking in January 1912 and stays with Longyu in the weeks before the abdication. The Articles of Favourable Treatment are history's, negotiated between Yuan and Nanjing; Pacifica's part is a recognition that waits on them. Before it recognises, Pacifica's minister checks the two terms against the documents: Sun's manifesto of 5 January 1912, under which the Republic recognises the treaties and loans made before the revolution, and the abdication edict with the Articles of Favourable Treatment, which the Nanjing Senate passed on 6 February. Pacifica recognises the Republic on 12 February 1912 (P-5).
   - *Why Sun and not Yuan.* Sun is a Honolulu man who has accepted Pacifica's terms in public; Yuan has the army and owes Pacifica nothing. Julian's support is asylum, his reception and the Chinese Pacificans' freedom to subscribe, not the Treasury's money (P-3); the declared grant comes in 1917 (P-14).
9. **Victoria and Julian.** Victoria holds that Julian let her friend's house fall for a man without an army. Julian holds that he kept every contract the house signed and made the Republic keep them. The banner bonds are paid through Fong's customs until they are redeemed (date MODEL). Victoria writes to Longyu until Longyu's death on 22 February 1913. China stays a strand of the drift beside the seal until Victoria's renunciation of 1921 after the Altai cover-up (§32).

*Tradeoffs of Stance B*
1. Peking sees Julian as the man who withdrew a dead friend's credit, and Canton as a sovereign who lends nothing to the revolution; the Chinese Pacificans' subscriptions do what his Treasury will not.
2. The Assembly rule makes Pacifica a party to China's constitutional quarrel from 1910, and the regency's turn to the consortium is partly its answer.
3. Victoria loses China as her own field in November 1908. Her diplomacy after it is Julian's errands (the funeral of 1909, Tokyo in 1915), and she resents it.
4. The press estate's film of the funeral gives the court's conservatives a grievance against Pacifica in the year after Cixi's death, and it feeds the regency's turn to the four-power banks (items 2 and 6).

*What P-4e changes elsewhere in Part C*

- **P-5:** unchanged; the trusteeship carries out Julian's first term.
- **P-7:** Tang's mission of 1908 stops at Thalassia; no new office.
- **Watch item on Xu Shichang:** the first reading (Xu acting from Peking as Minister of Posts and Communications).
- **CR-043 (PROPOSED: from 1908 Kaʻiulani travels with Julian's missions to the European capitals):** kept; she divides 1908–12 between the missions and the court, which she visits in November 1908, November 1909 and January–February 1912.

### C3. 1901–1911: the New Policies with more money, and the fall (D0.3)

- *Hist.:*
  - the examinations were abolished in 1905;
  - in May 1906 an edict put the whole customs service under a Revenue Council, with Tieliang as superintendent and Tang Shaoyi as associate minister;
  - Xu Shichang was the first viceroy of the Three Eastern Provinces from June 1907 to 8 February 1909, and then Minister of Posts and Communications until August 1910;
  - in 1908 the court set a nine-year programme for a constitution;
  - the provincial assemblies were elected in 1909 by about 1.7 million registered voters, 0.42% of the population, and the National Assembly met in 1910;
  - the cabinet of 8 May 1911 had thirteen members, nine Manchu or Mongol (seven of the imperial clan) and four Han (sources differ on one Mongol), and the provincial assemblies' federation protested at once;
  - on 9 May 1911 Sheng Xuanhuai nationalised the trunk railways, and on 20 May he signed the Huguang loan with the four-power consortium of British, French, German and American banks;
  - the Sichuan Railway Protection League formed on 17 June, and troops sent from Hubei to suppress it gave the Wuchang garrison its chance on 10 October.
- *Canon and Parts A–B:* more revenue (a moderate indemnity, a published customs), more railways (11,000–12,000 km by 1911, A8.4), a Chinese Manchuria, the Polytechnic of Formosa (1906) and the mission's men in office. The war of 1904–05 is fought on neutral China's ground around Harbin and the Sungari (§26), and Japan's army leaves Manchuria under the settlement signed at Urajio in 1905 (CR-039).

**P-4. Why the Qing still falls.** *(The mechanics of the fall under C2a's Option 1, chosen 21 November 2026.)* Canon fixes the fall. *Proposal:* the New Policies go further in money, railways and trained men, and the court still fails politically, as in history: the princes' cabinet of May 1911 alienates the constitutionalists, and the nationalisation sets Sichuan alight. The Hankow–Canton trunk, built on Pacifican credit from 1896 (canon, PROPOSED), is open by 1911 except the mountain section, which Zhan Tianyou finishes in 1916 (MODEL), so the crisis of 1911 is about the Sichuan–Hankow line and its Sichuan shareholders. Pacifica is not in the Huguang consortium, because its rule is to lend to Chinese-owned lines on the customs with no foreign management (A8.4), so the crisis is not Pacifica's. The mission's men are divided, as they were in history: Tang Shaoyi serves Yuan in the negotiations of 1911–12, and Liang Dunyan, foreign minister in the princes' cabinet, stays a monarchist.

**P-5. The customs in the revolution.** *Hist.:* in January 1912 the diplomatic body made the Inspector-General the trustee of all customs revenue, deposited in foreign banks at Shanghai for the loan service, so that he released the surplus only to the government the powers recognised; under the arrangement of 30 January 1912 between the Board of Foreign Affairs and the diplomatic body, a commission of the managers of the banks interested in the loans secured on the customs before 1900 and in the indemnity decided the priority of the claims, and three banks were the custodians. When the loans of 1896 and 1898 were raised, China undertook that the Customs administration would remain as then constituted during their currency. The Boxer protocol of 1901 assigned to the indemnity the balance of the customs after the obligations already secured on it, with the native customs and the unpledged salt revenue. *Proposal:* Fong does the same, because the customs secure the loans Pacifica's savers hold. Pacifica recognises the Republic on the abdication (12 February 1912), not a person, and so the trusteeship that serves the Republic in 1912 serves Yuan from March. This is Fong's divided duty again: Julian backs Sun, and Fong's revenue goes to Peking.

- The Boxer indemnity's total is history's. Pacifica's share (about 1.5–2 a year, MODEL) is paid like the others and returned to the scholarships and the conservancy.
- The railway loans' service on the customs is matched by the lines' net receipts, which go to the Ministry of Posts and Communications.
- Canon's gain is mostly the provinces': their Boxer remittances fall by about half, which is money for the New Policies. The free surplus itself stays small.
- *Yuan's need.* The Reorganisation Loan of £25 million, issued at 90 in May 1913, is about 164 million taels at 1913's rate (£6.7 million to 44 million taels), for the central government's liabilities, provincial loans, the revolution's losses, disbandment, provincial notes, administration and the salt administration. A surplus of 1.5–6 million taels a year is a small fraction of it and cannot replace it. Pacifica's refusal of the loan (P-6) therefore matters, and the surplus that reaches Yuan anyway is small and visible.

*1913.* About 1.5–6 million taels reach Yuan in 1913 (MODEL), released monthly under the trusteeship's rule, which serves the recognised government, not under Julian's rule on new credit (P-4e), which governs Pacifican lending and not China's revenue. The parliament still sits until January 1914. The releases of July to September 1913 fall in the months of the campaign against the Second Revolution, and because the returns are published every month, Sun's men can read them line by line. It is the strongest form of canon's criticism of Julian's bargains: the money is China's, the trustee is Pacifican, and it pays the man Julian's candidate is fighting.

**P-5a. Who governs the trusteeship (decision, comparable to A7's decision 5 on Shanghai's plant).** *Proposal:*
- *Fong's authority.* Fong is China's officer, appointed by China (canon). The arrangement of 30 January 1912 between the Board of Foreign Affairs and the diplomatic body directs him to account to the bankers' commission and deposit the revenue with the custodian banks, and the Republic confirms it.
- *The board.* The bankers' commission is history's: the managers of the banks interested in the loans secured on the customs before 1900 and in the indemnity. Because the Pacifican railway loan of 1896 is one of those loans, the San Francisco bank that leads the railway bonds' syndicate (its name OPEN) sits on it, and it is a fourth custodian beside the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, the Deutsch-Asiatische Bank and the Russo-Asiatic Bank. The commission decides priority among the claims by the contracts' dates. Releasing the surplus needs the diplomatic body's consent, as in history, and Pacifica's minister is one of its members.
- *Recall and dismissal.* Julian cannot recall Fong, who is in China's service. China can dismiss him in law, but the loans of 1896 and 1898 bind the Customs administration to remain as constituted while they run, and the Pacifican railway contracts carry the same clause, so the creditors would treat a dismissal as a breach and a successor would inherit the agreement. The commission cannot alter Fong's accounts; it can withhold the surplus only with the diplomatic body.
- *Options.* (a) The arrangement above (recommended). (b) Pacifican custody alone: rejected, because the older creditors would not accept it and it would make Fong Pacifica's agent. (c) Chinese custody under a creditors' audit: impossible in January 1912 with two Chinese governments; it is the arrangement of the recovery of 1928–29.
- *How it differs from history's trusteeship.*
  1. The Inspector-General is a Pacifican-born officer in China's service, and Britain holds no promise like history's of 1898 that he will be British; London's weight is that of the largest old creditor.
  2. Pacifica is a customs creditor through the railway bonds but not a member of the consortium, so it sits on the commission and in the diplomatic body with no share in the Reorganisation Loan.
  3. The free surplus is larger than history's but small; the larger gain is the provinces' relief from the indemnity.
  4. The conservancy charge ranks with the prior obligations, so the Yellow River works are paid through 1911–12.
  5. The returns are published monthly.
  The trusteeship is a creditors' institution in which Pacifica is one member, not the owner. Its working is public, and it pays the recognised government whether Julian approves of that government or not.

### C4. 1912–1916: Yuan, the loans and the Demands

- *Hist.:* Tang Shaoyi was the Republic's first premier from 13 March to 27 June 1912 and resigned over Yuan's disregard for the cabinet and the law. The Kuomintang won the elections of 1912–13; Song Jiaoren, who was to lead the cabinet, was shot at Shanghai station on 20 March 1913. On 18 March 1913 Wilson withdrew his government's support from the American bankers in the consortium. On 26 April 1913 Yuan signed the £25 million Reorganisation Loan with the British, French, German, Russian and Japanese banks without the parliament's consent, secured on the salt revenue under a foreign-inspected salt administration. The Second Revolution of July 1913 was crushed in about two months; Yuan banned the Kuomintang in November 1913 and dissolved the parliament in January 1914. In 1915 Japan dropped the fifth group of the Demands itself, delivered an ultimatum on 7 May, and China accepted it on 9 May, which became a day of national humiliation; the treaties were signed on 25 May.

**P-6. The Reorganisation Loan.** *Proposal:* Pacifica stays out and says why: it does not lend to arm a president against an elected parliament. The day after the loan is signed on 26 April 1913, Pacifica's minister hands the Board of Foreign Affairs a note that Pacifica takes no part in a general loan the parliament has not voted (P-4e); the note is published at Thalassia the same day and read to the Senate by the foreign minister. The declared grant to Sun's movement from 1917 (P-14) is not a loan to a government and pays for no arms, and its recipient is the movement of the members of the dissolved parliament at Canton; Peking's governments and Britain hold that it breaks the note's logic all the same (tradeoff 6). With the Union's bankers also out, Britain leads the five-power loan and its salt inspectorate. Japan, not yet in the Concord, takes its share. Britain gains a second foreign-run revenue in China beside Fong's customs, and Japan finances the man Pacifica's candidate is fighting. Pacifica's refusal follows the rule on new credit (P-4e): the loan is signed without the parliament's consent. It is weakened by the customs surplus that reaches Yuan through Fong anyway, and Sun's men say so. The surplus is 1.5–6 million taels in 1913 (P-5's table), and it cannot pay for the campaign against the Second Revolution: the consortium's banks met in February 1912 on an advance of 7 million taels that Tang said Nanjing urgently needed, and in March he asked them for 6.4 million taels a month for April to June (FRUS 1912, doc. 175). The campaign is paid from the loan. This is the first instance of canon's criticism of Julian's bargains in China. Why the rule does not stop Japan's lending, here or in 1917–18, is set out in P-24.

**P-7. Tang Shaoyi.** D0.1 asks which of the mission's men matter. *Proposal:* Tang Shaoyi is the one addition to the cast from the mission, because his historical posts touch Pacifica at each stage, without new events:
- 1906: the associate minister of the Revenue Council, to whom Fong reports;
- 1912: premier, who resigns over Yuan's disregard for the law. His cabinet confirms the customs arrangement of January 1912 (P-5a). He asks Pacifica for general credit, and Pacifica offers it under its rule (P-4e) on a budget the Provisional Senate votes. He resigns on 27 June before a budget is voted, and the offer lapses with him, as in 1909;
- 1918–19: a director of the Canton government and head of the southern delegation to the Shanghai peace conference of 1919, which he leaves in disgust with both sides' generals;
- 1929: head of Zhongshan, the county Nanjing makes a model county. *Hist.:* on Sun Fo's proposal Chiang made Sun's home county a model county in 1929 and put it directly under Nanjing, which appointed its magistrates, Tang among them. *Proposal:* Nanjing appoints Tang because he is the county's most eminent native and Sun Fo's ally, and because the works contract for Tangjiawan's port needs a county administration whose accounts the lender already trusts. The port is history's: the model county's status brought tax exemptions, extra budget and approval for a tax-free harbour, whose construction was later suspended. In canon Pacifican credit finishes it. The card register, the rent limit, the farm bank and the labour article are this CR's additions (S-5, S-6). In canon it is the first county where the card register and the rent limit (S-6), the farm bank (S-5) and the labour article all apply, its Pacifican-financed work being the port at Tang's own village, Tangjiawan;
- 1931: he joins the Canton opposition government (P-21).

Zhan Tianyou (the Peking–Kalgan line, 1905–09; the Canton–Hankow line, P-4; died at Hankou in April 1919), Liang Cheng (the scholarships) and Liang Dunyan stay in the historical notes.

**P-8. The Twenty-One Demands.** In canon Japan holds no lease in Manchuria or Shandong; Tsingtao was taken by Pacifica and Japan together in 1914 and its disposition is left to the peace (§28, §30); Hanyeping's debt is serviced on Pacifican credit (A8.6). *Hist.:* the demands were handed to Yuan on 18 January 1915; their first group concerned Shandong and their fifth Japanese advisers, police and railways. The genrō, angered at not having been consulted, met Katō in early May to object to his handling, and the ultimatum of 7 May reserved the fifth group except for Fukien. *Proposal:*
- *What Japan obtains* is commercial: residence, leasing of land for business and mining rights for Japanese along the Manchurian line, and confirmation of the existing Daye ore contract.
- *Why there is no Shandong group.* Tsingtao fell in November 1914 to the war council's joint force (§28), and the two crowns agreed in the war council that month to leave the German rights to the peace (MODEL date). Japan cannot demand from China what it holds jointly with Pacifica and has agreed to leave to the peace, so the first group is not drafted.
- *What China receives.* The rights are held under China's law: Japanese residents lease land and do not own it, pay Chinese taxes and come under Chinese police outside their consular courts, and the mining rights are taken with Chinese partners under China's mining law. Japan and Pacifica also undertake to support a revision of the tariff to an effective 5 per cent, which comes in 1918 instead of history's revision of 1922–23 (CE 157). It is a stage within the treaty tariff, whose rates the powers still fix; the recovery of tariff autonomy remains that of 1928–29 (§33).
- *Victoria's intervention.* When the fifth group leaks in February 1915, Julian sends Victoria to Tokyo as his envoy (canon, PROPOSED). She arrives in March (MODEL) and works through the genrō rather than Katō: her audiences with Yamagata and with Ōkuma's cabinet, with Kiyoshi beside her (canon), produce revised demands in April without the fifth group, and agreement without an ultimatum. The treaties are signed on 25 May, as in history. The channel lasts: Victoria's tie to Yamagata serves the coalition's agreement of 1918, which puts Japan's troops on Chinese soil under the coalition's command (P-24), and it ends with his death in February 1922. Katō and his party resent being bypassed, and as prime minister in 1924–26 Katō keeps China policy inside the Foreign Ministry.
- There is no 9 May, and the boycott of 1915 is short.

### C5. 1916–1923: warlords, war, The Hague and Harbin

- *Hist.:*
  - Yuan died in June 1916;
  - the Manchu restoration of 1–12 July 1917, in which Liang Dunyan served as Zhang Xun's foreign minister;
  - China declared war on Germany and Austria-Hungary on 14 August 1917 and took over their concessions at Tianjin and Hankou; some 140,000 Chinese labourers served the British and French armies;
  - Sun's Constitutional Protection government at Canton from 1917;
  - Xu Shichang, the only civilian president of the warlord years, from 10 October 1918 to 2 June 1922;
  - the Nishihara loans of 1917–18 to Duan Qirui (A8.4), and the secret Sino-Japanese military agreements of 16 and 19 May 1918, whose leak set off student protests in Japan and China;
  - the powers' arms embargo of 5 May 1919, cancelled on 26 April 1929;
  - the Zhili–Anhui war of July 1920 and the first Zhili–Fengtian war of 1922;
  - Hunan's provincial constitution, promulgated on 1 January 1922;
  - Chen Jiongming's federalism in Guangdong and his attack on Sun on 16 June 1922.
- *Canon:* the Chinese brigade (1918), Shandong at The Hague (1919), the Treaty of Harbin (1921), the standing alliance (1922), Huludao opened (1914, PROPOSED) and its industrial district under Wang Yongjiang (1923, CR-057).

**P-9. Who signs for China.** *Proposal:* the recognised government in Peking signs, and the southern government joins China's delegation. At The Hague in 1919 China sends one delegation with Canton's representative in it, as the Paris delegation had C. T. Wang in history, and Pacifica asks for that. *Hist.:* Canton's military government declared war on Germany on 26 September 1917 and sought the Allies' recognition in vain. *Proposal:* Canton joins because the joint delegation is the only seat at The Hague open to it, because no Chinese government can be seen to leave Shandong to the other, and because Pacifica, which asks, has paid Sun's movement its declared grant since 1917 (P-14; canon tension 1, case A). The north–south peace conference at Shanghai, with Tang leading the south (P-7), sits in the same months. Xu Shichang's government signs the Treaty of Harbin in 1921 with Zhang Zuolin's consent for Manchuria. *Hist.:* in 1924 Zhang refused to recognise Peking's agreement with the Soviets and signed his own at Mukden on 20 September, which shortened the railway concession by twenty years. *Proposal:* Zhang's price in 1921 is what he took for himself in 1924: a protocol signed at Mukden gives his government of the Three Eastern Provinces the line's Chinese management and its guard (P-25), the administration of the former Russian zone at Harbin, and Peking's recognition of his command. Japan agrees because the protocol confirms its running rights on the line (§25) at the line's published rates, the same for every shipper. It gets no share in the line's management, which comes only with the Treaty of Nanjing in 1928, and its officers count that among their grievances. The standing alliance binds the government of China. Nanjing does not inherit it: it holds Peking's governments to have been illegitimate and does not take their acts as its own. It accedes instead, by the Treaty of Nanjing of 1928 (CR-039), which restates the alliance's terms with the Concord as China's partner, so the alliance passes from Peking to Nanjing by accession, not succession. Xu, who wanted the Huludao port in 1909, is president when China joins the alliance, so the Huludao link of CR-042 carries through.

**P-10. No May Fourth outrage.** *Proposal:* the New Culture movement and the student unions still form, and the students' first protest is the historical one of May 1918 against the military agreements (P-24). There is no demonstration on 4 May 1919, because there is no Shandong decision to protest, and the movement does not take that date's name: the protest of May 1918 is its founding protest. With Shandong returned, their anger turns to the Anfu clique's loans and to the treaty system that remains: extraterritoriality, the concessions and the two foreign-run revenues, Fong's customs and Britain's salt. "Abolish the unequal treaties", which the Nationalists took up in 1924, becomes the movement's cry five years earlier. Fong answers in 1920 with a published plan. He is answering three things: the student unions' campaign of 1919–20, which names the customs among the unequal treaties; the Chinese graduates of the Customs College at Peking, founded in 1908 to train Chinese officers for posts the Revenue Council assigns, who find promotion stopping below commissioner; and his own age, sixty-one. The plan: Chinese commissioners at every port by 1930 and a Chinese deputy to succeed him, so that the recovery of 1928–29 is a promise kept. *Hist.:* there were significant boycotts of Japanese goods in 1908, 1909, 1915, 1919, 1925 and 1928, and then almost continuously from 1931 (Gerth, after Remer). *Proposal:* in Part C the boycotts of 1915 and 1928 are short and those of 1919 and 1931 do not happen. History's boycott of 1931 began with Wanbaoshan and the riots against Chinese in Korea in July and became continuous after Mukden in September. In canon Korea is independent, so Wanbaoshan is a dispute between China and Korea before the co-guarantors (C8), and the plot of 1931 is stopped (canon), so neither trigger falls on Japan. Fewer boycotts partly offset Japan's loss from the tariff of 1928 (A8, tradeoff 1; P-29).

**P-11. The federalists and the south.** *Proposal:* Pacifica gives federalism no political support; canon has it fund Sun's line, which is unification. Its works rule makes it deal with provincial governments, though, and in practice that rewards Hunan under its constitution and Chen Jiongming's Guangdong, which publish accounts and accept the labour article. Rewarding Chen's Guangdong strains Pacifica's relations with Sun, whose base Chen holds until June 1922. The strain has a date: in the spring of 1922, preparing his northern expedition from Shaoguan against Chen's advice, Sun asks Pacifica's consul at Canton to have the Pearl River works payments made to his headquarters. The consul answers that they go to the contractors under the contract with the provincial government, which is Chen's, and Sun says in public that Pacifica pays Chen (MODEL date). Mao takes part in Hunan's self-government movement of 1920, as in history, and, as in history, turns against Zhao Hengti's use of it after the governor's men kill the labour organisers Huang Ai and Pang Renquan in January 1922. He uses the works rule from the workers' side: the labour article in the contracts of the Pacifican-financed Pingxiang works (A8.6) is read in the night schools at the Anyuan collieries, and the strike there in September 1922, as in history, demands its terms. Mao treats the article as a tool and the rule as a critique of itself: it is a promise the employer and the governor have signed and can be held to, and a promise that stops at the edge of the Pacifican-financed sites leaves the rest of Hunan's workers outside it. It is the start of the method of P-18, where he argues for enforcing the rent limit everywhere.

**P-12. The Canton customs, 1923.** *Hist.:* part of the customs surplus was paid to the southern government in 1919–20; in March 1921 Peking pledged the surplus to its internal loans (P-23); in December 1923 Sun threatened to seize the Canton customs surplus, and foreign warships gathered at Canton to stop him. *Proposal:* the works share of the surplus, paid under A8.10 since 1891, has been divided since 1920 by published percentages for the regions where it is collected (P-23), on the model of the instalments of 1919–20, and paid to contractors, never to armies. Canton's share pays the Pearl River works, the Swatow warnings (S-14) and the Whampoa port. Sun wants the rest of the surplus, which belongs to Peking. He is stopped not by foreign warships but by the Pacific Company in Fong's preventive service, which guards the customs house under China's treaty of service with the recognised government (CR-074): Pacificans in China's service facing the man Pacifica backs. Sun accuses Fong by name in December 1923, as a Chinese who keeps China's money for foreign bondholders, and Fong answers by publishing Canton's share and its payments since 1920. Sun backs down. His public reason is that he will not have Chinese fire on Pacificans in China's service over money Canton's works already receive, and that he will take the customs question to the nation; he turns to the party's congress of January 1924. His private reason is that his province keeps its works share. The share frees Canton's own revenue for its army, as everyone at Canton knows, and other holders of customs ports copy the formula: Zhang Zuolin at Newchwang and Antung, the Zhili generals at Tianjin and Hankou. In 1931 Canton will cite it against Nanjing (P-21). Britain resents a Pacifican Inspector-General deciding what the diplomatic body decided in history, and its answer is procedural: its minister proposes that the diplomatic body confirm the percentages of 1920, so that no Inspector-General can change them alone. The body agrees in January 1924 (MODEL). The formula becomes the powers' arrangement, which is why Canton can cite it in 1931, and London regains a hold of the kind the Inspector-General's discretion had taken from it.

**P-23. The internal-loan pledge, 1921** (added after the review). *Hist.:* in March 1921 Peking's mandates created a consolidated service for its internal loans on the customs surplus, administered by the Inspector-General, which made him the finance minister of every Peking government that followed. *Proposal:* Fong declines to act as Peking's agent for internal debt. The customs pay the foreign loans and the works share by the published regional percentages, and the rest goes to Peking monthly, which Peking may pledge as it likes but not through the Inspectorate. Without the Inspectorate the pledge works through Peking's own banks: the Ministry of Finance pays each month's surplus into a consolidated fund held by the Bank of China and the Bank of Communications as trustees for the bondholders (MODEL). The pledge is only as good as Peking's honesty and its bankers' nerve, so Peking's internal bonds trade lower than history's and it borrows less at home. Peking's bankers and the factions blame Fong; the Peking governments of 1921–28 are weaker and Fong's standing with the provinces higher. The clearest sign comes in 1922: when Zhang Zuolin declares the Three Eastern Provinces autonomous, he leaves Newchwang and Antung in the Inspectorate, because the works share reaches Manchuria by the published percentages and none of the customs serves Peking's internal debt. This keeps A8.4's rule in substance.

**P-24. Peking and the war, 1917–18** (added). *Proposal:* China declares war on 14 August 1917 as in history and takes the German and Austrian concessions, which gives it its seat at The Hague (P-9). The Labour Corps serves in France. In 1918 Pacifica's railway board pays the Chinese brigade's wages directly on the line, on audited payrolls, as it pays the railwaymen (CR-030), not through Peking. Japan's Nishihara loans to Duan Qirui happen, smaller than history's 145 million yen (MODEL), and equip the War Participation Army. The military agreements of 16 and 19 May 1918 are signed and leak as in history, and the students protest them as in history. In June 1918 they are folded into the coalition's agreement with Peking on the Chinese line, here called the Khingan agreement (a proposal; canon has only Peking's price, that Chinese soldiers guard the line, CR-041). Three things change. Japanese troops cross Chinese territory only in transit on the line, under the coalition's line command and the war council, not under a bilateral Sino-Japanese command; the Chinese brigade, not Japanese troops, guards the line inside China; and the agreement is published.

*Julian's distinction between P-6 and P-24.* His rule governs Pacifica's own credit; it is not a veto on what partners lend. In 1913 Japan's share in the consortium was Japan's own affair, and in 1917–18 Japan is Pacifica's partner in the war (§28). He acquiesces in the Nishihara loans because the coalition needs Peking's consent to the line (March 1918), and he has the brigade's wages paid by Pacifica directly so that none of Pacifica's money passes through Duan's treasury. Julian does not defend it himself. His foreign minister states the rule's scope once, in the Senate in June 1918, and the government says nothing more. The distinction is consistent and convenient. Duan's parliament from 1918 is the Anfu parliament, which the rule would have refused, and Sun's men say that Pacifica keeps its own hands clean by letting Japan do what it will not. Japan's money to Duan with Pacifica's acquiescence is canon's criticism of Julian's bargains in concrete form.

**P-25. Zhang Zuolin, 1916–28** (added). *Proposal:*
- 1918–22: Pacifica's partner in Manchuria for Siberian security (canon). After 1921 the Chinese brigade becomes the Manchurian line's Chinese railway guard under his government. With Russia's privileges renounced at Harbin there is no Sino-Soviet railway war (history's of 1929).
- 1922: after his defeat by the Zhili generals he governs the Three Eastern Provinces as autonomous, as in history; Pacifica's works and the Huludao district (1923, canon) continue under his provincial government.
- *The works rule.* Zhang meets it while Wang Yongjiang runs his finances. *Hist.:* Wang, Fengtian's finance director from 1917, reorganised the police census on which taxes rested and stabilised the Fengtian dollar, and he resigned in 1926 as war spending ran out of control. *Proposal:* the Three Provinces publish their accounts from 1922 under Wang. Fengtian accepts the labour article on the works sites, because it binds the contractors there, and does not apply it in the province's own mines and arsenal; it meets the rule's other conditions in full (specified works, published accounts, service on the customs). From his resignation in 1926 no new works contract is signed with Mukden, and the existing ones run on.
- 1926–28: Grand Marshal in Peking from June 1927, as in history, and the Expedition's last enemy while Pacifica gives Sun's party its grant. This is canon's contradiction at its sharpest.
- May 1928: Pacifica and Japan, as the Concord, ask both sides to keep the war out of Manchuria. *Hist.:* Japan's memorandum of 18 May 1928 to both sides warned that it would not let the civil war spread into Manchuria and would let Zhang's army in if it retreated in good order. *Proposal:* the Concord sends it jointly. Its effect is that Zhang withdraws without a battle for Peking, the Nationalists stop at the Wall, and Manchuria's passage to Nanjing is negotiated, not fought. Zhang leaves Peking on 3 June, and Japanese officers kill him on 4 June (canon). They act against their own government's signature, so the murder breaks the Concord's undertaking; it gives Tokyo cause to punish them and Zhang Xueliang a guarantee to invoke. He invokes it when Manchuria joins Nanjing in December 1928, and Pacifica raises it at the Council at Hayama in 1929. Tanaka's cabinet, which will not punish the officers, falls in July 1929, as in history. In 1931 Thalassia's case for stopping the plot (canon) rests on the breach of 1928, because the plotters are the same officers' network.

**P-26. The 1924 coup and Puyi** (added). *Hist.:* during the second Zhili–Fengtian war Feng Yuxiang seized Peking on 23 October 1924; on 5 November 1924 Puyi was driven out of the Forbidden City, and in 1925 he moved to Tianjin. *Proposal:* as in history. Canon's "Puyi remains in Tianjin" (§35) rests on it, and the coup brings Sun north to die in Peking in March 1925, as in history. Feng's Guominjun has no Soviet arms in 1925–26 (P-28) and is weaker in the north than in history. It loses Peking in April 1926 and the Nankou pass in August, as in history but at less cost to its enemies, and Feng, with no Moscow to go to, falls back on the northwest and joins the Expedition from Shaanxi with a smaller army. Yan Xishan's Shanxi army takes Peking in June 1928, as in history, and Chiang's central army carries more of the Expedition, which raises Chiang's standing before the Central Plains war (P-19).

*Puyi and the term of 1911.* Feng's men revised the Articles of Favourable Treatment on 5 November 1924, taking Puyi's title and allowance (hist). At Victoria's request Pacifica invokes the third term of 1911 (P-4e) for Puyi's safety only, not for his title or his palace: its minister protests the manner of the eviction and asks the provisional government for Puyi's personal safety and his personal property, and obtains an undertaking. Pacifica offers no asylum. Puyi takes refuge in the Japanese legation at the end of November and goes to Tianjin in 1925, as in history.

### C6. 1923–1928: the Nationalists' model and the Expedition (D0.4, D0.5, D0.6)

- *Hist.:*
  - Chiang Kai-shek had studied at the Shinbu Gakkō in Tokyo from 1908 and served in the 19th Field Artillery Regiment of the 13th Division at Takada; he led Sun's military mission to Moscow from 2 September to 29 November 1923;
  - the Kuomintang's first congress (Canton, 20–30 January 1924) reorganised it on the Soviet model, declared the equality of the sexes and set up a Central Women's Bureau;
  - Whampoa opened on 16 June 1924;
  - Sun died on 12 March 1925; Liao Zhongkai was assassinated in August 1925;
  - the May Thirtieth shooting of 1925 and the Canton–Hong Kong strike and boycott (June 1925 to October 1926; at Shakee on 23 June 1925 British and French troops fired on the marchers);
  - Mao was acting head of the Nationalists' propaganda department from October 1925 to May 1926 and director of the sixth session of the Peasant Movement Training Institute in 1926;
  - the Northern Expedition began on 9 July 1926;
  - the British concessions at Hankou and Jiujiang were returned by agreement in February and March 1927; on 24 March 1927 Nationalist soldiers attacked the consulates at Nanjing, and British and American warships shelled the city;
  - Mao's report on the Hunan peasant movement (March 1927);
  - Japan's first Shandong expedition of May 1927, the second of April 1928, the Jinan incident of 3 May 1928, the killing of Zhang Zuolin on 4 June, and Zhang Xueliang's change of flag on 29 December 1928.

**P-13. Chiang's mission of 1923.** *Proposal:* Sun sends Chiang to Pacifica instead of Moscow, for about three months in 1923, by way of Tokyo, where he looks up the officers he trained with. In Pacifica he sees the Companies' school at Sacramento, the Polytechnic, a county board and the Party's machine. He comes back with a plan for Whampoa and a view of how an examined party governs, and Japan's army has acquaintances in Whampoa's founder.

**P-14. The Nationalists' model (D0.4).** *Proposal:* the Kuomintang takes three things from the Prosperity Party and refuses one.
- *It takes:*
  - examined cadres: party schools whose examinations qualify for posts. Sun's own five-power plan already had an examination branch, which Nanjing later set up as the Examination Yuan, so the Pacifican example reinforces an idea he held;
  - published accounts of the party's and Canton's finances, with T. V. Soong's Central Bank at Canton (1924, as in history) and the works account of P-12 as models;
  - a news wire and press of its own (no transmitter; S-12).
- *It refuses* contested elections and the secret ballot. Sun's three stages stay, and tutelage means one party without elections until a constitution. Canon's Pacifica is itself governed through one party (CR-072); the Kuomintang copies the one party and leaves out the ballot.
- *Whampoa's instructors* are mostly Chinese officers from Baoding and Japan's academies, as in history. A minority are Pacifican-trained Chinese, who leave the Companies before they go, as canon's sergeants did for Japan's army mission (CR-074); no Pacific Company serves Canton, which is not the recognised government.
- *The money (canon tension 1):* Pacifica's money for Sun's movement and Whampoa (canon; A8.7) is a declared grant from Thalassia from 1917, printed with the Office's other accounts: buildings, books and pay, not arms. *Alternative:* indirect money only (the Chinese Pacificans' subscriptions and P-12's works share), with canon amended to "Chinese-Pacifican money".
- *The result:* the warlords' accounts look worse beside the party's, and its own officers resent audits they cannot vote against.

**P-15. Labour in 1925.** *Proposal:* the May Thirtieth movement and the Canton–Hong Kong strike happen, led by the Nationalists' labour bureau and the seamen's union, since there is no Communist party. They are aimed at Britain and at the Japanese mills where the shooting began (B4). The strike committee exempts the China Merchants' Pacific line and Pacifican firms that carry the labour article, as its historical permits exempted ships that did not call at Hong Kong, so the boycott moves Hong Kong's trade to Canton, Chinese ships and Pacifica's. London calls it collusion. Britain pays most of the cost; Japan's mills pay some.

**P-16. The split of 1927, without communists.** *Proposal:* the Nationalists still split in 1927, over land and labour. The left at Wuhan (Wang Jingwei, the peasant and labour bureaus, and Deng Yanda, Whampoa's first organiser and the Expedition's political chief) stands against Chiang at Nanjing with the Shanghai bankers and officers whose families own land. In Shanghai in April the armed pickets of the labour unions are disarmed by force, with tens killed, not thousands. *Hist.:* on 21 May 1927 officers at Changsha turned on the Hunan peasant associations. In canon the associations are dissolved and their leaders dismissed, not hunted down. Wuhan rejoins Nanjing later in 1927, as in history. The paths of Chen Duxiu, Li Dazhao and Zhou Enlai with no Comintern are OPEN for a later CR. Land stays the party's open question, and the rent limit of 1930 (S-6) is its answer on paper.

**P-17. Land (D0.5).** S-6 has already decided the practice: the rent limit is enforced where a Pacifican-financed register exists. *Proposal:* Pacifica asks nothing of Nanjing's land law as a whole, unlike Komuch (CR-041), because the Concord guarantees China's frontiers, not its government. The conditions sit in the works contracts district by district. By 1932 the registered districts are the evidence the party's left cites for enforcing the limit everywhere.

**P-18. Mao Zedong (D0.6).** *Hist.:* he graduated from Hunan's First Normal School in 1918, founded the *Xiang River Review* in 1919, argued for Hunan's self-government in 1920 and was elected an alternate member of the Nationalists' Central Executive Committee in 1924. He Jian, whose army turned on the peasant associations in 1927, governed Hunan from 1929 to 1937. Deng Yanda, who founded a third party of the agrarian left in 1930, was arrested in Shanghai's International Settlement on 17 August 1931 and executed at Nanjing on 29 November 1931. *Proposal:*
- 1921–24: no Communist party to found; he joins the Nationalists in Hunan.
- 1925–27: propaganda and the peasant institute as in history; his Hunan report of 1927 is a Nationalist document, and he is on Wuhan's side in the split.
- 1928–31: out of favour in Nanjing and unwelcome to He Jian, he works for the Ministry of Railways' land offices in the works districts along the Canton–Hankow line, a national body outside the governor's reach, running the register and the rent limit. The census of 1931–32 (S-11, if CR-080 C3 is decided) prints his districts' tables. He stays out of Deng's third party.
- 1932, aged thirty-eight: Wang Jingwei's return as premier on 28 January 1932 (P-21) brings him into the party's peasant department as its head and onto the Central Executive Committee, the leading voice for enforcing the rent limit everywhere, against Nanjing's officers and bankers and He Jian's Hunan. He is a rising leader, not yet a successor to Chiang, and Deng's fate shows what Nanjing does to an agrarian leader without a patron.

**P-27. Shandong to Jinan** (added). *Proposal:* A8.13's settlement is made at The Hague in 1919 with its execution agreed in 1922: China buys back the Shandong railway on Japanese treasury notes with a Japanese traffic manager until they are paid, and the German mines go to a Sino-Japanese company. Japanese residents and interests in Jinan and Tsingtao are the reason the Tanaka cabinet sends troops in May 1927 and April 1928, as in history; the expeditions are Japan's home cabinet's, not the Concord's (the home army enters common command only for a war the Concord declares, §35). At Jinan in May 1928 Julian obtains Japan's withdrawal (canon) by the summer, not the following spring as in history.

**P-28. Arms without the Soviets** (added). *Proposal:* Pacifica joins the arms embargo of 5 May 1919, in keeping with P-3's rule, and it lapses with history's on 26 April 1929 after Nanjing's recognition. Whampoa and the Expedition are armed from the Canton arsenal, the arsenals they capture (Hanyang in 1926), German and Czech private sellers outside the embargo and the warlords' own stocks; the Mukden arsenal arms Zhang Zuolin. The Expedition advances more by defections and less by firepower than in history; the Nanjing incident of March 1927 happens and its settlements of 1928 follow history's.

### C7. 1928–1932: Nanjing with the customs

- *Hist.:*
  - the disbandment conference of January 1929 meant to cut the armies outside Manchuria to sixty-five divisions of 11,000 men, about 715,000, within a limit of 800,000, about half of those then under arms, and failed;
  - the Central Plains war of May–November 1930 against Yan Xishan, Feng Yuxiang and Li Zongren was decided when Zhang Xueliang declared for Chiang on 18 September 1930;
  - Nanjing's mandate of 28 December 1929 declared extraterritoriality abolished from 1 January 1930; Britain and the Union agreed to treat that date as the start of a gradual abolition, and the negotiations of 1931 were broken off after Mukden;
  - the Mixed Court at Shanghai had been handed to China from 1 January 1927, and the agreement of 17 February 1930 set up Chinese courts in the International Settlement;
  - Japan's tariff agreement with China, signed at Nanjing on 6 May 1930, accepted China's tariff autonomy, and China kept certain duties on Japanese goods for three years;
  - Weihaiwei was returned on 1 October 1930;
  - Hu Hanmin was put under house arrest on 28 February 1931; the National People's Convention met in May 1931, and the Provisional Constitution of the tutelage period was promulgated on 1 June 1931;
  - an opposition government formed at Canton in May 1931 under Wang Jingwei and Eugene Chen, with Tang Shaoyi among its leaders; in January 1932 Wang and Chiang agreed to lead together, Wang as premier from 28 January;
  - Nanjing floated 38 internal loans with a face value of Ch.$1,634 million in 1927–35, made necessary by its inability to "break rice bowls" by cutting its armies (CE);
  - the Civil Code, promulgated in 1929–30, gave daughters equal inheritance.
- *Canon:* the customs and tariff recovered; the Formosan administration a Concord affair with a Japanese deputy (PROPOSED); the Huludao trust until its bonds are paid; the 1931 plot stopped.

**P-19. Soldiers.** *Hist.:* about 2 million under arms in 1928 and 1.62 million in 1929 (Sheridan, with State Department estimates of 1.5–2.225 million), 2.23 million in 1935 (R, table 1.7); Rawski's point is that finance, not men, limited the armies. *Proposal (MODEL):*
- about 1.8 million in 1928 and 1.3–1.5 million by 1932: Nanjing's central army about 0.3–0.4 million, the provincial forces about 1.0–1.1 million;
- the recovered customs could pay more soldiers, as they did in history, so demobilisation is written into the works contracts district by district, like the rent limit (P-17): a province whose commander discharges men gets works, and the works hire the discharged men under the labour article;
- fewer famine refugees (S-1) and more work in the modern sector and the Manchurian settlement (S-4) help only where commanders release men;
- the Central Plains war is still fought, because Yan, Feng and Li refuse disbandment and the loss of their provinces' revenue, and Zhang Xueliang still decides it.

**P-20. Extraterritoriality and the concessions.** *Proposal:* consular jurisdiction for the Concord's nationals ends on 1 January 1930, answering Nanjing's mandate on its own date. For a term of years (proposal: ten) Concord nationals are tried in Chinese modern courts in designated cities, with foreign legal advisers and a right of appeal to a higher Chinese court, on the model of the Shanghai courts agreement of February 1930. There is no separate jurisdiction on the Manchurian line. The renunciation is decided in the Concord's common foreign ministry, normally Pacifican, over Japan's army's objections, and its officers say so. Japan's residents at Shanghai, Tianjin, Hankou and in Shandong lose their consular courts, a cost to Japan beside the cotton tariff, which P-29 offsets in part. Britain and the Union sign their treaties in 1932; the plot crisis of 1931 delays them by months, not years. The concessions follow history where canon allows: Hankou and Jiujiang in 1927; the Shanghai Settlement and the French Concession remain in 1932; Pacifica keeps only the Tsingtao anchorage (canon). In canon there is no British Weihaiwei to return (C8).

**P-21. Canton in 1931.** Remittances, the Pacific line and Cantonese film and radio make Guangdong the richest base outside Nanjing's control (B, tradeoff 6). *Proposal:* Pacifica recognises only Nanjing, a party to the Treaty of Nanjing; remittances stay untouched and untaxed (S-17). The Inspectorate is China's own after 1929, and its Chinese staff, many of them Fong's men, pay Canton its works share by the 1920 percentages and nothing more; Canton cites the 1923 formula for the rest. Tang Shaoyi sits in the Canton opposition government, so the man of Pacifica's showcase county stands with the rival base. Without Mukden the reconciliation of January 1932 needs another cause, and the 1931 flood and Nanjing's debt crisis supply it: Wang becomes premier and Chiang keeps the army, as in history. Chinese bankers and Canton both point at the foreign railway bondholders, whose loans are a first charge on the customs (A8.4) while Shanghai's bonds are cut.

**P-29. Japan's tariff offset, 1930** (added). *Proposal:* the Concord and China exchange notes in 1930 on the model of history's Sino-Japanese agreement of 6 May 1930: Japan and Pacifica accept China's tariff autonomy (already canon under the Treaty of Nanjing), and China keeps its duties on a schedule of Japanese goods unchanged for three years. Whether that schedule included cotton goods is to be confirmed before the decision; if it did, it softens A8's largest cost to Japan for the years 1930–33.

### C8. Questions carried from CR-080 and Parts A and B

**P-22. The cipher question.** The cipher room never reads a partner's traffic (CR-077 D-6: Germany 1914–18 and the Reds and Whites 1918–20 are read, Japan never, Britain in 1927 only after a check against CR-046). *Proposal:* it never reads any Chinese government, before or after China joins the standing alliance in 1922, and it reads no warlord. The General Staff's reasons are practical: Chinese traffic runs over lines Pacifica helped build (the customs' telegraph, the Mukden lines, the cables), so a discovered intercept would end Fong's and the railways' standing, and Peking and Zhang Zuolin are coalition partners in 1918–20. Pacifica's picture of China comes from the customs returns, the Chinese Pacificans and the open circular telegrams of warlord politics, so Feng Yuxiang's coup of October 1924 in Peking surprises it. The killing of Zhang Zuolin in 1928 surprises it for canon's reason, Mikhailov's death, and because the killers were Japanese officers, whom the room never reads. Whether D-6's reading of Britain in 1927 covers Britain's China traffic waits on the CR-046 check. *Alternative:* read the factions' military traffic in Manchuria in 1918–22 for Siberian security, which breaks the partner rule with Zhang Zuolin.

**P-30. Formosa's politics** (added). *Proposal:* the Chinese governor governs beside the Pacifican Director-General (§25). From the 1920s the Formosan gentry and the Polytechnic's graduates petition for an elected provincial assembly with a voice over the administration's surplus, the form history's Formosan petitions to Tokyo took in 1921–34. The Japanese deputy from 1928 (PROPOSED) is seen in Taipei as a second foreign officer, not a partner. A8.12's published end date after 1932 is the answer to the petitions.

**Other items, with recommendations**
- **The warlords' revenue and the railways.** Pacifican railway loans are secured on the customs, not on line receipts, so a warlord who seizes a line's takings does not default a Pacifican loan. Commandeered rolling stock is recorded in the railway's published accounts and billed to the province, and the bill is part of the works settlement in 1929–30 (P-19).
- **Opium.** S-9 decided enforcement. The warlords' poppy revenue (B8) is one reason disbandment fails in Sichuan and Yunnan. The Formosan stores (S-9) are the Realm's model, and Nanjing's own opium policy is China's.
- **The salt.** The salt inspectorate of 1913 stays British-led to 1928 and continues under Nanjing, as in history.
- **The party station (S-12, B tradeoff 4).** Pacifican transmitters go only to the Ministry of Communications' stations. Nanjing's party station of 1928 is equipped elsewhere. The state stations carry the party's hour at a published rate, on the Wireless Act's model of 1913; under tutelage no other party buys time, and the rule is kept for the day there are other parties.
- **Women in the Nationalists' programme and the census.** The programme follows history: equality of the sexes in the 1924 manifesto, the Women's Bureau, and the Civil Code's equal inheritance. If CR-080 C3 is decided, the census of 1931–32 (S-11), run largely by the women operators and order-writers of S-18, prints for the first time county tables of women's literacy (about 5%, S-8), schooling, employment and age at marriage, which the Women's Bureau uses in its demands on Nanjing's school laws.
- **Britain's position (A8, tradeoff 6).** Britain's face in Peking from 1885 is Hart as its minister (canon, PROPOSED). Britain keeps Hong Kong and the New Territories (leased in June 1898, as in history, which canon does not touch), its Yangtze trade, the salt inspectorate of 1913 and the lien on the Mukden–Huludao line (CR-042, PROPOSED). *Hist.:* the Weihaiwei lease of 1898 was to run "for so long a period as Port Arthur shall remain in the occupation of Russia". In canon Russia never takes Port Arthur, so Britain takes no Weihaiwei (proposal). Britain bears most of the nationalist anger of 1925–27 and turns to treaty revision from 1926–27, as in history. Its retreat after 1927 (canon, CR-049) makes it give way faster in China, Hong Kong excepted. Pacifica does not bid for British holdings.
- **Korea's rice and the Yalu.** *Hist.:* Japan's rice imports from Korea grew from 2.79 million koku a year in 1919–23 to 6.65 million in 1929–33 under the rice plan of 1920. In canon Korea is independent, so the rice comes by contract on Japanese credit, and the Korean farmers in Manchuria are Korea's citizens, not Japan's subjects. A dispute like Wanbaoshan (July 1931) becomes a matter between China and Korea for Japan and Pacifica as co-guarantors of 1894, and Japan's army loses a pretext. The Yalu crossing and the Antung–Mukden link belong to CR-080 Part E. *Recommendation:* a Chinese line with Japanese transit rights, under the Treaty of Nanjing's transit guarantee like the Harbin line.
- **Fong's succession.** Fong (born 1859) hands the Inspectorate to China in 1928–29 at seventy, with the Chinese deputy of his 1920 plan (P-10) as Inspector-General; the deputy's name is OPEN.
- **Mongolia, Tuva and Tibet.** Mongolia and the Tuva sphere are canon's (1921); Mongolia's government after the Bogd Khan's death in 1924 stays OPEN. Tibet follows history and is outside this CR.

**Tradeoffs that cannot be avoided**
1. **Neutral revenue and the chosen leader.** The trusteeship that protects Pacifica's savers (P-5) pays Yuan in 1912–16 and Peking's governments until 1928, while Julian backs Sun. Canon's criticism of Julian's bargains starts here.
2. **Staying out of the 1913 loan.** It keeps Pacifica's lending rule and gives Britain the salt and Japan a creditor's place with Yuan, while Fong's surplus reaches Yuan anyway.
3. **The students' target.** Without Shandong, nationalism turns on the treaty system, and Fong's customs is part of it. Pacifica becomes a target before Japan does; Fong's plan of 1920 and the recovery of 1928–29 are its answer.
4. **A party that publishes its accounts and holds no elections.** The Prosperity Party model gives the Nationalists discipline and credit; they refuse the secret ballot that Pacifica made its own in 1911, and Hu Hanmin's critics and the Canton opposition use Pacifica's example against tutelage.
5. **Works contracts as politics.** Conditions district by district (P-11, P-12, P-17, P-19) reward the provinces and commanders that accept them, so Pacifica's money takes sides in the provinces, and the works share frees the receivers' own revenue for their armies.
6. **Declared political money.** The grant to Sun's movement (P-14) keeps canon's wording and makes Pacifica openly a party to China's civil wars of 1917–28, which the Peking governments, Zhang Zuolin and Britain hold against it.
7. **Land.** The 1927 split and Mao's career both come from the rent question, and enforcing it only in the registered districts leaves the rest of China to the landlords and Mao his issue.
8. **Extraterritoriality.** Renouncing with Japan costs Japan its consular courts and adds to its officers' grievances; the tariff schedule (P-29) offsets part of the cost. Holding out with Japan would keep China's main grievance against both crowns.
9. **The cipher rule.** Reading no Chinese government keeps faith with an ally and leaves Pacifica blind to coups in Peking.
10. **Britain.** Most of the political cost of a nationalist China aligned with Pacifica falls on Britain: the customs, the 1925 boycott, the concessions, no Weihaiwei. Britain's retreat after 1927 in canon absorbs it, but London keeps the record (as it does at Tomsk, CR-044).
11. **Canton.** Pacifica's remittances, line and films build the south that challenges Nanjing in 1931 (B, tradeoff 6), and the 1923 formula gives Canton its argument.

## Canon tensions

Two tensions, from Part C (tensions 1 and 2 below; tension 2 was decided on 21 November 2026). Parts A and B stay inside canon. The ones to watch are:
- Kaiping's refinancing in 1900–01 by Hoover (a Pacifican in canon; history's Kaiping engineer from 1899), which canon does not mention;
- Fushun's ownership;
- an end date for the Formosan administration after 1932 (no tension if it falls after 1932);
- whether canon's §14 and §16 inputs (PROPOSED) are approved with this CR;
- **Canon tension 1 (P-14, Pacifica's money for Sun and Whampoa).** Canon: "Pacifica funds Sun in Canton" (§29, PROPOSED) and "Pacifican money supports Whampoa" (§33, PROPOSED); A8.7 (decided): "officers train at Whampoa on Pacifican money". *Case A, canon kept:* a declared grant from Thalassia from 1917, buildings, books and pay, not arms (P-14's recommendation). *Case B, canon amended:* indirect money only (the Chinese Pacificans' subscriptions and the works share), with §29 and §33 reading "Chinese-Pacifican money" and A8.7 amended. No fact changes either way. *Recommendation:* case A.
- **Canon tension 2 (C2a, the Qing's fall). Decided 21 November 2026: case A.** Canon (§27, unlabelled narrative): "The Qing falls in 1911–1912. Julian, four years on the throne, supports Sun Yat-sen's movement". The author chose Option 1, so canon is kept: Guangxu dies in November 1908, and the dynasty ends with the abdication of February 1912. Case B (Option 2, the dynasty's end moved to 1915–16) was rejected. No fact changes.
- **Watch items (proposals canon neither states nor excludes):**
  - "The Khingan agreement" (P-24) is a name this CR proposes for the coalition's agreement with Peking of 1918; canon names only Peking's price, that Chinese soldiers guard the line (CR-041).
  - Xu Shichang left the viceroyalty on 8 February 1909 (*Hist.*). Canon's 1909 charter (PROPOSED for Xu's part) then needs Xu acting from Peking as Minister of Posts and Communications, or an early-1909 charter; P-9 reads it the first way.
  - No British Weihaiwei in canon, since its lease depended on Russia holding Port Arthur (C8).
  - Chiang's mission of 1923 to Pacifica (P-13); the works share by regional percentages (P-12, P-23), which canon does not mention; the Concord's renunciation of consular jurisdiction in 1930 (P-20); Tang Shaoyi as a new cast member (P-7); the Pacific Company at the Canton customs house in 1923 (P-12); Zhang Zuolin's autonomy after 1922 and the Concord's request of May 1928 (P-25).
