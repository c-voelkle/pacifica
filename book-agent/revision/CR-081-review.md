# CR-081 outline and A-series: fresh-context review

## Summary verdict

The A-series is careful: of 31 figures and citations checked, none is a wrong number; a few attributions are loose. A8 is the weak part. Its output gain rests on waterworks that are too small by more than ten times; its coal share contradicts the outline's own figures; and it leaves out Formosa, canon's largest Pacifican-controlled asset in China. Several canon labels are wrong. "Minimal cost to Japan" is asserted, not shown: A8 lists what Japan gets, not what canon already took from Japan or what a stronger China costs Japan's exports.

## Blockers (fix before the author decides)

**B1. Output per person (A5, decision 8, decision 10).** Pomeranz's loss is for Huang-Yun alone, a region of about 15 million people with farm output of about ¥500 million (P 218–220). Recovering ¥48–100 million is about 0.2–0.3% of China's GDP, which was about ¥29–30 billion in 1933 prices (Rawski's ¥2,646m gross factory output is 2.1% of GDP on a value-added basis, R 72). Even the whole lower Yellow River and Huai basin would add only one or two points. So the 10–20% case (A5, A7 item 8) and the 10–15% "conditional on decision 10" (A8 item 8) are unsupported.

The 5–10% case is defensible: a modern sector one and a half to two times history's adds about 4–7%, plus one to three points from agriculture (seed, fertilizer, railways in famine years). Argue the waterworks on floods and famine, not GDP. ("Doubling adds 7%" is in Perkins's 1957 prices, CE 98; about 5% in 1933 prices.)

**B2. The coal share is inconsistent (A2, decision 2).** A2 says Chinese-owned mines produced 20–30% of modern output, Sino-foreign mines about 30% (Kailan the largest), and Fushun "nearly all the rest". If Kaiping/Kailan and Fushun are both Chinese, the Chinese-owned share comes to roughly 70–85% by 1928, not 55–65%. Name the mines that stay foreign, with tonnages, or raise the figure. Keelung's mines (Formosa) are outside China's historical series; add and classify them.

**B3. Canon labels.**
- **A1, row "Credit … on the Kaiping concession (§16)".** Storyline para 361 grants the 1884–85 concession in the Keelung window, so it is Keelung's, not Kaiping's, and it is [PROPOSED].
- **A1 rows on steamships and Kaiping (1870s) and the 1876–79 wheat.** These sit in para 345 after a [PROPOSED] marker, in the same run as the Educational Mission, which the outline does label PROPOSED. Confirm the scope of that marker. CR-080 §0 repeats the same reading.
- **A1, the 1921 renunciation and the railway's return** (para 659). These are [PROPOSED — CR-031], not plain canon.
- **A8, "Shandong returns …, Tsingtao anchorage on lease (PROPOSED)".** This is under-labelled. The return is APPROVED (CR-043, para 609), and the anchorage sentence in para 612 carries no PROPOSED label.
- **A8 heading "What canon already settles".** It includes the Twenty-One Demands block, which is PROPOSED. Decision 6 is built on it.
- **"Japan's compensation is the Ussuri region, Urajio, Karafuto …".** These were won from Russia in 1898–99, not given for China. The list also omits the Transbaikal client sphere (APPROVED, CR-040 R11), the Kamchatka protectorate (APPROVED, CR-031), running rights and joint management of the Manchurian line (paras 542 and 673; Treaty of Nanjing, APPROVED), Japan's share of the Formosan receipts (para 493), the Russian indemnity of 1906–10 (MODEL, para 543), and the Japanese deputy of 1928 (PROPOSED).

**B4. Formosa breaks two of A8's premises.** A8 says alignment rests "never on Pacifican ownership" and that Pacifica "must accept every rights-recovery step". Yet canon keeps the Formosan administration (camphor, the Keelung mines, the customs, the harbour) as China's grievance in 1932 (para 775), the Huludao trust until the 1930s, and the Tsingtao anchorage. A8 must either give Formosa an exit date, which would be a canon tension, or narrow the premise. Formosa also changes the numbers:
- railways: Rawski's track figures and CE's "before 1912" figure have different coverage of Taiwan (CE 148 totals include Manchuria and Taiwan);
- coal (Keelung);
- trade: in CE 66 "Japan" includes Taiwan from 1895, and in canon that trade is China's own;
- customs: the Formosan customs sit outside Fong's Inspectorate.

It is also Japan's cheapest win: Taiwan historically supplied most of Japan's sugar and much of its rice, so long Formosan sugar and rice contracts are the natural "contract instead of territory" (at a cost to Hawaiian and Philippine sugar).

**B5. The Japanese army.** The target says China's poverty "is what lets Japan's army live with it", but canon has the 1931 Manchurian plot and the May 1932 coup (paras 743, 731), and tradeoff 5 says the opposite of the target. Canon already counts Huludao among the officers' grievances (para 729). Drop the sentence: the army's material interest is the Manchurian corridor, which canon secures by joint management.

**B6. Inventions that need decisions.**
- **"Benxi stays Sino-Japanese"** assumes a Japanese railway zone in southern Manchuria (the Antung–Mukden line) that canon does not grant.
- **"Locomotive and car orders" for Japan** (decision 4) conflict with the Hayama annex's "heavy machinery" for Pacifica (APPROVED, CR-054) and with Huludao's rolling stock (para 729).
- **Tradeoff 1's "light current, which the annex already gives them"** is CR-080 X-3, a recommendation in a parked CR, not canon.

## Improvements to the recommendations

1. **Method.** Add levers for Formosa, silver and Manchurian migration, and a Japan series so that "a third of Japan's output per person" is computed. Historically it is already about 0.3 (Maddison, 1932); with CR-080 B1's faster Japan it falls toward a quarter.
2. **Coal.** Recompute as in B2. For Japan, the cheaper win is in Shandong, not Benxi. The German mines and the Shandong railway return to China in 1919 (canon). History's Washington solution, in which China bought the railway back with a Japanese loan and the mines went to a Sino-Japanese company, gives Japan a stake without territory. Hoover (Kaiping's engineer in 1899–1901, a Pacifican in canon) is the hinge for keeping Kaiping Chinese, with no new character.
3. **Tungsten and antimony.** The premise is weaker than it looks. California had tungsten at Atolia (from about 1904) and Pine Creek (1916), and Korea had Sangdong (1916) for Japan, so Pacifica's security need for Chinese tungsten is small and tradeoff 4 is largely avoidable. "Two provinces grow rich" overstates it: native miners dug the tungsten, warlords taxed it, and antimony crashed after 1918. Hunan already had its own antimony company (Huachang, 1908; verify). K. C. Li (Wah Chang) is a historical carrier.
4. **Railways.** The 1911 target is credible: history already built 544 km a year in 1895–1911 (CE 151), so 11,000–12,000 km needs only about 1.2–1.3 times that. Section 9.1's comparison with the 1928–37 pace is misleading. The 1912–28 target is not credible without saying whom Pacifica lends to through the warlord years. History slowed for political reasons (R 208; CE 151); the Nishihara loans show what lending to Peking provoked. *Better:* 11,000–12,000 km by 1911 on early credit; close to history plus the Chinese Manchurian lines and the Hankow–Canton trunk to 1927 (about 14,000–15,000 km); then Nanjing's pace from 1928 on the recovered tariff (about 16,000–17,000 km by 1932). This lowers the debt backlash and the warlords' prize. A8 must also say what secures loans after China recovers the customs in 1928–29.
5. **Electricity.** Two to three times history's use per person is fine, but state absolutes: roughly 5–8 kWh a head in 1932, against Japan's roughly 200 and over and Pacifica's 2,600. Shanghai's settlement utility, the largest plant, cannot be "Chinese municipal" without an extraterritoriality decision. Formosan hydro (history's Sun Moon Lake) is the natural showcase.
6. **Hanyeping.** Start in 1904, when the Japanese ore loans began, not in 1915. Refinancing Japan's loans at market ore prices is a cost to Yawata, not "minimal". *Better:* Pacifican credit for the coke supply and the haulage, the cause CE 147 gives (slow work on the Canton–Hankow line and the river haul of coke), so that Hanyeping can service its Japanese loans. Keep the ore contract with a price review, and make the 1915 clause unnecessary rather than blocked.
7. **The technical class and the Chinese Pacificans.** Quote the population model; 2–3% of 37 million is 0.7–1.1 million and needs the 1868 family-emigration article (PROPOSED).
8. **Output.** 5–10% above Rawski by 1932 (see B1).
9. **The Pacific line.** The win for Japan is overstated: the San Francisco route is exactly the Tōyō Kisen Kaisha's line (from 1898), and Pacifican shipowners lose too. Keep the line, but say what it costs both partners.
10. **Waterworks.** Tie this to canon's Huai conservancy survey under Sibert in 1914 (APPROVED, CR-051). Start from 1891, under Fong's revenue, not 1902. The Boxer indemnity cannot be redirected without the other powers, so use Pacifica's own share and the customs surplus. Tradeoff 7 is partly avoidable: the canal lakes covered more than 500 square miles (P 214), and reclaimed land can be sold or taxed.

## Missing tradeoffs or decisions

- **Silver and money.** China was on silver, and Pacifican loans are in gold. Silver fell by about half between 1873 and 1902 and again in 1929–31, so China's debt service rises in its own money. Canon's trade dollar (CR-073) puts Pacifica on both sides. Decide the loans' currency and any gold link (Kemmerer's commission of 1929 is the historical hook).
- **Opium revenue.** Opium paid duty and likin in the customs, the 1906–17 suppression cut that revenue, and opium later financed the warlords. This bears on what the customs can secure.
- **Warlord finance.** Railways and rolling stock were the warlords' prize in 1916–28: more line means more to capture.
- **Japan's exports to China.** Tariff autonomy from 1928 hits Japanese cotton goods as well as Japan's mills in China. This is Japan's largest cost and is missing from tradeoff 1.
- **Britain.** Britain was the largest investor (37.7% in 1914, CE 174). Kaiping kept from the British company, Fong in Hart's place and a Pacifican third (section 9.8) come at Britain's expense; held as loans, that third makes Pacifica China's near-monopoly creditor. The salt revenue and the 1913 Reorganisation Loan are absent.
- **Treaty ports and extraterritoriality.** Pacifica keeps "ordinary treaty rights" (1868, PROPOSED). The status of the settlements, cabotage and the Yangtze (Japan's Nisshin Kisen) is undecided.
- **Korea.** Its rice, the Antung–Mukden link and the Yalu are absent.
- **Russia, Siberia and Manchurian migration.** The Manchurian corridor to Transbaikalia is absent, and so is the migration from Shandong and Hebei to Manchuria, which was the largest of the period and bears on Huang-Yun and on famine.
- **Labour.** Kailan's labour contractors (R 309) against canon's paid, directly managed crews (CR-031-R1): does Pacifican credit carry a labour clause, as Komuch's credit carried a land law?
- **Famine.** The famines of 1920–21 and 1928–30 belong in A as railway and grain capacity, not only in B.
- **Missing from A.** Agriculture (60% of GDP: soybeans to Japan, cotton seed, silk at 0% growth, R 354), steel (0.03 Mt against Japan's 3.2, R 75; Anshan), cement, chemicals, the Formosan economy, ports (Dalian after 1899), roads, paper and print.
- **Add to A7:** Formosa's programme and exit; currency; agriculture and food; the Manchurian railways and Japan's transit; Shandong's railway and mines after 1919; steel.
