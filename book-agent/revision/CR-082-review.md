# CR-082 — fresh-context review

Fresh-context review, 21 November 2026. The reviewer read the draft (`CR-082.md`), AGENTS.md, the two shared rule files, the storyline passages named below, `canon_facts.toml`, the ledger's Canada entries and `population/outputs/provisioning.md`. The reviewer did not read `CR-082-outline.md` or the session records. Line numbers are the storyline's and the draft's as of this date. Approved decisions are not relitigated; where one collides with canon the collision is reported.

**Verdict.** The account (Part A) and the two schools (Part B) give Pacifica the self-interest the author asked for, and the nations' road to the Pacific crown is the most convincing thing in the draft. The CR is **not ready to integrate**. Part G adds a fourth crown without any accepting authority on the Realm's side and without touching the canon of three crowns, Ka Lōkahi and the Concord. It never mentions the question canon itself left open about British Columbia: its laws against Asians. It gives the Yukon to the colony twice, by two incompatible routes. Its 1931 treaty spends money in the one winter canon says the Realm refuses to spend. And the central case of Part A shows a country that is twice fed in 1910, which undercuts the urgency the doctrine rests on unless the draft says where the surplus goes. The impact notes list nine storyline passages; at least twenty more are affected.

## Blockers

**B1. The fourth crown has no accepting authority, and canon's "three crowns" is untouched.**
- *Passage:* G6–G7 (draft 249–251); `## Fact changes`; impact notes.
- *Problem:* The draft says who offers the crown (the legislature at Victoria) and who confirms it (the vote). It never says who accepts it. Canon is specific:
  - Line 1208: the Crown is "made of three sources: Hawaiian rank, a Filipino people's offer and the Senate's law"; Ka Lōkahi "gives the Realm, Hawaii and the Philippines one vote each over the succession, the agreed matters renewed every ten years and disputes between them".
  - Lines 1208 and 1537: the reviews fall in 1917 and 1927, so the next is 1937. A crown added in 1931 falls between reviews.
  - Line 1539: "his three crowns one party in the Concord". Lines 25 and 1332 ("under all three crowns") say the same.
  - Line 1537: the Senate refused the Concord in 1927 and gave consent and indemnity only in 1928. It is not a body that passes a new crown without a fight.
  - Line 1663: Japan demands an alternating executive at the renewal of 1932 and is refused. A fourth crown in Julian's party one year earlier changes the weight of that party, and Tokyo is not asked.
- *Fix:* Add one paragraph to G6: the Senate passes an Act of Union (name the vote; a narrow one costs nothing and gives the sufficers their last stand); Ka Lōkahi holds an extraordinary sitting, admits a fourth delegation and settles what happens on a tie of two against two; Japan is notified at the Hayama Council of 1931 and takes it up at the renewal of 1932. Add impact notes for lines 25, 1208, 1332, 1537, 1539 and 1663, and Julian's style (line 1208, PROPOSED) with a fourth title. State whether Hawaii and the Philippines vote for or against in Ka Lōkahi.

**B2. British Columbia's laws against Asians are not mentioned, and they are the hardest fact on the path.**
- *Passage:* G5–G7; storyline line 1810, unresolved item 36: "BC's anti-Asian laws against Pacifica's Japanese and Chinese citizens".
- *Problem:* A1 of the draft counts 4.7 million residents of Chinese origin and 5.6 million of Japanese origin in 1932. The sovereign's wife is Japanese and his heir is betrothed in Japan (line 1661). *Hist.* (general knowledge, to verify): British Columbia's settlers disfranchised Chinese and Japanese residents, rioted at Vancouver in September 1907 and cut Japanese fishing licences in the 1920s. These settlers are asked to vote themselves under that sovereign, beside Japan as a coequal partner. The draft does not say what franchise the vote of 1931 uses, whether Asian residents vote, what the terms of union say about entry and civic standing, or how wide the margin is. A sceptical reader stops here.
- *Fix (least cost, using what the draft already has):*
  - G1–G2 already make the miners of 1858 Pacificans and tie the colony south by rail and free entry. Say what that does to the settlers by 1931: a large part are of Pacifican birth or descent, including Asian Pacificans, and the exclusion laws are the colony's standing quarrel with Thalassia from 1907 (the riot falls in the month before Julian's accession and marriage).
  - Terms signed before the vote (the Philippine precedent, line 1208): the Realm's floors for every citizen; Caledonia keeps its own entry rule, as Hawaii does (B7).
  - Make the vote narrow, as Hawaii's was in 1907, and let one region vote no (Vancouver Island, with the capital and the naval town).
  - Close or amend item 36 at line 1810.

**B3. The Yukon is given to the colony twice, and the second way takes Canada's richest ground for nothing.**
- *Passage:* G2 (draft 241): the river grant of 1871 "is made to Britain for the colony". G4 (draft 245): "Canada has no road to the Klondike that does not cross British Columbia or Alaska. London attaches the Yukon to the colony."
- *Problems:*
  - A grant of the Yukon's and the Porcupine's navigation to the colony in 1871 makes sense only if the colony already holds the upper Yukon. G4 attaches it only in 1898.
  - *Hist.* (general knowledge): the Yukon basin went to Canada in 1870 with the North-Western Territory. In 1898 London would be taking the Klondike, and its gold royalty, from Canada in the year of the strike. The draft gives Ottawa no reply.
  - "No road" is not true: the Mackenzie, Peel and Porcupine route lies wholly in Canadian territory. It was dreadful, but it existed.
  - Canon to revise that the impact notes miss: line 1124 has Bonanza Creek "on Canadian soil where no finder's compact runs", "Ottawa's own reports" on the wire and "Canada's own wire to the Yukon".
- *Fix:* Move the decision to the fork. In 1870–71 London keeps the Pacific and Yukon slope west of the divide out of the transfer to Canada and joins it to the colony (the Stickeen Territories were joined to it in 1863; to verify). Then the grant of 1871 is coherent, nothing is taken from Canada in 1898, and G4 becomes "the colony governs the Yukon from Victoria, and the rush of 1898 makes it rich for a decade".

**B4. The treaty of 1931 spends in the winter canon says the Realm will not spend.**
- *Passage:* G6, G9 (draft 249, 255–258); R-1.
- *Canon:* line 1631 "farm and metal prices fall"; line 1635 "Pacifica raises neither tariffs nor taxes"; line 1637: the Bank "refuses to issue beyond its cover … Farms are foreclosed and county banks fail that winter". Line 1629: the Union is rescued in the same winter.
- *Problem:* In that year the draft has the Realm take over a bankrupt dominion and give Canada's prairie farmers a ten-year wheat contract at a floor price. By A3 the Realm itself has a surplus of 11 million mouths in wheat and feed. Its own foreclosed farmers get no floor. Giannini and the Industrial Board (line 1637) would say so in the Senate, and the draft gives nobody the answer.
- *Fix:* Keep the contract (R-1 is approved) and give it a price and an argument. Either the floor is a forward purchase for the years after 1941 at slump prices, stored or resold to Japan and China, and defended in the Senate as the cheapest wheat the Realm will ever buy; or the contract is an option on acreage and not a purchase. Say what the dominion's debt costs and who pays (the Patrimony, which canon lets invest abroad from 1919, is the natural buyer). Add lines 1631–1637 to the impact notes.

**B5. A Realm that is 204% fed in 1910 has no visible reason to hurry.**
- *Passage:* A3, "What it finds" (draft 127–142), and D.
- *Problem:* On the central case the Realm grows twice what it eats in 1900–1910 and still has 31% to spare in 1932. In those same years the draft has it buy more than half of Chile's nitrate and irrigate 22 million acres. A farmer buys nitrogen when it pays; with half the crop already unsold, it does not. Three consequences:
  - The motive for bringing Chile "into the fold" in 1891–1900 is forty years ahead of any need a voter or senator can feel.
  - No character before 1932 meets scarcity. The whole conflict is over a table. That can work, but the draft should know it is choosing it.
  - A surplus of 16–20 million mouths in 1910–1920 would make the Realm one of the three largest grain sellers on earth. Canon knows it only as a seller of apples, salmon and famine wheat (lines 748, 1641). Nothing in the storyline carries a grain trade of that size.
- *Fix:* Separate capacity from crop. The research is done and banked early; the fields take it up as the margin narrows (mostly in the 1920s). Then say where the surplus that is grown goes, because that is the visible payoff the author asked for: the Volga in 1891 (line 1036 already has Victoria sending grain), Japan's and China's coast, and the Entente in 1914–1918 on credit, which is part of the debt London still owes in 1927 (line 1556). Re-run the nitrogen need on that course; it also eases S1 and T9.

**B6. "Victoria Kamakea's governments hold to that for forty years."**
- *Passage:* G2 (draft 241).
- *Problem:* The name is not in canon; she is Victoria Kamāmalu. From 1871 to 1887 the government is Elias's (Providentor to 1 August 1887). Victoria is Queen of Hawaii from December 1872 and Regent of the Realm from 1887 to November 1907. No reading gives her forty years of governments from 1871.
- *Fix:* "Pacifica asks for nothing more, and Elias's governments and then the Regent's hold to that."

## Should fix

**S1. Nitrogen: the dates and tonnages do not fit canon's industry.**
- *Passage:* A3 item 6, Part C, T9.
- *Problems:*
  - The case needs 439 thousand tonnes in 1915, the year canon's Columbia ammonia starts (line 1148, under a BASF licence of 1913). No plant reaches that in its first year. By rough reckoning it is several times Germany's whole wartime synthesis, and about 6 TWh a year of electrolysis, when the river has one dam (Bonneville, 1913).
  - The script holds Chile's exports at 480 thousand tonnes of nitrogen from 1913 to 1930. That is not a sourced series (the data file interpolates three round figures). *Hist.* to verify: exports peaked near 2.7 million tonnes of nitrate in 1913 (about 430 thousand tonnes of nitrogen), fell in 1914–15, went to the Allies' explosives during the war and collapsed in the 1920s.
  - Canon passages not in the impact notes: line 1470 ("the war's arc nitrogen furnaces" stand idle in 1919, which is why the halls get their power) and line 1516 (Tizard sees "that the Sierra district's nitrogen output fell after the war and its power did not"). If the fields want 460 thousand tonnes in 1920, the furnaces do not fall idle unless the draft says the Columbia's ammonia undersells them. Also line 1033 (Haber stays in Germany), line 1544 (the annex: no Pacifican fertiliser into Japan) and line 1504 (the 90,000 tonnes).
- *Fix:* Let Chile carry the fields through the 1920s on a falling share, give the Columbia a ramp (for example 50 thousand tonnes in 1920, 250 in 1930), state that arc nitrogen cannot compete for fertiliser, and add the four lines to the impact notes.

## C. Literary effect

- **Self-interest.** Yes, and it is the draft's gain: the table gives every treaty a second reason. It is weakest where it is most needed, in 1890–1910, because the Realm is then twice fed (B5). Say what the surplus buys and the payoff becomes visible.
- **Conflict.** Sufficers against provisioners is a real quarrel between mother and son and fits the drift canon already has (1908, 1921). At present the sufficers never win anything. Give them the Gulf's tests (S11), the Fraser clause and the refusal of the four further rivers by name.
- **Agency.** The nations act: they petition, they refuse to sell, they set the waterline, they close the Fraser. The settlers, London and Ottawa are acted upon. Each needs one decision of its own (B2, S6, B3).
- **Too convenient:** London's offer; Canada building to a foreign port in the 1880s; the Yukon in 1898; a plebiscite, a treaty with Canada, a bankrupt dominion and a wheat floor all in the winter the Bank will not print; no settler party against the union.
- **Viewpoint.** The set pieces are all rooms of state. One from below would carry more: a Fraser canner or a Peace River homesteader on the day of the vote, or a Dane-zaa family shown the waterline.

## D. The open tensions and R-7b

**T3: case A.** The Charter of 1932 is brokered in Hawaii for the islands (line 1667) and is Keawe's and Victoria's last scene; widening it to four crowns dilutes it. Compacts by nation are also what canon does on every mainland river. Add only that the two instruments are a year apart and Julian accepts a limit in each.

**T5: case B, with a different motive.** Case A unpicks lines 704, 1127 and 1365 for no gain. But case B as written has Canada build over the Rockies to another country's harbour in the 1880s for trade that does not yet exist. *Hist.* (general knowledge) the line was the price of British Columbia's entry. Supply the motive London had: an imperial route to Esquimalt and Hong Kong, with a mail subsidy, on which Pacifica's quiet money rides. Allow the date to slip a few years and check "from 1887" in line 704.

**T8: case A, amended.** Keep the sentence to 1931, but the draft's own G5 (the nations forced into the Columbia compact, the petitions received) means Victoria's ministers have reason to count acres well before 1931. Let a British Columbian or Canadian voice say so in the 1920s, and let the sentence end in 1931 in the storyline's own words.

**R-7b: keep the direction, change two means.**
- Holding the *Itata*'s rifles is right in law (the venture licence of 1854, line 260; the Companies Act's "never in the Americas"). But *Hist.* the rifles were the Congress's, the Congress won, and the power that held them earned Chile's lasting resentment (to verify: the *Baltimore* affair). The draft then has that same Congress take the Realm's loan. Add the step that turns it: the Realm holds the rifles, recognises the junta at Iquique as a belligerent, and brokers the armistice before the last battles.
- Do not buy out the British houses. The draft names this as its weak point, and the Realm needs the nitrate, not the fields. Use long purchase contracts at Iquique, a loan to the Chilean state secured on the duty, and Chilean buyers taking the fields over twenty years on Realm credit. That avoids a quarrel with Britain in the 1890s and fits the PROPOSED line 1116.
- "Guarantor by 1900" should say against whom, and should be squared with the Pampas (S11).
