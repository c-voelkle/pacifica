### A2. What canon says about food

- **Land and water:** Powell's irrigation districts by watershed; farms sold by the Land Fund on instalments; the family farm of 80 irrigated or 160 dry acres as a ceiling per household (CR-071). The energy workbook pumps about 2.6–3.6 million acres electrically in 1929.
- **Limits canon set itself:** every Columbia dam is leased from the peoples of its reach and none stands where salmon cannot pass; none at The Dalles, the lower Snake or the Grand Coulee (CR-055). The Thalassia Plan of 1928 reserves watersheds and minimum stream flows, and the Charter of 1932 makes that law.
- **Nitrogen:** electrolytic ammonia on the Columbia and Haber–Bosch on Sacramento Valley gas feed the Central Valley, the Imperial Valley and the Columbia Basin, the crowns' sugar and rice, Korea and the China trade (CR-054).
- **Exports:** California wheat goes to North China's famine in 1876–79; the Northwest sells apples and salmon to Asia and the Union.
- **The sea:** the seal convention, the salmon and halibut treaties (CR-058, CR-070); Heʻeia's and Puʻuloa's fishponds protected.
- **What canon does not say:** how many acres are irrigated, what they yield, what a Pacifican eats, whether the Realm is a net food exporter in any year, or how many people the domain can carry.

### A3. The central case (MODEL)

`population/provisioning.py`, with `data/provisioning.toml`; the full report is `population/outputs/provisioning.md`. It reads the canon run and feeds nothing back.

**How it counts**
- **Diet (author):** the Realm's present diet, held constant. Our timeline's Union used about 2.1 acres of crop land a head for its own food and fibre in 1910–34, and that is the measure.
- **Land (census, our timeline):** irrigated acres by state in 1929 and the totals of 1889–1929, cut to canon's frontier; 13.8 million irrigated acres there in 1929. The ceiling is the 1940 census's irrigable area in enterprises, 22 million acres, with dry land at 1.2 times its 1929 extent, 15.8 million.
- **Canon's farmers:** canon has about three times history's people on the same land, so they work history's acres a head until the ceilings stop them. They reach the irrigated ceiling about 1910.
- **Concerted research (author, 18:16 and 18:30):** our timeline's gain on Washington's dry-farmed wheat is a verified series: flat at about 19 bushels an acre from 1900 to 1929, then 1.37 times that in the 1940s, 1.81 around 1960 and 2.13 around 1970. The script splits that gain into six innovations, each with the years it reached the fields in history and the years canon's programme could have it there:

| Innovation | Gain | Our timeline | Canon, earliest | Canon, central | Years gained |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanised fallow and timely work | +15% | 1930–45 | 1905–20 | 1910–25 | 20 |
| Breeding and seed treatment before short straw | +15% | 1930–45 | 1895–1905 | 1900–12 | 33 |
| Cheap nitrogen | +22% | 1950–65 | 1900–15 | 1900–15 (author) | 50 |
| Selective hormone weedkiller | +8% | 1948–58 | 1925–35 | 1930–40 | 18 |
| Short-straw wheats (need nitrogen) | +12% | 1961–70 | 1905–15 | 1905–15 | 55 |
| Deep-furrow drill and early sowing | +7% | 1965–70 | 1900–05 | 1910–15 | 55 |

  - **What sets each date.** Breeding is bound by the breeder's calendar, twelve to fifteen years from first cross to release (*Hist.:* Farrer 1889–1901, Strampelli 1900–14), which winter nurseries in Hawaii and Baja can halve. The drill and the fallow are bound by nothing but a station that asks. The weedkiller is bound by a science that did not exist before the 1920s. Nitrogen and the short wheats are bound by nitrogen's price. Canon's own ammonia comes on the Columbia in 1915 (CR-055); before that the world's fertilizer nitrogen is Chile's nitrate. **Decided (author, 18:41):** the programme goes after nitrogen from about 1900, and Chile is brought into the Pacific fold.
  - **What matters most.** Nitrogen with the short wheats is +37% of the +109%, and it is the critical path: a third of the whole gain waits on one industrial date.
  - **The result.** The Realm's yields are 1.37 times history's in 1910, 1.85 in 1920 and 1.93 in 1930, which our timeline reached in 1944, 1961 and 1964. The lead is 34 years in 1910 and 41 in 1920; after that the Realm has used what there is, and only the weedkiller is still to come.
  - **Sourced and not.** The total, the dates and three magnitudes (Gaines a quarter above the wheats it replaced; the drill's 30% in the driest zone; breeding about half of wheat's yield gains in the plains literature) are sourced. The split among the six is MODEL, and when dated as history it fits the verified series within about 10%. *Canon:* the Colegio de Santa Fe has taught acequia irrigation and dry-land farming since 1864 and Deseret's university measures water (CR-036 D13, D14).
- **The sea:** our timeline's Pacific landings at their peak of 1936 (the Pacific states' 1.9 billion pounds, verified, and Alaska's), reached by 1930 and counted as animal food that needs no crop land.
- **Growth (author, 17:59 and 18:52):** from each table year the yearly rate falls in a straight line from the last decade's rate over a century: to 1.5% in the tables before 1908, and to 2.0% in the tables of Julian's reign, because Julian is a provisioner and the Office assumes less slowing for the time being. Both figures are a story assumption (author, 19:09).
- **The Philippine crown (author):** counted from 1908, as its own ledger on its own diet. It roughly feeds itself.

## B. The doctrine (F-4 to F-8, F-14)

**B1. Three steps.**
1. **Powell's ceiling, 1888.** Powell's office reports how many acres the domain's water can reach: about 22 million. He is believed, because his office draws the districts.
2. **The first table, 1890.** With the Realm's first census the Office of Public Measures prints the population's course beside Powell's acres, and the year they cross: 1923.
3. **The revision with each census** (1900, 1910, 1920, 1930), laid before the Senate. Research moves the crossing back with each table, by less each time (A3).

**B2. Where the question comes from.** It does not come from Elias as a charge. It arises in the offices under Victoria. She orders the table to show that the domain suffices. Julian reads the same table the other way.

**B3. Two schools.**
- **The sufficers.** The domain carries what its water carries. Entry is selective; the Realm should be rich and few. Victoria, Powell's office, the Hawaiian court and the nations' councils hold this view, and later Keawe.
- **The provisioners.** Numbers are power. The Realm is the smallest of the great powers and will be outgrown unless it grows, so the half-billion must be fed and the acres found. Julian holds this view, with the Polytechnic's engineers, the Works and the Navy.
- The names of the men and women in each school are settled when the storyline is drafted.

**B4. The Office's assumptions (story assumptions, author).** The diet is held constant. The regency's tables let the growth rate fall over a century to 1.5% a year. Julian's tables, from 1908, let it fall to 2.0%, and count the Philippine crown.

**B5. The rule of two figures.** From 1890 every treaty, loan, lease and holding abroad goes to the cabinet with two figures beside its price: the mouths it feeds or the people it brings, and by what year. Under Victoria the figures are read as a limit. Under Julian they are read as a target.

**B6. Public table, unstated purpose.** The table is public, like every table of the Office. The conclusions drawn from it are cabinet minutes. The neighbours can do the arithmetic, and some do (T1).

**B7. Entry.** Each table carries an entry course. Victoria's tables hold entry to what the home soil feeds. Julian lifts it from 1908. Hawaii keeps its own rules, as in canon.

## C. At home and at sea (F-1c to F-1e, F-9, F-10)

## F. The provisioners' plan for the century (P-1 to P-5)

The home ceiling is passed about 1941–43. Julian's table of 1930 has 562 million in the Realm a century on. The plan has four works.

1. **Yields at home go on rising.** About 75 million mouths from the home soil, against 47 million in 1932.
2. **The northern water** (Part H).
3. **The sellers grow more, and the Realm buys it.** Seed and method are sold to anyone, at one royalty. Long nitrogen contracts go with long crop contracts. They are refused only to a power at war with the Concord. The Pampas are a sixth theatre, by contract only, reached through Chile. About 250–300 million mouths.
4. **The table changes without being lowered, and the umbrella is counted differently.** Poultry, pork, fish and the soybean give the same table from a fifth to a quarter less land than beef. Every people under the umbrella that is made self-feeding adds to the Concord's numbers and takes nothing from its granary.

The plan closes, barely, and only if all four works succeed. Three of the four are not territorial. Its weak point is the third: it holds only while the sellers sell.

**Script.** The four works go into `provisioning.py` as a century module, with the northern water on sourced flows.

## G. Caledonia: British Columbia and the Yukon change crowns (N-1 to N-5)

**G1. 1858.** The miners who go up the Fraser are Pacificans from San Francisco. Britain makes the mainland a colony, as in canon and in history.

**G2. 1869–71, the fork.** British Columbia does not enter Confederation. Pacifica does not encourage it to. It offers the bankrupt colony a railway south to Puget Sound, a loan, and free entry for its coal and fish. Britain keeps the colony and Esquimalt. Canada stops at the Rockies. Pacifica asks for nothing more, and Victoria Kamakea's governments hold to that for forty years. The grant of the Stikine, the Yukon and the Porcupine in the spring of 1871 is made to Britain for the colony.

**G3. The Canadian Pacific.** It reaches Burrard Inlet over the colony's soil, on running rights (T5).

**G4. 1898.** Canada has no road to the Klondike that does not cross British Columbia or Alaska. London attaches the Yukon to the colony, and it is governed from Victoria. The boundary, the railway from Skagway and the assay offices stay as in canon. In 1897 Pacifica sells the old telegraph line to British Columbia, not to Canada (T7).

**G5. 1900–27.** The colony signs no treaties with the nations. South of the line their kin lease dam sites and keep their valleys. The petitions of 1909 and 1913 go to London and also to the Pacific crown. The western waters treaty of about 1906, the Columbia compact of 1922–25 and the Halibut Treaty of 1923 are British Columbia's business (T6). At Pacifica's insistence the Columbia compact is also signed with the nations of the upper river. British Columbia becomes a dominion in 1907, with Newfoundland (date PROPOSED).

**G6. 1927–31.** Britain's retreat is complete in 1927. The slump of 1929 breaks the dominion's finances. London offers three courses: government by commission, union with Canada, or the Pacific crown. The legislature at Victoria offers the crown. In 1931 the vote confirms it: the settlers together, and each nation for itself. The nations' compacts are signed inland, at Spences Bridge.

**G7. The Crown of Caledonia.** Julian wears it with the crowns of Pacifica, Hawaii and the Philippines. The legislature stays at Victoria. The Yukon is a territory of the crown, with its council at Dawson. Alaska stays under the Northern Commission.

**G8. The Fraser clause.** It is the one entrenched term of the union. No dam is built on the main stem, and no water leaves the basin. Only the nations of the river can reopen it.

**G9. What Canada is given** (a treaty of 1931, and no share of the Columbia):
1. Its railway's running rights to tidewater, and a bonded Canadian quay on Burrard Inlet.
2. A ten-year wheat contract at a floor price. This is R-1's contract.
3. A Mackenzie waters commission. The Peace and the Liard run east into Canada, so the northern water makes the Realm the upstream party. Canada is paid for the water it loses and sits on the commission.

The boundary is declared settled. The Realm repeats that it takes no people who have not voted. Ottawa can read that either way.

*Hist.:* about 30,000 miners on the Fraser in 1858; the annexation petition of 1869; entry into Confederation on 20 July 1871 and the threats to leave in 1874 and 1878; almost no treaties in the province, the Nisga'a petition of 1913 unheard, and land-claim funds made an offence in 1927; the Yukon's 27,219 people in 1901 and 4,157 in 1921; Western Australia's vote of 1933 refused at Westminster; Newfoundland's bankruptcy of 1932.

## H. The northern water (P-2, N-6, N-7)

- **Approved (author, 19:29):** Julian's engineers propose it and prevail on it, because it is critical to national survival.
- **Dates.** Surveyed from the 1910s. Proposed with the plebiscite of 1931. Leased from the nations of each river under the compacts of Spences Bridge (T3). Built in stages after 1932.
- **Size (author, 20:32).** The first stage is about 20 km³ a year, which keeps each river's loss near a fifth. The ceiling in law is 45 km³.
- **Source.** The Peace and the Liard, which carry no salmon. The Fraser is closed (G8). The Yukon, the Stikine and the Skeena are salmon rivers and fall under canon's rule.
- **What 45 km³ waters.** 36.5 million acre-feet: 10–12 million acres at 3.0–3.5 acre-feet an acre. That feeds 18–22 million people at the yields of 1930 and 30–35 million at the century's, which is 6–7% of the century's gap.
- **Energy.** Lifting 45 km³ by 300–450 m takes 4.8–7.2 GW as a year-round average, and 11–17 GW of pumps if the lifting is done in five high-water months. The water gives back 4.3–6.1 GW falling 350–500 m to the fields. The drops are built on the canal, off the salmon rivers, and the pumps are fed from a dam in the Peace's canyon.
- **Limits.** The reservoirs stop where the lessor nations set the waterline. Every irrigated acre has a drain.
- **The larger plan.** The engineers draw the plan at about 135 km³, with four more rivers. The compacts refuse it. *Hist.:* the Parsons plan of 1964.
- The river flows, lifts and elevations are from general knowledge and are verified before integration.
