# CR-083 review

Fresh-context review of CR-083 revision 3, 22 November 2026. The reviewer read the draft, AGENTS.md and current canon, and not the writer's notes or earlier reviews.

**Verdict.** Not ready to integrate. Part A (Caledonia) is close: it needs a calendar that fits 1931, a defined entry rule, and the water made public. Part H (Hayama) does not yet fit canon's Concord: the bargain is written as if Japan had a foreign policy and a navy of its own, and what Japan receives is mostly taken back by canon's renewal of 1932. Six blockers, eleven should-fix items. `lint_canon.py --only` is clean; `cr_facts.py preview` finds the five canon passages the draft already lists.

Line numbers are those of `master_storyline.md` on `drafts/cr083-caledonia` today.

## Blockers

**B1. The year 1931 does not fit after September.**
- *Passage:* A6, A7, A10, A12, A13, J-2, J-5.
- *Problem:* A6 makes Britain's leaving gold the reason London gives way. Canon dates that to September (1687: "Britain leaves gold in September while its debt to Pacifica keeps its gold-dollar value"). Everything else then has to follow in fifteen weeks: Westminster's Act (Parliament was dissolved on 7 October 1931 and the Statute of Westminster took the December session), the terms, compacts "nation by nation" in three places, the settlers' vote, each nation's vote, the Senate's Act of Union, the treaty with Canada, the board's wheat contract, and a Hayama Council that must come after the Manchurian plot (J-5), which canon places after Wanbaoshan "in July" (1720). The same Senate is, that winter, backing the Bank's refusal and hearing Giannini on the granary (1695), having refused Julian the Gulf's shorter count (1100). The draft also never says whether Japan consents before or after the settlers vote; if after, Julian has signed terms he cannot yet honour.
- *Fix:* Only the plebiscite is fixed at 1931 (N-2). Move the default to 1930 and spread the rest: the legislature's offer in the winter of 1930–31; terms in spring; the lessor nations' compacts and Spences Bridge in summer; the plot stopped in early autumn; the Hayama Council after it; September's gold crisis as the event that ends London's resistance; the settlers' vote in late autumn; Westminster's Act, the Senate's Act of Union and the treaty with Canada in the winter of 1931–32, in the same sittings as the granary. The union takes effect in 1932. The fact-change events `caledonia-act-of-union` and `canada-treaty-1931` then need the year the author chooses, and "counted in the Realm's population from 1931" (A11) becomes 1932.

**B2. The bargain treats Japan as a power with its own navy and foreign policy; canon's Concord gives it neither.**
- *Passage:* J-2, J-4, J-5, J-6; ledger row "The Hayama bargain".
- *Problem:* Canon, 1597: "Foreign Affairs, the common Navy and their Finance belong to the Concord … convention gives Japan the Navy, while Foreign Affairs and Finance normally remain Pacifican. The western squadron is reserved to the Japanese naval party until the 1932 renewal." 1595: the executive "is entrusted to the Crown of Pacifica". 1734: "Japan's overseas commands passing to common control at the price of renewal … The naval party loses its reserved squadron arrangement". Three consequences:
  1. "The Realm's undertaking not to oppose Japan in the Netherlands Indies" has no subject. A treaty with the Dutch is negotiated by the common Foreign minister, a Pacifican, and any fleet "in sight" of Batavia is the common fleet under Julian's executive. Julian is not a bystander to the pressure; he is its author. That collides with "money, partners and published terms, and no flag" (1620), which the draft cites in its own support, and with the author's rule of contract over coercion.
  2. What Japan gets is withdrawn within a year. Davao is a station for the reserved western squadron in 1931 and a common station in 1932 (J-4 says so). The same renewal refuses the alternating executive, and the winter refuses Takahashi's dollars (1695). Add J-3's freeze on Japanese titles and J-5's rule that the oil is pooled and refined by Pacifica. Japan pays with its consent and its army's humiliation and receives a base it commands for months. The author asked for a symmetrical bargain with a strong Japan; this one is lopsided toward the Realm, and a strong Japan would not sign it.
  3. Zamboanga (J-6) grants nothing. Japanese-crewed ships are common-fleet ships and already berth at common stations; the Philippine crown has "its own budget and army" (1234) and no navy, so "the Philippine crown's own station" does not exist in canon.
- *Fix:* Restate the bargain in the Concord's terms. (a) The Council of 1931 resolves that the common ministries will seek concessions in the Indies for Japanese houses, by treaty the Dutch sign and ratify, with no territory and no landing; the Realm's delegation undertakes not to block it. Julian then owns the policy, and the draft should say what limit he sets on the fleet's use. (b) Give Japan something the renewal does not take back: the Davao lease in the Emperor's name for a long term with a Japanese commandant by right, as the Navy ministry is Japan's by convention; an amendment of the Hayama annex (1600) letting Japan refine its own Indies crude, or lifting the bar on aluminium "before 1937" for Bintan's bauxite; the chair of the oil pool. (c) Say that the renewal of 1932 is negotiated with Davao already on the table, so 1734's "price of renewal" and the refused alternating executive are the second half of one negotiation, not a reversal of the first.

**B3. Nobody with authority leases Philippine soil.**
- *Passage:* J-2, J-3, J-6; A10.
- *Problem:* The bargain is struck at Hayama by "the two parties of the Concord", and the Philippine crown pays for the Realm's northern crown with its own land. Canon: "Neither crown is subordinate"; the kingdom has "a single-chamber Cortes with a permanent commission, its own budget and army" (1234); in the Cortes "those who would separate the Philippine crown from Julian's" already call Mariveles an enclave (1681); Ka Lōkahi exists for "disputes between them" (1234). Julian as Rey de Filipinas leasing Davao so that Julian as Sovereign may wear Caledonia is the conflict of interest the separatists have been waiting for. Rent and a freeze on titles are not a price for that; they are the minimum. The draft also never has the Cortes vote. The century plan counts "Mindanao and the Philippine crown's own frontier" among the Realm's sellers (1672), so the Realm has its own reason to keep Davao's land out of Japanese title; the draft should say so, because it shows whose interest J-3 serves.
- *Fix:* The Cortes votes the lease, narrowly, with the separatists against. Its price is the Philippines' own: the freeze, the rent, Philippine law over the settlers' courts, and one thing it has wanted from the Realm (the author's choice; candidates are a Philippine seat on the oil pool, since Tarakan lies across the Celebes Sea from Jolo, or the common fleet's southern dockyard work at Mariveles). The extraordinary sitting of Ka Lōkahi (A10) is where the Philippine delegation records the price; that gives the sitting a purpose beyond a minute.

**B4. "Caledonia keeps its own rule on entry" is undefined, and every reading breaks something.**
- *Passage:* A7 terms 1 and 2; A11 (N-12); A10 (Japan's consent).
- *Problem:* Caledonia's people are "citizens of the Realm" (A11), and the Realm counts about 5.8 million Japanese-origin and 4.8 million Chinese-origin citizens of some 37 million (1203). If the entry rule can stop a Realm citizen from San Francisco settling at Vancouver, it is a race bar inside the Realm, against the floors ("no bar of race or origin", 918) and against Japan's treaty right of 1907: "family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans" (1200). If it cannot, the rule protects the settlers from nothing they fear, and the draft's main reason for their yes is hollow. Canon tolerates White Australia in a foreign ally (1752); it has never tolerated it inside the crowns. Japan is asked in the same year to consent to this crown, and the draft has it say nothing about a rule aimed at its own emigrants; it takes Davao instead. Canon's only support for "as Hawaii does" is one clause about the 1880s (752: "Hawaii keeps its own entry rules"), before the Act of Settlement.
- *Fix:* Define the rule in the terms. The least-cost version: it governs entry from outside the crowns and the Concord, names no race, and cannot bar a citizen of the Realm or a Japanese subject entitled under 1907. Then give the settlers a reason that survives it. Canon has one: the labour article's "the same wage for the same work" and the eight-hour day (AGENTS.md, CR-071), which removes the undercutting that white labour in Vancouver organised against. State Japan's price for accepting any entry rule at all, or state that the 1907 terms run in Caledonia from the day of union.

**B5. The Crown's treaty promises a purchase by a board the Crown cannot direct.**
- *Passage:* A12 item 2, A13, ledger row "Treaty with Canada".
- *Problem:* Canon, 1695: "the board, which the Crown cannot direct, votes the granary as an investment". A13 makes the wheat contract "a forward purchase by the Patrimony's board" and A12 makes it a term of a treaty signed by the Crown. Either the Crown has bound the board or the treaty's main consideration to Canada is not the Crown's to give. Second, canon's granary buys abroad only "beans and rice, which the Realm's fields do not grow", and pays "in the Columbia's ammonia and the Realm's machines from plants the slump has half idled" (1695). The Realm exports wheat until 1941–43 by the draft's own figure. A ten-year floor under Saskatchewan's price, in the winter when "farms are foreclosed" and "the granary takes crop, not mortgages", resold into the Realm's own markets in Japan and China, is the purchase Giannini can beat. The three answers in A13 are the granary's answers repeated; the third ("cheapest the Realm will ever buy") is untrue of a ten-year floor if 1932 is lower than 1931, which it was.
- *Fix:* Two instruments. The board votes its contract with the prairie pools' selling agency first, as an investment, paid in machines, ammonia and carriage in the Realm's ships and through the bonded quay, so that the money comes back as orders (canon's own argument for the granary). The treaty records that the contract exists and gives it the quay and running rights. Shorten the floor or make it a price band. Let Giannini win something: the home crop's purchase is enlarged before the prairie contract is voted.

**B6. The water is hidden from the voters whose consent the union rests on, and the hiding cannot hold.**
- *Passage:* B ("The canal south is in the compacts' text and in nobody's speech"); A7; A10; A12.
- *Problem:* The draft contradicts itself. The sufficers "argue that the crown is taken for the water" in the Senate (A10). The treaty pays Canada "for the water it loses" (A12). The Fraser clause, in the terms signed before the vote, forbids water leaving the Fraser's basin (A9), which tells any reader that water is leaving another. The compacts are signed by a dozen councils before the vote. Yet the settlers are to vote on "Caledonia's own power and irrigation". A7 claims "Nobody can say afterwards that the union was carried by people the settlers had kept from voting"; they will say instead that it was carried by people who were not told. Canon's rule is silence about aims, never a false paper (CR-050), and "published terms" (1620).
- *Fix:* Publish it. The northern water is the bankrupt dominion's one asset large enough to refund its debt: the lease rent is the security for A13's refunding (proposed item 9), and that is the settlers' reason to vote for it. What stays in the cabinet's minute is CR-082's aim, as A13 already has it. The split of the rent between the lessor nations and Caledonia's treasury must then be stated; at present the nations lease the water (B) and "Caledonia's Crown lands" earn the rent (A13).

## Should fix

**S1. The fork of 1869–71 has no motive for Pacifica or for London.** Canon gave the opposite reasoning: "a strong Canadian buffer is worth more to it than a province that would provoke Britain" (708). The draft replaces it with "Pacifica asks for nothing more", which reads as altruism or as a sixty-year plan. *Fix:* give 1871 its own interest: Nanaimo's coal for the fleet and San Francisco, the Fraser's trade, and no second transcontinental power on the Pacific. London's interest was to be rid of the colony's cost; say that Pacifica's loan removes the cost and that Esquimalt needs a hinterland London does not have to pay for. Also on 708: "The scandal of 1873 over the foreign money behind Allan's syndicate" arose from the railway promised to British Columbia in 1871; without that promise the sentence needs a new cause or goes.

**S2. A6: who holds the dominion's bonds, and why is there no rescue short of union?** Bonds "payable in gold dollars" were sold in San Francisco, so the Realm's banks are the creditor that ends up with the debtor. In the same winter Pacifica "lends gold rather than hoarding it" and rescues the Union (1687, 1693), and CR-082 calls nothing in from Chile. Ottawa and the sufficers will say the Realm foreclosed. *Fix:* the Realm's banks offer the refunding without union; London refuses to guarantee a dominion refinanced from San Francisco; the legislature then prefers the crown to the commission. London's price should be its own debt: canon has Britain "managed through debt" (1754) and its gold-dollar debt with "base rights … as possible payment in kind [OPEN-019-05]" (1385). An instalment remitted for the Act is a motive; "London gives way" is not.

**S3. The Yukon's boundary leaves the Liard's head in Canada.** A2 reserves "the basin of the Yukon and the Porcupine". The Liard rises in the south-east of the historical Yukon, east of the divide, and enters British Columbia at Lower Post. Under A2 Canada is upstream of Caledonia on the Liard and downstream again at Fort Liard, and A12's "the Realm is the upstream party" is only half true. *Fix:* reserve the upper Liard too, as history's Yukon boundary does, or accept Canada as an upstream party on the Mackenzie commission. Also "Canada stops at the Rockies" (A2): the colony's boundary north of 54° is the 120th meridian, and the Peace country lies east of the Rockies. That is why the Peace is Caledonia's; say so.

**S4. The geography of the settlers' vote is the wrong way round.** The riot of 1907 was in Vancouver, and the organised hostility to Asian labour was on the mainland coast and in the Fraser's fisheries, not on the Island. The Island's no is about the capital, the navy and Britain. *Fix:* Vancouver and the canneries split or vote no; the interior (the Kootenays, the Okanagan, the Cariboo, the Peace) carries it, where sixty years of the railway south have settled Pacificans (A5). Name who campaigns: the Canadian Pacific is the largest interest in Vancouver and, by T5, partly Pacifican money; Ottawa's option is on the ballot paper or it is not. The draft should say which.

**S5. The nations' vote and the signing places.**
- "A nation that votes no keeps its compact" confuses two acts. What is voted: the compact, or the crown? Britain leaves either way, so a nation cannot vote to stay British. *Fix:* each nation ratifies or refuses its compact. Where there is none, CR-070's rule applies ("no land is granted on a nation's country without its compact"), and the Realm's line "it takes no people who have not voted" is amended to what is true.
- Compacts "nation by nation" across the dominion cannot be made in one season. *Fix:* the lessor nations and the Fraser's nations sign in 1931; the rest follow under the standstill rule.
- The Sekani's country is the Finlay and the Parsnip (McLeod Lake, Fort Grahame, Fort Ware), not Fort St John, and it is their valley the Peace reservoir floods. The Fort Nelson Dene (the Liard's largest tributary in the dominion) and the Tahltan (the Dease) are missing. The canal crosses Dakelh and Secwépemc country in the upper Fraser's basin and needs their leases; they do not live near Spences Bridge. Fort St James, the capital of the fur-trade New Caledonia, is the obvious central signing place and ties to A11's name.
- A5 has the Columbia compact "also signed with the nations of the upper river"; B has the water enter the Columbia "above every compact reach". It enters in exactly their reach.

**S6. The northern water: physics and neighbours.**
- Canon's Realm generates about 2,600 kWh a head in 1929 (AGENTS.md, CR-052), about 11 GW on average. The draft's lift is 4.8–7.2 GW average at the ceiling and 2.1–3.2 GW for the first stage: a fifth to a third of the Realm's whole generation of 1929 for the first stage alone, on a line a thousand kilometres north of canon's 287 kV grid. A dam in the Peace's canyon yields perhaps 1.5–2 GW on average from the whole river; it does not feed the pumps. The return energy is generated at the far end.
- The lift may be overstated for the Peace's share (the divide from the Parsnip to the Fraser at Summit Lake is low) and is about right for the Liard's (over Sifton Pass). Both need the sourced elevations the draft already owes; I could not verify them.
- The fields: 10–12 million acres do not exist on the Columbia Plateau, and canon forbids the Grand Coulee (1163, 1506). Fields further south need lifts that are not in the energy sum. Until the fields are named, "26 million mouths" is not a result.
- Twenty to 45 km³ a year added to a Columbia of roughly 90 km³ at the line changes every compact reach below: fish passage, flood, temperature. "Above every compact reach" does not exempt the reaches the water then runs through. The peoples of each reach have a say under CR-055.
- Downstream: the Peace feeds the Peace–Athabasca delta and the Slave, and the Slave is the Works' own ore road from Great Bear (1265: "down the Athabasca, the Slave, the Mackenzie"). Low water is Canada's and the Works' navigation problem; it belongs in A12.
- "The Peace and the Liard … carry no salmon": true of Pacific runs. A small chum run from the Mackenzie has been recorded in the Liard as far as British Columbia (1979–80; not found again in 2000–01). The lessor nations would know it. One clause covers it.
- By the draft's own figure the ceiling meets 5% of the century's need. That is the sufficers' best argument (see Literary effect) and is nowhere used.

**S7. The oil pool removes the lever Julian has just used.** Canon, 1720: "Julian threatens the resources Tokyo cannot easily replace: oil from Pacifica and the Gulf Republic". J-5 then offers Japan the Indies' oil in the same season and calls the result "a dependence that runs both ways". For the Realm it is the loss of its one proven restraint on Japan's army, months after proving it. The draft's device (the pool takes crude; products stay Pacifica's) leaks: Tarakan's crude was burned in ships' boilers unrefined, and the Indies had their own refineries at Balikpapan and Palembang. The oil trust, which sells Japan its products under the annex (1600: "Japan gains cheap aluminium and oil"), loses its market and says nothing. *Fix:* decide who controls the pool's allocation (if the common Finance ministry, the lever survives and Japan's gain is price, not independence; say so), and give the trust and the Senate their objection. Also decide whether the bargain is published. If it is, the Dutch and British replies fall in 1931–32, inside the book and before the Thalassia conference (1746–1748), and cannot all be left to the partners' CR. If it is secret, list who knows; Australia signs its defence treaty in 1932 (1752) either knowing or not.

**S8. Ka Lōkahi: the stated reason is the author's, not the world's.** "What Japan gains in the south gets no seat … so what the Realm gains in the north gets none either" is not an argument anyone in 1931 would make: Japan gains concessions, not a crown. Canon supplies a better one. Hawaii and the Philippines each hold one vote in three "over the succession, the agreed matters … and disputes" (1234); a fourth, settler, vote would side with the Realm and end their ability to outvote it. They refuse it from interest. Put the refusal in the terms signed before the vote, because the settlers are entitled to know they get a crown without a voice, and note that the review of 1937 is where Caledonia will ask. Also resolve "the Hawaiian model" (N-12): Hawaii has a crown, a legislature, a count inside the Realm and a vote; Caledonia has the first three. And say whether Caledonia sends senators. Canon's Constitution "draws 124 counties" (914) with Alaska outside them; a Caledonia counted in the Realm either adds counties and senators (and changes the Senate that votes the Act) or is a second Alaska. The draft is silent.

**S9. Fact changes.**
- `seal-convention-signed`: the widened pattern still accepts "Britain for Canada", and no forbidden entry rejects it, so 1142's old wording passes the linter unchanged. Add a forbidden pattern as was done for the telegraph.
- Nothing catches "grants Canada, for nothing but the asking" (708; ledger C-1871-PAC-NAVIGATION), "Britain, for Canada, with Canadians doing the negotiating" (1142), "on Canadian soil where no finder's compact runs" (1137) or "then Canadian, soil" (731). At least the river grant and Bonanza Creek deserve forbidden patterns; they are the facts N-8 changes.
- "Japan renounces any southern advance" forbids a sentence canon never contained; harmless, but the message asserts the Davao lease as fact before Part H is settled.
- The event years depend on B1.

**S10. Canada's interest.** Canon holds Australia's crop guarantee back "while the imperial preference agreed at Ottawa holds it to London" (1752). Canada is that conference's host, and its prime minister in 1931 is R. B. Bennett, whose programme was imperial preference for wheat. The draft has him sign a ten-year contract with Thalassia and let the coast go without a word at Westminster. It can work (a wheat buyer in 1931 is worth more to him than any preference), but the draft must say it, and say who asked first: CR-082 R-1's rule is that "the Realm never asks first". Canada also loses the option London offered (union with Canada) and its only road to tidewater now runs through the Realm; a bonded quay "confirms what exists" and is no compensation for the change of landlord. Ottawa should extract something the draft has not thought of (a term on the quay that outlasts any government at Victoria; a seat for the prairies' pools in the contract's administration).

**S11. Population.** If Caledonia is counted in the Realm, canon's "about 37 million" for 1932 (41, 1203, 1670) changes by the model's figure for Caledonia. Not in Part C.

## Minor

- M1. Cross-references: the header and CR-082 cite the treaty as "A9"; it is A12–A13. B says "The Fraser is closed (A8)"; it is A9.
- M2. Part C's first list uses line numbers of 21 November. Current: 367→371, 727→731, 1124→1137, 1127→1142, 1365→1407, 1498→1554, 1561→1617, 1810→1881.
- M3. "Dominion" in canon means Canada (705, 731 "the Dominion's offices"). From 1907 the draft has a second one. The prose will need "the dominion at Victoria" or similar throughout.
- M4. The riot of September 1907 falls on Japanese and Chinese residents, some of them the Realm's citizens, two months before the Sovereign marries Chiyo and in the year Japan wins "civic equality for Japanese Pacificans" (1200). "Standing quarrel" is too little; history's Canada paid compensation through a commission. Say what Thalassia and Tokyo obtained.
- M5. The premier of 1931 was Simon Fraser Tolmie (1928–33), born at Victoria, son of a Hudson's Bay Company chief factor: a real person and an Islander leading a government whose Island votes no. Use him. Charles Camsell, born at Fort Liard and already registered (CT-CHARLES-CAMSELL), was Canada's deputy minister of mines in 1931 and is the natural Canadian on the Mackenzie commission.
- M6. Caledonia's drink law (OPEN): history's province ended prohibition in 1921 with government liquor stores, which is the Realm's Spirit Stores system. Line 1024 ("until British Columbia goes dry in 1917") then needs the stores' year.
- M7. "The dominion has its own law of 1927 against raising money for land claims": history's was Canada's Indian Act. A dominion of 300,000 settlers passing it on its own is plausible; mark it as an invention, not *Hist.*
- M8. "The Realm's delegation speaks for them" (A10) while the Realm's own delegation voted for the Concord "without the Senate's consent" in 1927 (1593): Caledonians inherit a delegation with that record. Worth a line in the Island's campaign.
- M9. The hundred Japanese "killed in land conflicts" at Davao in 1918–21 I could not confirm; the source named is the *Kasarinlan* article.

## The twelve proposed items

1. **The instrument of 1870 and Macdonald's protest.** Keep, with a motive. In 1869 history's Hudson's Bay post at Fort Yukon was found to stand in Alaska; here it stands on Pacifican soil, and the Yukon's only road is the Pacific's. That is London's reason to attach the basin to the Pacific colony. Fix the boundary (S3).
2. **London's imperial route as Canada's motive for the Canadian Pacific.** Keep. Add who builds the section on the colony's soil and on whose money; history's was Canada's government contract.
3. **The petitions of 1909 and 1913 acknowledged and filed.** Keep. It is the right kind of small shame. Let one petitioner live to Spences Bridge and say so.
4. **London's two courses, the legislature's third; the Act, dockyard and cable.** Change. London needs a price (S2). The Royal Navy left Esquimalt in 1905 in history, so the dockyard is either already the dominion's in 1931 or the draft must say why the navy stayed. The cable station at Bamfield is the dominions' line to Britain, and canon's cipher room has read "British official traffic in China and the Pacific" since 1925 (AGENTS.md, CR-081). London would not cede it; a lease to the cable's board, on the Realm's own rule that land is leased, is believable and better.
5. **The northern signing places; a nation that votes no.** Change both (S5).
6. **Ka Lōkahi's minute on the succession.** Keep, in the terms and with the Philippine delegation's price recorded at the same sitting (B3, S8).
7. **The name Caledonia.** Keep. Dropping "British" needs no argument in 1931; give the Island's no-voters the old name to hold on to.
8. **Downstream peoples' seats on the Mackenzie commission; Ottawa signs and does not contest.** Change. Canada in 1931 forbids its nations to hire a lawyer; it will not seat them on an international commission. The Realm seats them among its own commissioners or pays them directly, and Ottawa objects. That is a conflict worth having. On "does not contest", see S10.
9. **The debt refunded by the Patrimony's board against Crown-land rents.** Change. Inside the Realm the lender is the Patrimony's home arm, the Development Fund, and the security is the water's lease rent (B6). Crown-land rents presuppose that the land is the Crown's, which the compacts exist to deny.
10. **The surveys under the western waters commission; the canal in no speech.** Change the first, drop the second. The treaty's waters are listed (1142: "the Columbia, the Kootenay, the Okanagan, the Similkameen, the Skagit, the Pend Oreille, the Fraser's salmon and the Alaskan rivers of 1871"); the Peace and the Liard are not among them and run to Canada, which would not read a Pacifican survey of them as innocent. Let the dominion's own lands department and a railway survey to the Peace do the work, with Polytechnic men on contract. On the silence, B6.
11. **Crude to the pool, products to Pacifica until 1937; the bargain after the plot.** Keep the order (stick, then carrot; it is also the only order B1 allows). Change the crude-and-products split (S7).
12. **Zamboanga's footing.** Change (B2.3). If Zamboanga is to mean anything it is a second leased anchorage or a dockyard contract; "the right to berth" is a right the western squadron has.

## Literary effect

The Caledonia half has the makings of the book's best late chapter, and the draft keeps flinching from it. Every party "gives way", "signs and does not contest", "consents". London, Ottawa, Tokyo, Manila and the board each yield in one sentence. The author's rule is that every power acts from its own interest; here only the settlers do (A7's list is good), and their one real fear is answered with a clause that cannot mean what they think (B4).

The sufficers deserve better than a "last stand". They have three true arguments and the draft gives them one. The water is 5% of the need. The price is a Japanese station on Mindanao and a licence against a neutral neighbour whose crown hosted the Realm's peace (1374). And the Senate that refused Julian the Gulf on principle in the same year (1100) is asked to take a dominion whose terms preserve an entry rule. Victoria is alive, ninety-three, and a sufficer; the nations' senators are sufficers (1020) and are voting on other nations' water. Someone should lose this vote on the page.

Julian has no decision in the draft. He is offered a crown, consulted, advised. His decision is in B6: to tell the settlers what the water is for, or not. The second is Part H: he stops Japan's army with oil in the autumn and gives Japan's navy the south in the winter. That is a man choosing which Japan to strengthen, and the draft hands the thought to "the partners' CR". It should be here, because May 1932 follows: the officers' manifesto (1732) names the betrothal and the Bank, and after this CR it would also name the fourth crown and Davao's fence.

The Philippine crown is treated as furniture. It is the party with the most to lose and the only one given no voice.

Convenience: the dominion goes bankrupt in the year the Office's table says the margin closes, its creditor is its suitor, and its water has been surveyed for twenty years. One of these can be luck. The draft should make the other two somebody's work and somebody else's suspicion; the member at Victoria in the 1920s (T8) is the right instinct, one sentence too small.

From below: the candidates in D are right. Add a Japanese abaca planter at Davao reading that his colony has a fleet at the door and can buy no more land, and a Chinese Pacifican fish-buyer at Steveston who can vote on the day after the vote.

## History checked

**Confirmed**
- Stickeen Territories: created 19 July 1862, north to the 62nd parallel, east to the 125th meridian, under the colony's governor; dissolved July 1863, the part north of 60° returned to the North-Western Territory (Wikipedia, "Stickeen Territories"; Legislative Assembly of British Columbia, "1862 Stikine Territories"). Dawson lies near 64°, so the draft's "restores and extends" is mostly "extends".
- British Columbia's franchise: Chinese removed 1872, Japanese 1895, South Asians 1907; riots in Vancouver in 1907 (The Canadian Encyclopedia, "Anti-Asian Racism in Canada"). Also there: exclusion from metal mining, 1897.
- Davao: first thirty Japanese workers April 1903; over 1,000 by 1914; over 10,000 in 1929; about 20,000 in 1939 (SunStar Davao, "History of Japanese immigrants to Davao"). The draft's "more than ten thousand by about 1930" stands.
