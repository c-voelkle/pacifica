## A. Caledonia: From British Colony to Sovereign Republic (1858–1932)

In this file, **"the dominion at Victoria"** is British Columbia (with the Yukon) from 1907 to 1932. **"The Dominion"** alone is Canada, as in canon.

**A1. 1858.** The miners who go up the Fraser are Pacificans from San Francisco. Britain makes the mainland a colony, as in canon and in history (*Hist.:* about 30,000 miners on the Fraser in 1858).

**A2. 1869–71, the fork (`T4`; approved by the author, 21 November 2026, 19:45).** British Columbia does not enter Confederation. Pacifica does not encourage it to. It offers the cash-strapped colony a railway south to Puget Sound, a loan, and free entry for its coal, timber, and fish. Britain keeps the colony and Esquimalt. Pacifica asks for no flag and no sovereignty. Elias's governments hold to that until 1887, and the Regent's after them. Under Julian the rule holds because the Senate holds it: in 1932 he wants the coast and is refused (`A6`).
- **Pacifica's interest (PROPOSED):** Nanaimo's coal for the fleet and San Francisco, the Fraser's trade, and no second transcontinental power with a Pacific coast. This replaces canon's earlier motive for encouraging Confederation (*"a strong Canadian buffer"*).
- **London's interest (PROPOSED):** The colony costs London money. Pacifica's loan removes the cost, and Esquimalt keeps a hinterland London does not pay for.
- **Canada's line:** Canada stops at the colony's boundary—the continental divide, and north of the 54th parallel the 120th meridian. The Peace country east of the Rockies is therefore the colony's.
- **The Yukon is settled here (N-8, approved):** When London transfers the North-Western Territory to Canada on 15 July 1870 (*Hist.*), it reserves the country that history later made the Yukon Territory (which already includes the head of the Liard) and joins it to the colony.
  - *Hist.:* the Stickeen Territories (Order of 19 July 1862) ran north to the 62nd parallel under the colony's governor until the Act of 28 July 1863 returned the part north of 60° to the North-Western Territory. London here extends the 1862 precedent northward.
  - *London's motive (PROPOSED):* in 1869 Captain Charles Raymond fixed the Hudson's Bay Company's post at Fort Yukon in Alaska (*Hist.*). With Alaska Pacifican since 1867, the Pacific coast is the Yukon's cheapest road, though the Company keeps its Mackenzie route.
  - The spring 1871 navigation grant of the Stikine, the Yukon, and the Porcupine through Alaska is made to Britain **for the colony of British Columbia**, not for Canada (`T6`, `T7`).
  - **Ottawa's reaction (PROPOSED):** Macdonald protests the reservation and does not make it a quarrel with London in the year of the *Chicora* crisis and the Treaty of Washington.

**A3. The railways and the gold-dollar debt (`T5`, approved; details PROPOSED).**
- **Canada's motive:** Canada builds the Canadian Pacific Railway to the colony's boundary for the reason canon already gives (`L705 ("Grant's war is part of a plan")`): Grant's war and the *Chicora* incident proved the Union can cut the Dominion in two at the Sault canal.
- **The mountain section:** The colony builds its own section from the divide to Burrard Inlet on **gold-dollar bonds sold in San Francisco** and grants the Canadian Pacific running rights to Burrard Inlet in the 1880s. Pacifica's quiet money stays in the Canadian Pacific's finance through London houses (`T5`), and London adds an imperial mail subsidy for the Vancouver–Hong Kong liners.
  - *Canon note (`L708 ("In the spring of 1871 two treaties are made")`):* Because Ottawa never promises a railway to British Columbia as the price of Confederation, the scandal of 1873 over Allan's syndicate is removed from `L708`.
- **The Pacific Great Eastern and war debt (*Hist.*-grounded, debt size MODEL):** In 1912 the dominion at Victoria charters and guarantees the **Pacific Great Eastern Railway** northward into the Cariboo, financed in San Francisco gold dollars alongside London; after the private builders fail, Victoria takes the incomplete line over in 1918 (*Hist.:* 1912 charter and 1918 provincial takeover). Combined with the mountain-section bonds and Great War borrowing (1914–18), the dominion enters 1930 prosperous in output and trade, yet heavily indebted in **gold dollars payable in San Francisco** (sums to be sized in the finance workbook).
- **Canada's 1911 election:** With a customs line at the Rockies, Laurier still loses narrowly to Borden in 1911; the CPR, the Montreal banks, and the bonded wheat route through Burrard Inlet still pull the Prairies away from Union reciprocity.

**A4. 1896–98, the Klondike (`T7`; approved 23 November 2026, 23:14).** Bonanza Creek lies on the colony's Yukon soil, where no Pacifican finder's compact runs. Victoria governs the rush from Dawson, and for a decade Klondike royalties enrich the colony and make a separate western dominion fiscally viable. Nothing is taken from Canada in 1898. The boundary, the White Pass railway from Skagway (under the Chilkoot compact), and the assay offices stand as in canon. In the spring of 1897 Pacifica sells its old BC telegraph line (held by the telegraph company `BCCO`) to **British Columbia**, not Canada (`T7`).
- **Pattullo's route north (PROPOSED):** Because Ottawa's Commissioner Walsh does not govern Victoria's Yukon, **Thomas Dufferin "Duff" Pattullo** (born 1873 in Ontario) comes west to Victoria as a young journalist in the mid-1890s, enters the colony's lands and gold office, and serves at Dawson City from 1897 to 1902 before moving to Prince Rupert in 1908 (mayor in 1913, MLA and Minister of Lands from 1916, Opposition Leader from 1928; *Hist.* for post-1908 career).

**A5. 1900–30, the dominion at Victoria (`T6`).**
- **Dominion status (1907, PROPOSED):** British Columbia (with the Yukon) becomes a Dominion in 1907 alongside New Zealand and Newfoundland. *Hist.:* the Royal Navy vacated Esquimalt Dockyard on 1 March 1905; the dockyard is already the dominion's own from then.
- **Western treaties (`T6`):** The fur seal convention of 1886 (Britain for British Columbia), the western waters treaty of c. 1906, the **Columbia storage compact of 1922–25** (giving British Columbia half of the extra firm power its storage makes downstream, `L1554 ("a compact gives British Columbia half")`), and the **Halibut Treaty of 1923** are British Columbia's business. (PROPOSED addition to an approved compact:) At Pacifica's insistence the Columbia compact is also signed with the nations of the upper river.
  - *Canada's 1918 brigade (`L1407 ("Canada sends a brigade of 4,192 men")`):* embarks at Vancouver in CPR liners under transit rights, accompanied by a smaller volunteer contingent from the dominion at Victoria (PROPOSED).
  - *Drink (`L1024 ("until British Columbia goes dry in 1917")`):* prohibition runs from 1 October 1917 to 15 June 1921, when Victoria opens government liquor stores (*Hist.*), matching the Realm's Spirit Stores.
- **Unceded Indigenous lands:** The colony signs almost no treaties; because the Peace and Fort Nelson country lie west of Canada's boundary, Canada's Treaty 8 (1899–1900) never runs there. Petitions of 1909 and 1913 go to London and are copied to the Pacific Crown; Julian's secretary acknowledges and files them as addressed to another sovereign's ministers (PROPOSED). One of the interior chiefs who met at Spences Bridge in 1911 lives to sign there in 1932 (PROPOSED; the Nisga'a petition of 1913 came from the Nass, and its signers would not sign an Nlaka'pamux compact).
  - The **Allied Indian Tribes of British Columbia** form in 1916 (with James A. Teit as initial secretary, and by 1923–27 **Rev. Peter Kelly**, Haida, as chairman and **Andrew Paull**, Squamish, as secretary; *Hist.*). In 1927 Victoria passes its own statute barring the raising of money or hiring of counsel for land claims (an invention of this timeline mirroring Canada's historical 1927 Indian Act amendment). Unlike history, where the ban dissolved the organization, the Allied Tribes survive informally under Kelly and Paull to 1932 (PROPOSED).
- **Anti-Asian laws:** The colony strips the vote from Chinese residents in 1872, Japanese residents in 1895, and South Asian residents in 1907, and Vancouver riots on 7–9 September 1907 (*Hist.*), two months before Julian accedes and marries Chiyo. Because many victims are Realm citizens or Japanese subjects, Thalassia and Tokyo obtain a commission of inquiry and compensation (*Hist.:* Mackenzie King's commission paid $26,000 to the Chinese community and $9,000 to the Japanese). After Britain signs the **1919 Treaty of The Hague** carrying Japan's racial equality clause (`L1381 ("racial equality clause")`; PROPOSED in canon), Thalassia has a formal treaty ground for its standing quarrel with Victoria.
- **Who the settlers are by 1930:** Sixty years of rail and free trade south have settled Pacificans across the interior (Kootenays, Okanagan, Cariboo, Peace) and the Yukon. Most naturalized as British subjects to hold mining, timber, and homestead titles (PROPOSED). **Vancouver Island** (Victoria and Esquimalt) remains predominantly British and monarchist.
- **Suspicion before the crash (`T8`):** A member of the legislature at Victoria warns in the 1920s that San Francisco's banks already hold the dominion's mortgage (PROPOSED).

**A6. September 1931 – February 1932: The Monetary Divorce and Julian's Refused Leave.**
Britain's exit from gold is a rational defensive act, and British Columbia is a thriving dominion caught between incompatible monetary systems—not a failed state.
- **Why Britain leaves gold (21 September 1931):** Britain's 1925 return to gold rested on San Francisco credit (`L1566`), while Pacifica's falling industrial prices squeezed British exports (`L1562 ("get cheaper year after year")`). When Creditanstalt (May 1931) and Germany's banks (July 1931) fail (`L1687 ("Britain leaves gold in September")`), freezing London's continental bills, the Bank of England's reserves drain. Staying on gold would require unsecured San Francisco credit that hands Thalassia control of the Bank of England. Floating sterling down on 21 September 1931 lets London cut sterling export prices (`L1689 ("sterling-priced aluminium undercuts the Hilo smelter")`: sterling-priced aluminium immediately undercuts Hilo) and assemble a defensive **Sterling Area and Imperial Preference tariff wall** (Abnormal Importations Act Nov 1931; Import Duties Act Feb 1932; Ottawa Conference July 1932).
- **The map (author, 24 November 2026, 06:53 and 06:55).** The dominion's whole southern line, from the Strait of Georgia to the Rockies, runs against the Realm: Columbia, the Idaho country and Missoula County. Its north-west runs against Alaska. Its only other neighbour is Canada, across the mountains. The Union touches it nowhere. Missoula County reaches a few miles past the divide, to the crest of the Lewis Range, and meets Alberta at Waterton Lake (`CR-087` gives the line and its reason), so the Realm and Canada do touch, on a lake with no road or rail to it; the Union's border with Canada begins east of that. Two things follow, and both are used in 1932. A Caledonia outside the Empire can be reached by land from no power but the Realm and Canada, which is what makes the guarantee cheap to give and the perimeter quiet. And Canada has no usable land contact with the Realm at all, which is why Bennett needs the quay at Burrard.
- **The northern line (PROPOSED).** The railway to Prince Rupert is the dominion's own: chartered at Victoria in 1903, built with Grand Trunk money from the east end and the dominion's guarantee from the west, and fallen into the dominion's hands in 1919. Its bonds are part of the gold-dollar debt. Canada's trains reach Prince Rupert on it by running rights, which the Trilateral Treaty entrenches with the quay.
- **The Monetary Scissors at Victoria:** Sixty years of southern integration leave most of the dominion's trade (share **OPEN**; no workbook yet carries it), its Columbia downstream power income (`L1554`), its clearing, and its mountain-railway and PGE bonds, whose largest issue falls due on 1 December 1932 (date PROPOSED), in **Pacifica gold dollars**, while its Imperial exports (canned salmon, lumber, apples to Britain) earn **floating sterling**. When sterling drops in September 1931, Victoria's Imperial revenues fall sharply while its San Francisco debt holds par.
- **London's and Ottawa's rational refusals (British cabinet PROPOSED, grounded in *Hist.* National Government formed 24 August 1931):** Premier **Simon Fraser Tolmie** (born at Victoria, loyalist Islander Conservative, premier since 1928; local dominion career in this timeline, *Hist.* dates) asks London to guarantee a refinancing of Victoria's gold-dollar debt without forcing Victoria behind the new Imperial tariff wall. In Whitehall's National Government (MacDonald, Baldwin, and Chancellor **Neville Chamberlain**, with Bank of England Governor **Montagu Norman**; PROPOSED—canon has not yet named a 1932 British cabinet), the Treasury refuses on sound financial grounds: Britain will not spend its last gold reserves paying San Francisco bondholders for a dominion outside the Sterling Bloc. London offers sterling backing only if Victoria joins the Sterling Area and Imperial Preference, or merges with Canada. Prime Minister **R. B. Bennett** (*Hist.:* PM 1930–35), carrying Prairie relief and unsold wheat ahead of the Ottawa Conference, declines to assume Victoria's gold-dollar railway debt or import an anti-tariff Pacific electorate.
- **Why British Columbia breaks where Canada and New Zealand stay:** New Zealand sells about 80% of its exports to Britain in sterling (*Hist.*), and Australia's primary export trade likewise leans heavily on London; Ontario and Québec want Imperial Preference tariffs. British Columbia has no Ontario—an Imperial tariff wall against Pacifica would ruin its mainland economy and trigger default on its San Francisco bonds. Staying on the Pacifica gold dollar requires a full monetary, power-grid, and defense association incompatible with British Crown sovereignty while Britain builds a rival Sterling Bloc and pursues an atomic weapon (`L1614 ("fully committed from 1928")`; `CR-084` `B5` tells how that programme ends in May 1932).
- **The dominion asks first (late January – February 1932; the resignation is approved, item 83-3; the request for union is PROPOSED).** Tolmie resigns in late January 1932 rather than break with the King. Pattullo's mainland coalition opens talks with Thalassia, and its first request, pressed by the Vancouver and interior members, is the largest one: to enter Ka Lōkahi as a fourth crown under Julian.
- **Julian asks for leave, and the Senate refuses him a third time (February 1932).** It refused the Concord's treaty in 1927 (`L1593 ("refuses the treaty")`), when he applied it all the same, and it refused to shorten the Gulf's count in 1931 (`L1100`). He does not override it now. The act of 1927 was unlawful and was cured only by the Senate's consent and indemnity of 1928, on the Senate's conditions; and in July he will need two-thirds of this Senate five times (`CR-086` `E1`). A sovereign who took the coast against its vote in February would not get them. With Victoria's request on the table, Julian asks Pacifica's Senate in closed session for leave to offer a fourth crown, for the provisioner's reason he gave when he asked it to shorten the Gulf Republic's admission count (`L1100`). **The Senate refuses him leave.** Victoria Kamāmalu (opposing from Honolulu, where at ninety-three she has no vote, `L927 ("twelve Polytechnic seats and eight aliʻi seats")`, while gathering allies ahead of the Charter bargain of 1932, `L1738 ("one last bargain")`), the sufficers, the nations' senators and the majority that has just held the line on the Gulf's parity tests will not authorize union with a settler dominion that has anti-Asian laws and unceded Indigenous land. Hawaii's and the Philippines' delegations in Ka Lōkahi signal that they would refuse a fourth settler vote that ended their two-to-one majority over the Realm (`L1234 ("gives the Realm, Hawaii and the Philippines one vote each")`). Julian did not bid before he was asked; he is asked, wants to say yes, and is not allowed to.
- **The fallback: the Gulf Republic path.** Thalassia's answer to Pattullo is therefore the one offer Julian has authority to make, an **Association Compact on the Gulf Republic model (`C-1900-GULF-COMPACT`, `L1091–1093`)**: an independent **Republic of Caledonia** outside the crowns under hard upfront civic and Indigenous conditions and a **25-year outcome-based admission clause** (`A8`).

**A7. Spring 1932: The Referendum, Vancouver Island, and London's Bitter Exit.**
- **The Referendum (April 1932):** Taken across the dominion as a single indivisible electorate **on the dominion's own existing roll under the dominion's own law** (British subjects, including naturalized Pacifican settlers in the interior and Yukon; excluded Asian residents do not vote on the referendum itself and gain full constitutional floors on Proclamation Day, honouring the author's 16:57 decision). The ballot offers: (1) entering the Sterling/Imperial Preference system (or seeking terms from Canada), or (2) establishing the independent **Republic of Caledonia** on the published draft constitution and Association terms.
  - *Vote geography (PROPOSED):* **Vancouver Island votes decisively No**, denouncing the narrow mainland victory as a "foreigners' vote" of naturalized Pacificans. Vancouver and the Fraser canneries split nearly evenly (white labour divided over the 8-hour equal-wage article). The mainland interior (Kootenays, Okanagan, Cariboo, Peace), Prince Rupert, and the Yukon carry the Yes. Exact vote percentages are OPEN.
- **Why a No-voting Vancouver Island stays in:** (1) The referendum runs dominion-wide under one act; (2) London refuses to carry a small, isolated, indefensible island micro-colony (roughly a sixth of the dominion's population, **MODEL**, across a narrow strait from the mainland and dependent on mainland food, power, and trade); (3) Victoria's civil service and Esquimalt's dockyard live off the mainland treasury. In exchange for staying without secession, the Island extracts three entrenched guarantees: **Victoria remains the Republic's capital**, **Island provincial autonomy**, and **protected Crown pensions plus a two-year British nationality opt-in**.
- **London's bitter retrenchment (PROPOSED cabinet; one annuity schedule with `CR-084`):** Whitehall does not exit smiling. Beaverbrook and the Empire diehards denounce the National Government's *"Pacific Liquidation"* at Westminster for abandoning loyalist Vancouver Island and Britain's last North American Pacific coast. (Churchill's one moment in the season is `CR-084`'s.) Baldwin and Chamberlain carry the **Recognition / Severance Act (June 1932)** only because the Admiralty admits the coast is indefensible against an atomic Concord and the Exchequer needs relief on the war debt. The Act follows the request and consent of the legislature at Victoria, as the Statute of Westminster requires for a Dominion. Under `OPEN-019-05` Pacifica cancels a share of Britain's 1923 62-year annuities (`C-1923-UK-FUNDING`, `L1385 ("OPEN-019-05")`). `CR-084` `B1` owns the schedule: one schedule of 15 to 20 cancelled annuities (MODEL) in three dated tranches, and it owns the question of which years are cancelled. Caledonia's tranche is two annuities from the near end, those of 1932–33 and 1933–34 (author, 24 November 2026, 00:12), worth about $93m on the workbook's Story figures (MODEL). That is relief the Exchequer can show the House in the year it gives up the coast. The first tranche is this CR's and its trigger is the Severance Act. The Realm's chambers vote all three in one bill in July, so for a month Britain has released a dominion on the strength of an initialled accord. Pacifica also guarantees a long commercial lease on the **Bamfield Pacific Cable station** for **Imperial and International Communications Ltd.** (the company's ownership of the cable from 1930 is to be verified; the cable links to British Fanning at `L1139 ("British Fanning as the station")`). **A8. The Republic of Caledonia, Its Constitution, and the Admission Clause (July 1932).**
Proclaimed in the first days of July 1932 under President **Duff Pattullo**, the Republic takes the fur-traders' name (*New Caledonia*, dropping *British* and *New*; Island no-voters keep the old name in speech; PROPOSED). It is sovereign, outside the crowns and Ka Lōkahi, and its people are not counted in the Realm's ~37 million.
- **Upfront constitutional terms** (approved: the compacts as a condition, the repeal of the 1927 ban, the labour article, the end of racial disabilities, the examined civil service, the Office of Measures, the Fraser clause, audited accounts and debt-service limits, items 83-4 and 83-5; "strong executive" is PROPOSED):
  1. **Pacifica-modeled institutions:** Strong executive, **examined merit civil service**, independent **Caledonian Office of Measures**, and audited accounts and debt-service limits.
  2. **Immediate repeal of the 1927 land-claims ban & First Nations Title Recognition (A9):** Lease-not-sell title and an entrenched **Fraser River Salmon Clause** (no main-stem dam; reopenable only by the nations of the river).
  3. **Immediate 8-Hour Equal-Wage Labour Article:** Same wage for the same work and the eight-hour day on public and chartered works (`CR-071` pattern).
  4. **Floors for every resident on Proclamation Day (author, 16:57):** Abolishes the 1872, 1895, and 1907 disabilities in voting, licences, and the professions for **every person resident in Caledonia on the day of Proclamation**, including long-resident Chinese, Japanese, and South Asian residents previously barred from naturalization. *(Future entry from outside the Association is Caledonia's sovereign law, naming no race. Realm citizens have entry and residence by the Association Treaty, and Japanese subjects by the Concord's guarantee, `A10a`; both are reciprocal.)*
- **The Gulf-Style Admission Clause (PROPOSED, matching `C-1900-GULF-COMPACT`, `L1091–1093`):**
  - *Why 25 years of measured outcomes after Proclamation Day cures the statutes:* Repealing discriminatory statutes and the 1927 ban on paper does not undo sixty years of dispossession, exclusion, and unceded territory. As with the Gulf Republic (`L1091 ("equal civil rights")`), the Senate refuses to inherit unresolved land conflicts or a de facto underclass on paper promises alone.
  - *Three outcome tests* (measured each year by the Realm's Office of Public Measures together with the Caledonian Office, on Pacifica's yardstick; the tested party does not measure itself alone). The count is twenty-five consecutive years, as the Gulf's is (`L1093`; author, 24 November 2026, 00:12). A year in which a test fails ends the count and it begins again. The Senate that refused in 1931 to shorten the Gulf's count, because the tests are the Gulf's promise to its Black citizens, gives a settler dominion with unceded land no easier terms a year later; the nations' senators say so and are not answered.
    1. **Indigenous parity:** among First Nations people, infant mortality no higher than 1.05 times the Republic's general rate, and the measured health and literacy of youth no lower than 0.95 of it, as the Gulf's tests measure what happens to people; and no compact breach left unredressed in the joint audits of the nations and the Offices. A nation's refusal to lease or sign is its right under `A9` and is never counted against the Republic, so no nation becomes a veto and the Republic gains no motive to press one.
    2. **Resident civic parity in practice:** for residents of Asian origin, the rates of licences granted, titles registered, jury service and examination passes are no lower than 0.95 of the general rate, in the Republic as a whole and in each district where at least a thousand such residents live (threshold PROPOSED). Counting by origin is done for this audit only, under a constitution that otherwise names no race, and the Convention accepts that.
    3. **The general floor:** infant mortality, literacy, real wages, homicide and typhoid meet "the bar set by Pacifica's weakest former-territory state", as in the Gulf's compact, with revenue sufficient for the debt quota. A balanced budget in every year is not a test.
  - *What must accompany the tests, and is not counted among them:* gold parity, the AC standard, standard-gauge rail, leasehold and Crown-share concession law, open dockyard access for the common Navy and a foreign policy in common with the Concord's. These are met on Proclamation Day.
  - *Pacifica's discretion:* as with the Gulf, Pacifica may demand a second generation, and admission must add neither risk nor a veto.
  - *Treaty succession (PROPOSED):* the Republic succeeds the dominion in the seal convention of 1886, the western-waters treaty, the Columbia compact and the Halibut Treaty. The Association Treaty says so in one article, and the western-waters commission becomes a Pacifica–Caledonia water board.
  - *What admission means (PROPOSED):* If Caledonia ever completes the 25-year count and both peoples vote to unite (decades beyond 1932), Caledonia enters **the Realm as counties under the Constitution** (drawn by survey on the 1887 pattern, seated in both Senate and Chamber of Deputies, with the Yukon joining the northern territories)—**never as a fourth crown in Ka Lōkahi**. New counties would change the 124 and add senators, one a county; the nations' senators see that the dilution they feared in February returns in another form a generation on, and say so in the debate of `A10`.
- **Territory:** Victoria is capital; Vancouver is the commercial port; the **Yukon** is a self-governing territory with its council at Dawson; government liquor stores continue.

**A9. The First Nations Compacts of 1932 (the compacts as a condition are approved; the pace is decided, `T15`; the Commission, the places, the nations and the refusal are PROPOSED).**
- **Before the talks open (March 1932).** The legislature at Victoria repeals the statute of 1927, so that the nations can raise money and hire counsel before they are asked to sign anything. It names a **Treaty Commission**: members of both parties in the legislature and of the Convention, under a chairman Pattullo appoints. What the Commission initials binds nobody until the Republic ratifies it.
- **Kelly and Paull.** **Rev. Peter Kelly** and **Andrew Paull** of the surviving Allied Tribes are counsel and advisers at every table and sign only for their own nations. Paull signs for the Squamish at Burrard Inlet. Kelly's Haida do not treat in 1932, and Haida Gwaii stays under the standstill rule.
- **Who votes:** the referendum of April is taken on the dominion's roll, which excludes First Nations people as it excludes Asian residents. First Nations people are among "every resident" who gains the floors and the vote on Proclamation Day (item 83-5).
- **The nations' hand.** The Republic cannot be proclaimed on its own terms without compacts, and the treaties of July need ground the nations have never ceded. The nations know it. They do not sign in fourteen weeks what they have asked for since 1909. By Proclamation Day two compacts are ratified, Burrard and Spences Bridge. Burrard closes fast for a reason: the nations' hand is strongest in the weeks when Canada, the syndicates and the Canadian Pacific all need the inlet before July. Paull's side wins there what it could not win a year later: the Kitsilano and Capilano reserve lands restored, rent on the harbour works from Proclamation Day, and a seat on the harbour board (all PROPOSED). The railway and the district's promoters pay it. Musqueam signs with reservations it enters in the text. - **Standstill rule (`CR-070`/`CR-071`):** No land is granted and no dam, mine, or railway is built on a nation's unceded country without its compact. Signing nations lease rather than sell, take annual rents/power royalties, and hold published fishery remedies.
- **The rule binds the treaties of July (PROPOSED).** Every grant in the Association Treaty and the Trilateral Treaty, the chartered districts, the Alaska corridor, the power lines and Canada's quay, is made "subject to the compact of the nation whose ground it is". Works already built and in use, the Canadian Pacific's line among them, continue and pay rent to the nation from Proclamation Day until its compact fixes the terms. Nothing new is built before the compact. Burrard Inlet is Squamish, Musqueam and Tsleil-Waututh ground, so Canada's quay and the Burrard district wait on a compact there, and Paull sits across the table from the syndicates that want the district. Prince Rupert is Tsimshian ground; no compact is made there in 1932, and its district and Canada's transit shed wait.

  - **Spences Bridge:** Nlaka'pamux and southern interior nations (with a chief of the Spences Bridge meeting of 1911 signing on-page); Fraser salmon clause entrenched.

  - **Northern & Yukon nations:** Sekani (McLeod Lake, Finlay–Parsnip), Dane-zaa (Fort St John, to verify), Kaska (Lower Post / Dease River), Fort Nelson Dene (to verify), and Yukon First Nations (Dawson/Whitehorse corridor). *(Tahltan country on the Stikine around Telegraph Creek stays under the standstill rule.)*
- **A real refusal (PROPOSED):** The Sekani of McLeod Lake refuse a hydro lease that would flood the Finlay–Parsnip valley, and the standstill rule holds—just as the Chilkat pass stayed closed in Alaska (`L1137 ("their pass stays theirs and closed")`).

**A11. The Prairie Wheat Contract (December 1931) and the Trilateral Treaty (July 1932) (`CR-082` R-1; `T9`, `T14`).**
The contract and the treaty are separate, and the treaty does not mention the contract.
- **Instrument 1: the Patrimony board's prairie wheat contract (December 1931).**
  - *The seller.* Ottawa asks first (`CR-082`'s rule). By late 1931 the federal government has guaranteed the prairie pools' bank debts and taken over the sale of their unsold stocks (*Hist.*). The seller is the pools' **Central Selling Agency**, a commercial body under that guarantee, and not the government of Canada. The pools' growers gave up their five-year delivery contracts in 1931 (*Hist.*, from memory), so the Agency cannot pledge crops it does not own. It sells to deliver: each autumn it buys a million tonnes in the country elevators at the market and delivers at the band, and Ottawa's guarantee covers the difference in a year when the market is above the band. That is the risk Bennett takes, and his finance minister tells him so.
  - *The Patrimony's rule.* The Patrimony invests "never in a government in default or in treaty negotiation with Pacifica" (`L892`). The seller is not a government, and that is the first answer. The second is needed because Ottawa guarantees the seller: this draft reads the bar as applying on the day an investment is made (`T14`, decided). In December 1931 Canada is neither in default nor in treaty negotiation with Pacifica. No treaty conditions the contract, so it does not become an investment in a government when the treaty talks open in the spring.
  - *Not the granary.* The Crown's granary buys abroad only "for beans and rice" (`L1695`), and that sentence stands as written. The board is not the granary. It buys for return and resells; nothing it buys enters the Crown's stores.
  - *The terms.* A forward purchase of ten harvests, 1932–41, of 1.0 million tonnes a harvest inside a band of 0.8 to 1.2 million (author, 24 November 2026, 00:12; MODEL), at a price band the workbook has still to set. That is about 18% of what Canada sold abroad in 1931–32. Payment is a gold-dollar balance in San Francisco that the Agency owns, credited against each delivery; it is not a loan. Carriage is in Pacifican ships out of Burrard Inlet. The contract is unconditional and carries no tariff or treaty term.
  - *The board's reason.* The board "publishes the return it aims to beat". Its case is a ten-year position bought at the slump's price in a crop Asia's cities will need more of each year, resold to Japan and China in place of Canada's distressed sales. The contract takes new harvests. The Agency's unsold stock of 1929–31 stays the Agency's, and the board's one promise about it is to bid for none of it below the band. The Realm's own wheat goes on being sold until its margin closes about 1940–42 (`CR-082`). The share of Canada's sales the contract replaces is OPEN for the workbook.
  - *Giannini.* He and the Industrial Board object in the Senate. His real case is about the market and not about charity: the board will resell prairie wheat to the same buyers in Japan and China who take the Realm's own wheat, which is sold abroad until about 1940–42, and the home grower pays for the board's return. The board's million tonnes are a third of the three million the Realm itself sells abroad in 1932 (MODEL). The board answers that Canada's distressed sales were already in that market at worse prices and that it has taken them out of it. He loses because nobody can instruct the board: canon has "the board, which the Crown cannot direct" (`L1695`), and the drafter takes it that the Senate cannot either (PROPOSED). He says so. The granary has already bought "the home counties' wheat and feed first" (`L1695`); that is canon's rule and no concession to him.
- **Instrument 2: the Trilateral Treaty of Thalassia (mid-July 1932).**
  A treaty of the Concord, signed by the common Foreign minister, by Pattullo for Caledonia and for Canada by Bennett's plenipotentiary. Three governments sign, the Concord as one party, which is the name. It is the Concord's and not the Realm's because its quota opens Canada's tariff to both partners' champions, its ore feeds stations in both, and the quay stands beside the guarantee's naval clauses. The consequence is that it goes to the delegations and not to the Senate. Tate's bloc votes against it among the Realm's sixty delegates, says that a quota for machines was moved out of the Senate's reach on purpose, and loses. It is Canada's first treaty with Pacifica in its own name (`T6`).
  1. **Pacific tidewater for Canada:** perpetual running rights for the Canadian Pacific across Caledonia and a bonded Canadian quay on Burrard Inlet, with transit at Prince Rupert, entrenched so that no government at Victoria can revoke them.
