## A. Caledonia: British Columbia and the Yukon change crowns (N-1 to N-5, N-8 to N-12)

In this file "the dominion at Victoria" is British Columbia from 1907. "The Dominion" alone is Canada, as in canon.

**A1. 1858.** The miners who go up the Fraser are Pacificans from San Francisco. Britain makes the mainland a colony, as in canon and in history.

**A2. 1869–71, the fork (N-1, N-8).** British Columbia does not enter Confederation. Pacifica does not encourage it to. It offers the bankrupt colony a railway south to Puget Sound, a loan, and free entry for its coal and fish. Britain keeps the colony and Esquimalt. Pacifica asks for nothing more. Elias's governments hold to that until 1887, and the Regent's and Julian's after them.
- **Pacifica's interest (PROPOSED).** Nanaimo's coal for the fleet and for San Francisco; the Fraser's trade; and no second transcontinental power with a Pacific coast. This replaces canon's reason for encouraging Confederation, the Canadian buffer.
- **London's interest (PROPOSED).** The colony costs London money. Pacifica's loan removes the cost, and Esquimalt keeps a hinterland London does not pay for.
- **Canada's line.** Canada stops at the colony's boundary: the continental divide, and north of the fifty-fourth parallel the 120th meridian. The Peace country east of the Rockies is therefore the colony's.
- **The Yukon is settled here (N-8, approved).** When London transfers the North-Western Territory to Canada in July 1870, it keeps back the country that history made the Yukon Territory in 1898, with the head of the Liard, and joins it to the colony.
  - *Hist.:* the Stickeen Territories of 1862 ran to the sixty-second parallel under the colony's governor, and the Act of 28 July 1863 gave the colony only the part south of the sixtieth. London here extends the arrangement of 1862.
  - *London's reason (PROPOSED).* *Hist.:* in 1869 the Hudson's Bay Company's post at Fort Yukon was found to stand in Alaska. Alaska is Pacifican from 1867, so the Yukon's only road is the Pacific's.
  - The instrument and its date are PROPOSED.
- The grant of the Stikine, the Yukon and the Porcupine in the spring of 1871 is then made to Britain for the colony.
- **Ottawa's decision.** Macdonald protests the reservation and does not make it a quarrel with London in the year of the Washington treaty (PROPOSED).

**A3. The Canadian Pacific (T5, case B, approved).** Pacifica's quiet money stays in the line, as in canon. The line reaches Burrard Inlet in the 1880s on running rights over the colony's soil.
- **Canada's motive (PROPOSED).** London wants an imperial route to Esquimalt and Hong Kong and pays a mail subsidy for it. Pacifica's money rides on that route. Who builds the section on the colony's soil, and on whose contract, is OPEN.
- British Columbia outside Confederation puts a customs line at the boundary. The election of 1911 has to account for it.

**A4. 1896–98.** The strike on Bonanza Creek is on the colony's soil. The colony governs the rush from Victoria, and for a decade the royalty makes it rich. Nothing is taken from Canada in 1898. The boundary, the railway from Skagway and the assay offices stay as in canon. In 1897 Pacifica sells the old telegraph line to British Columbia, not to Canada (T7).

**A5. 1900–30.** The colony signs no treaties with the nations. South of the line their kin lease dam sites and keep their valleys.
- **The petitions of 1909 and 1913** go to London and also to the Pacific crown. Julian's secretary acknowledges each one, answers that it is addressed to another sovereign's ministers, and files it. Nothing is done on them before 1931 (PROPOSED). One petitioner lives to sign at Spences Bridge.
- The western waters treaty of about 1906, the Columbia compact of 1922–25 and the Halibut Treaty of 1923 are British Columbia's business (T6). At Pacifica's insistence the Columbia compact is also signed with the nations of the upper river.
- British Columbia becomes a dominion in 1907, with Newfoundland (date PROPOSED). *Hist.:* the Royal Navy left Esquimalt in 1905; the dockyard is the dominion's own from then.
- **The laws against Asians.** The colony takes the vote from its Chinese residents in 1872, from its Japanese residents in 1895 and from its South Asian residents in 1907, and Vancouver riots against them on 7 September 1907. *Hist.:* all four.
  - Some of those attacked are the Realm's citizens. Thalassia and Tokyo obtain a commission and compensation (PROPOSED). *Hist.:* Canada paid compensation after a commission of inquiry.
  - From 1907 these laws are the standing quarrel between the dominion at Victoria and Thalassia. Julian accedes and marries Chiyo two months after the riot.
- **Who the settlers are by 1931.** Sixty years of the railway south and free entry have settled Pacificans in the interior: the Kootenays, the Okanagan, the Cariboo and the Peace. Asian Pacificans are among them (PROPOSED; to be modelled in Part E).
- **T8.** A member of the legislature at Victoria says in the 1920s that the Realm counts the dominion's acres already. The storyline gives him the sentence, and gives his suspicion a cause: the Realm's banks hold the dominion's bonds (PROPOSED).

**A6. 1929–31: the offer.** Britain's retreat is complete in 1927. The slump of 1929 breaks the finances of the dominion at Victoria.
- **The creditor.** The dominion's bonds are payable in gold dollars and were sold in San Francisco.
- **The rescue that fails (PROPOSED).** Early in 1930 the Realm's banks offer to refund the debt without any union. London refuses to guarantee a dominion refinanced from San Francisco, and offers two courses: government by commission, or union with Canada.
- **The legislature's decision.** In the autumn of 1930 the legislature at Victoria adds a third course and offers the Pacific crown (the author, 22:27: the offer can come earlier; the season is PROPOSED). The premier is Simon Fraser Tolmie, an Islander (PROPOSED). *Hist.:* Tolmie was premier from 1928 to 1933, born at Victoria.
- **London's decision.** London resists for a year. Britain leaves gold in September and cannot carry the dominion's bonds. London's price for its Act is its own debt: an instalment of its gold-dollar debt to Pacifica is remitted (PROPOSED; canon's OPEN-019-05 concerns payment in kind for that debt).
- The imperial cable's station at Bamfield is leased to the cable's board and is not ceded (PROPOSED; *Hist.* for Bamfield's ownership in 1931 to verify).

**The calendar (PROPOSED; it answers the review's B1).** Only the plebiscite's year is the author's (N-2).

**A7. The terms and the settlers' vote (N-2, N-10).** The order is terms first, compacts second, vote third, as it was for the Philippine crown in 1907.
- **The terms of union** are signed and published before the vote:
  1. **The entry rule (author, 21:47).** Caledonia keeps its own rule on entry from outside the crowns and the Concord. The rule names no race. It cannot bar a citizen of the Realm, nor a Japanese subject entitled under the terms of 1907, which run in Caledonia from the day of union.
  2. **The floors (author, 16:57).** They hold for every person resident on the day of union. The dominion's disabilities of race in voting, licences and the professions end on that day.
  3. **The labour article.** The same wage for the same work, and the eight-hour day on public works, as in the Realm since 1887. This is the settlers' protection against undercut wages, which is what Vancouver's white labour organised against (author, 21:47).
  4. The legislature stays at Victoria.
  5. The Fraser clause (A9).
  6. The northern water and its rent (Part B).
  7. Caledonia has no vote in Ka Lōkahi (A10).
- **The settlers' vote (N-10, approved).** It is taken in November 1931 on the dominion's own roll, under the dominion's own law. The excluded Asian residents do not vote and gain the floors on the day of union (author, 16:57). The settlers vote for the union narrowly.
  - *Their reasons are their own interest:* the dominion is bankrupt; the other courses are rule by commission or rule from Ottawa; sixty years of rail and trade run south; the water's rent refunds the debt (Part B); the labour article ends the undercutting.
  - *Vancouver Island votes no* (approved). It has the capital and the old naval town, and it votes for Britain.
  - *PROPOSED:* Vancouver and the canneries of the Fraser split nearly evenly, and the interior carries the vote. Union with Canada is on the ballot paper and comes third. The Canadian Pacific, the largest interest in Vancouver and partly Pacifican money, campaigns for neither course.
  - The figures are not set (OPEN).

**A8. The compacts (N-3; T3, case A, approved).** They follow the mainland rule of CR-071: a nation leases and does not sell. The Lands and Waters Charter of 1932 is their island counterpart a year later, and Julian accepts a limit in each.
- **What a nation decides (PROPOSED).** Each nation ratifies or refuses its own compact. Britain leaves either way, so no nation votes on the crown. Where there is no compact, CR-070's rule holds: no land is granted and no work is built on a nation's country without its compact.
- **Who signs in 1931.** The lessor nations of the northern water, the nations whose country the canal crosses, the nations of the Fraser and the nations of the upper Columbia, whose reach the water enters. The rest follow, under the same standstill rule.
- **Places (PROPOSED; names to verify).**
  - Spences Bridge, for the southern interior (approved, N-3).
  - Fort St James, the old capital of the fur traders' New Caledonia, for the Dakelh and the Secwépemc of the canal's line.
  - In the north each nation signs in its own country: the Sekani of the Finlay and the Parsnip at McLeod Lake, whose valley the Peace reservoir floods; the Dane-zaa at Fort St John; the Kaska at Lower Post; the Dene of Fort Nelson; the Tahltan of the Dease.
- The dominion at Victoria has its own law of 1927 against raising money for land claims. The terms repeal it. This law is an invention of this history; history's was Canada's.

**A9. The Fraser clause (N-4).** It is the one entrenched term of the union. No dam is built on the main stem, and no water leaves the basin. Only the nations of the river can reopen it.

**A10. Who accepts the crown (N-9).** The legislature at Victoria offers it and the vote confirms it.
- **The Senate** passes the Act of Union in December 1931, narrowly. The count is not set (OPEN). The sufficers lose, and they lose with three arguments on the page (PROPOSED):
  1. The water at its ceiling meets about a twentieth of the century's need (Part B).
  2. The price is a Japanese station on Mindanao and pressure on a neutral neighbour whose crown hosted the peace of 1919.
  3. The Senate refused Julian the Gulf's shorter count on principle in the same year.
  - Victoria, at ninety-three, and the nations' senators, who are voting on other nations' water, are among them. Who speaks is OPEN.
- **Ka Lōkahi (N-9 as amended; author, 16:57).** Caledonia gets no vote. The council keeps its three: the Realm, Hawaii and the Philippines.
  - *The reason inside the story (PROPOSED).* Hawaii and the Philippines can together outvote the Realm. A fourth, settler, vote would side with the Realm and end that. They refuse it from interest.
  - The refusal is a term of union (A7), so the settlers know they receive a crown without a voice. Caledonia will ask again at the review of 1937.
  - At an extraordinary sitting in 1931 the three delegations record that the Crown of Caledonia follows the Realm's succession, and the Philippine delegation records the price of the Davao lease (J-3).
- **Japan** consents at the Hayama Council of 1931 (Part H).

**A11. The Crown of Caledonia (N-3, N-12).** Julian wears it with the crowns of Pacifica, Hawaii and the Philippines. His four crowns are one party in the Concord. Three of them vote in Ka Lōkahi.
- **Status (N-12, approved).** Caledonia's people are citizens of the Realm and are counted in its population from 1932. Caledonia has what Hawaii has, a crown, a legislature and a place inside the Realm's count, without Hawaii's vote in Ka Lōkahi.
- **The Senate (author, 22:27).** Caledonia stays outside the 124 counties and sends a small fixed number of senators from 1932. It therefore has no vote on its own Act of Union. The number is OPEN.
- **The name.** The legislature takes the fur traders' name for the country, New Caledonia, and drops "British" and "New" (PROPOSED). The Island's no-voters keep the old name in use.
- The Yukon is a territory of the crown, with its council at Dawson. Alaska stays under the Northern Commission.
- **Drink (PROPOSED).** *Hist.:* the province ended prohibition in 1921 with government liquor stores. Caledonia's stores are the Realm's Spirit Stores in all but name and join them.

**A12. The treaty with Canada** (December 1931; no share of the Columbia).
- **Who asks first.** Ottawa does. Its wheat is unsold, and the Realm's rule is never to ask first (CR-082). The prime minister is R. B. Bennett, whose programme is imperial preference for wheat. A buyer in 1931 is worth more to him than a preference not yet agreed (PROPOSED). *Hist.:* Bennett held office from 1930 to 1935.
- **The treaty's articles:**
  1. The railway's running rights to tidewater and a bonded Canadian quay on Burrard Inlet, on a term no government at Victoria can end (the entrenchment is PROPOSED, and it is what Ottawa extracts).
  2. It records the board's wheat contract (A13) and gives it the quay and the running rights. The Crown does not promise the purchase.
  3. A Mackenzie waters commission. The Peace and the Liard run east into Canada. Canada is paid for the water it loses, and for low water on the Slave, which is also the Works' ore road from Great Bear.
  4. The boundary is declared settled.
- **The downstream peoples (PROPOSED).** Canada will not seat its own nations on an international commission. The Realm seats spokesmen of the lower Peace, the Slave and the Mackenzie among its own commissioners, and Ottawa objects. Charles Camsell, born at Fort Liard and already in the registry, is Canada's commissioner (PROPOSED).
- **Ottawa's decision.** Ottawa signs and does not contest the vote at Westminster. It is the first treaty Canada signs with Pacifica on its own (T6).
- The Realm's line that it takes no people who have not voted is amended to what is true: no land is taken from a nation without its compact, and no settlers without their vote.
- **T8, case A, approved.** "The giant its neighbours need not fear" stands for the years to 1931. After 1931 Ottawa has reason to count the Realm's acres, and the storyline says so.

**A13. The wheat contract (N-11, approved; CR-082 R-1).** It is a forward purchase by the Patrimony's board, not Treasury spending and not printed money. Canon: the board is one "which the Crown cannot direct". So there are two instruments (the review's B5).
- **The board's contract comes first.** The board votes it as an investment, with the prairie pools' selling agency (*Hist.* for the agency in 1931 to verify).
  - It buys forward for ten harvests from 1932 inside a price band, not at a fixed floor (PROPOSED; the band, the tonnage and the credit are sized in the workbook and are OPEN).
  - It pays in the Realm's machines, the Columbia's ammonia and carriage in the Realm's ships through the bonded quay, so the money comes back as orders to plants the slump has half idled. This is canon's own argument for the Crown's granary.
- **The treaty** then records the contract (A12).
- **What becomes of the wheat.** The Realm grows more than it eats until about 1941–43 (CR-082, MODEL). Until then the wheat is stored or resold to Japan and China (approved).
- **The argument in the Senate.** Giannini and the Industrial Board object that the Realm's own foreclosed farmers get no floor in the winter the Bank will not print.
  - Giannini wins something (PROPOSED): the board enlarges its purchase of the home crop before it votes the prairie contract.
  - The board's money is rent, not tax, and it has invested abroad since 1919.
- The cabinet's further reason is in its minute and in no speech: the contract makes the Realm the prairie's buyer and carrier, a step toward the aim of CR-082.
- **The dominion's debt.** The Development Fund, the Patrimony's home arm, refunds it once Caledonia is inside the Realm. The security is Caledonia's share of the water's rent (Part B; PROPOSED, to be sized).

*Hist.:* about 30,000 miners on the Fraser in 1858; the annexation petition of 1869; entry into Confederation on 20 July 1871 and the threats to leave in 1874 and 1878; the transfer of the North-Western Territory on 15 July 1870; almost no treaties in the province, the Nisga'a petition of 1913 unheard, and land-claim funds made an offence in 1927; the Yukon's 27,219 people in 1901 and 4,157 in 1921; Western Australia's vote of 1933 refused at Westminster; Newfoundland's crisis of 1932 and commission of 1934.

## B. The northern water (P-2, N-6, N-7)

- **Approved (author, 21 November, 19:29):** Julian's engineers propose it and prevail on it, because it is critical to national survival.
- **Published (author, 22 November, 21:47).** The water is in the terms of union and in the campaign. Nothing about it is hidden from the voters.
  - It is the bankrupt dominion's one asset large enough to refund its debt, and that is a reason for the settlers' yes (A7).
  - What stays in the cabinet's minute is only the aim of CR-082.
  - **Julian's decision.** He tells the settlers what the water is for before they vote.
- **Two rents (PROPOSED).** Each lessor nation receives a lease rent under its compact. Caledonia's treasury receives a wayleave for the canal and the power works on its soil. The wayleave secures the refunding (A13). The split is OPEN.
- **Dates.** Surveyed from the 1910s. Proposed with the terms of 1931. Leased under the compacts of 1931 (A8; T3). Built in stages after 1932.
- **The surveys (PROPOSED).** The dominion's own lands department and a railway survey to the Peace do the work, with Polytechnic men on contract. The western waters commission has no part: its waters are listed in canon, and the Peace and the Liard are not among them.
- **Size (author, 21 November, 20:32).** The first stage is about 20 km³ a year, which keeps each river's loss near a fifth. The ceiling in law is 45 km³. *Hist.:* the Liard at Fort Liard carries about 59 km³ a year.
- **Source.** The Peace and the Liard. The Fraser is closed (A9). The Yukon, the Stikine and the Skeena are salmon rivers and fall under canon's rule.
  - The Peace and the Liard carry no Pacific salmon. *Hist.:* a small run of chum from the Mackenzie was recorded in the Liard in 1979–80. The compacts cover it in one clause (PROPOSED).
- **What 45 km³ waters.** 36.5 million acre-feet: 10–12 million acres at 3.0–3.5 acre-feet an acre, and about 26 million mouths at the century's yields in CR-082's century module (MODEL), about a twentieth of the need. **This is not a result until the fields are named.**
- **The route.** The canal crosses the upper Fraser's basin and takes nothing from it (A9). The water enters the Columbia in the reach of the upper river's nations, who are party to the compacts (A8). It then runs through every compact reach of CR-055 below, and the peoples of each reach have their say under those compacts on fish passage, flood and temperature (PROPOSED).
- **Open engineering, found by the review and not yet answered:**
  1. **Energy.** The lift at the ceiling takes 4.8–7.2 GW as a year-round average, and the first stage 2.1–3.2 GW. Canon's Realm generates about 11 GW on average in 1929. A dam in the Peace's canyon does not feed the pumps. The sum must be redone on sourced elevations, pass by pass.
  2. **The fields.** Ten to twelve million acres do not exist on the Columbia Plateau, and canon forbids the Grand Coulee. Fields further south need lifts that are not in the energy sum.
  3. **The Columbia below.** Twenty to 45 km³ added to a river of roughly 90 km³ at the line changes every reach.
- **Limits.** The reservoirs stop where the lessor nations set the waterline. Every irrigated acre has a drain.
- **The larger plan.** The engineers draw the plan at about 135 km³, with four more rivers. The compacts refuse it. *Hist.:* the Parsons plan of 1964.

## H. The Hayama bargain of 1931 (J-1 to J-6)

This part follows B. Its letter is H because Parts C to G keep the letters other files cite.

**The Concord as canon has it.** Foreign Affairs, the common Navy and their Finance belong to the Concord. The executive is entrusted to the Crown of Pacifica. Convention gives Japan the Navy ministry, and Foreign Affairs and Finance are normally Pacifican. The western squadron is reserved to the Japanese naval party until the renewal of 1932. Japan therefore has no foreign policy and no fleet apart from the Concord's, and the bargain is written in those terms.

- **J-1 (author, 15:43).** Japan is consulted on the fourth crown, and the bargain is symmetrical: the Realm goes north, Japan goes south.
- **J-0. Japan's wish is older than the bargain (author, 22:27).** From 1927 the Concord is the world's only atomic power, and Japan has every reason to want the Indies from the start. The slump and Britain's troubles then give the opportunity.
  - PROPOSED: Japan's delegation first asks for a course toward the Indies at the first Hayama Council in 1928. Julian defers it for three years. At the Council of 1930, when the legislature at Victoria has offered the crown, Japan names the Indies as its price.
  - The army's plot of 1931 then has a second motive beside Manchuria's land: the navy is about to be given its south (PROPOSED).
- **J-2. The resolution on the Netherlands Indies (author, 16:40, 21:47 and 22:13: rung B).** In the autumn of 1931, after the Manchurian plot is stopped, the Hayama Council resolves, and publishes, that the common ministries will seek for Japanese houses, by a treaty the Dutch sign and ratify:
  1. a guaranteed yearly tonnage of Indies crude at a fixed price;
  2. exploration and production rights, first in east Borneo;
  3. the same for bauxite, nickel, tin and rubber;
  4. a larger share of the Indies' import trade for Japanese goods, and Japanese lines in the inter-island carrying trade;
  5. entry for Japanese firms and their staff;
  6. the lease of one or two oil ports, with Japanese guards inside the leased ground (PROPOSED: Tarakan and Balikpapan).
  - No territory is ceded. There is no protectorate and no annexation.
  - *Hist.:* in 1940–41 Japan's missions to Batavia asked for much the same: oil exports raised from 570,000 tons to 3.75 million, about half the Indies' output, and oil areas for Japanese companies to work.
  - **Julian owns the policy.** The fleet that shows itself off Batavia is the common fleet under his executive, and the Foreign minister who negotiates is Pacifican. The Realm's delegation undertakes not to block the policy. *The limit he sets (PROPOSED):* the fleet may show itself; it does not fire, blockade or land except on ground the Dutch have leased.
  - **Julian's second decision.** He stops Japan's army with oil in the early autumn and gives Japan's navy the south a few weeks later. He is choosing which Japan to strengthen.
- **J-3. The Davao lease and the Cortes (author, 16:57 and 21:47).** The Philippine crown is the lessor, and its Cortes votes the lease. The vote is narrow. Those who would separate the Philippine crown from Julian's vote against, and say that Manila pays with its land for the Realm's northern crown.
  - **The Cortes's price:**
    1. No Japanese land title in Davao is added to those registered on the day of the lease.
    2. The rent goes to Manila.
    3. Philippine law and Philippine courts run outside the station's fence, over the settlers too.
