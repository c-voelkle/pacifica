Second fresh-context review of CR-084, revision 3, 23 November 2026. The reviewer read the draft, AGENTS.md and current canon, and not the writer's notes or earlier reviews.

Line numbers are raw lines of `book-agent/revision/master_storyline.md`. History marked "(from memory)" was not verified in this pass.

## Verdict

Ready after fixes, not ready to integrate as it stands. The approved shape (M-1 to M-4) is sound and most of it survives. The draft fails in three places: it gives votes and voices to bodies that have none in canon (Houston and Ka Lōkahi in the Realm's Senate, a ratification rule canon does not state, Victoria leading an opposition that contradicts her own school); it leaves Brunei's government and the order of consents unsettled (Brunei and Tonga sign while Britain still conducts their foreign relations; the British Resident is never mentioned); and its list of islands to be transferred does not match either canon or the 1932 map. Pacifica's own gain is asserted more than shown, and Japan's price is certain while its gain is prospective and partly contradicts canon's 1932 renewal.

## Blockers

**B1. The Senate scene gives seats to people who have none, and casts Victoria against her own doctrine.**
*Passage:* A3; B3.4 ("Houston objects in the Senate and wins a concrete amendment"); Calendar, late May–June; set piece 1.
*Problem:* (a) The Gulf Republic is a sovereign state outside membership (L1756: "The Gulf remains a sovereign ally and protégé outside membership"); Houston is its district (L1681: "the Gulf Republic's district on the Houston channel"). It has no senator. "Gulf allies" and "Ka Lōkahi" cannot "extract binding conditions" in a Senate vote either: Ka Lōkahi is the council of the crowns (L1234), not a bloc in the Realm's Senate. (b) Victoria is not a senator, and canon has already spent her 1932: L1738, "At ninety-three or ninety-four Victoria retains the independent standing for one last bargain with the son whose methods she condemned", is the Lands and Waters Charter. A second bargain in the same months contradicts "one last". (c) The argument put in the sufficers' mouths is not theirs. L1020 defines them by mouths and selection ("rich and few"), and L1560 says of a comparable case: "The sufficers would write the loan down as the price of the Strait; the provisioners convert it." A treaty that adds no mouths, takes no people and writes down a loan for sea room is close to the sufficers' own position. The savers-and-foreclosures argument belongs in canon to Giannini and the Industrial Board (L1695: "Giannini answers in the Senate that the money should go to the mortgages") and to Tate's county bloc (L1593: "foreign coupon-clippers"). (d) Keawe "joins the hydro board" (L1738); canon gives him no Senate seat.
*Fix:* Recast the opposition. Giannini, the Industrial Board's senators and the county bloc oppose cancelling gold annuities in a foreclosure winter. The Navy's provisioners oppose it from the other side: they wanted the base rights L1385 floated, and get a halt and a harbour whose command is Japanese. The sufficers (the aliʻi seats of L927 can speak for the Hawaiian court) support it on conditions: no people taken, no guarantee that polices a colony. Victoria is not in the scene; if she must touch it, one line that she declines to. The Gulf's price is made by the Gulf's government in the compact negotiation of 1932 (see the PROPOSED items), not in the Senate.

**B2. A1 states ratification rules canon does not contain and cites lines that do not say them.**
*Passage:* A1 table.
*Problem:* L1604 records one event: "In 1928 she wins two-thirds Senate consent and an Act of Indemnity", for the Concord itself. Canon states no general rule that treaties need two-thirds of the Senate; I found none in the constitutional passages (L916–927) or elsewhere. The citation is correct as to the line and wrong as to what it proves. Second, canon gives the Concord its own ratifying bodies, which the table ignores: L1604 ("Pacifica's delegates chosen half by each chamber") and L1734 ("ratified by both crowns and delegations"). A Concord treaty ratified by "Pacifica's Senate (two-thirds) + Japan's Privy Council/Diet" bypasses them. Third, L1597 gives Japan's prime minister a countersignature on the appointment of the three common ministers ("Julian appoints the three common ministers with Japan's prime minister countersigning"), not on treaties. Fourth, L1234 gives Ka Lōkahi "the succession, the agreed matters renewed every ten years and disputes between them", with reviews in 1917 and 1927; an "extraordinary session" is a new power (docs/authority-and-scope.md R3: a new right is a new premise). Fifth, cancelling a claim of the Realm's Treasury is a money matter; canon does not say the Chamber of Deputies has no part.
*Fix:* Either (i) use what canon has: Concord instruments are made by the common Foreign minister and ratified "by both crowns and delegations"; the Realm's Senate and Chamber vote only the Realm's own acts (the cancellation of `C-1923-UK-FUNDING` and the Realm's separate signature), by whatever majority the author sets; or (ii) raise the two-thirds treaty rule, the prime minister's countersignature on treaties and Ka Lōkahi's extraordinary sitting as canon tensions with both cases, as AGENTS.md requires. The atolls can also wait for nothing: Ka Lōkahi can take them at a sitting the three delegations call by agreement, if the draft says that is new.

**B3. The "sugar protection wall" contradicts the slump policy and is given to the wrong body.**
*Passage:* A3 condition 4; B3.5; B4; C2.
*Problem:* L1693: "Pacifica raises neither tariffs nor taxes; reserves and borrowing fund the response." A new wall in 1932 is a tariff or quota raised in the slump. And customs are not Ka Lōkahi's or the Cortes's to set: L1597, "Customs, gold parity ... are agreed matters reviewed at every renewal" of the Concord. Japan's home market, which Formosa supplies "on long contracts" (L1541), is Japan's.
*Fix:* No wall. The Batavia Compact simply grants Java sugar no entry: existing duties and the Formosan contracts stand, and the trading houses' Java purchases are in bond for third markets. The Hawaiian planters' and the Cortes's demand is then "no concession on sugar", which the Concord's negotiators can grant at Hayama without changing a duty.

**B4. Brunei: the British Resident is missing, the compact does not say who protects or governs Brunei afterwards, and Brunei and Tonga sign before they can.**
*Passage:* B3.2, B3.3, B4 (Tonga), Calendar.
*Problem:* (a) *Hist.* From the agreement of 1905–06 Brunei is governed on the advice of a British Resident, which the Sultan is bound to take on everything but religion and custom; the Resident in 1932 is T. F. Carey (September 1931 – October 1934). The draft has the Sultan "advised by Brunei's wazirs" sign a compact for "Concord external maritime protection" and never mentions the Residency. It therefore does not say whether Britain's protectorate of 1888 ends, whether the Resident stays, or who lets "new blocks by open auction" (in 1932 the Resident's government does). CR-074 M1 records that "Britain keeps Labuan, Sarawak and Brunei" (CR-029 A42, APPROVED). (b) The author's M-1 approves "Labuan return to Brunei + Concord harbour lease". It does not approve a transfer of the protectorate; "Concord external maritime protection" is the drafter's addition and is not listed among the PROPOSED items. (c) Order of consent. Brunei's and Tonga's foreign relations are Britain's until Britain releases them (1888/1906; Tonga's treaty of 18 May 1900). The calendar has both treaties "signed in spring" and the British treaty signed in June–July after a Senate vote in late May–June. Labuan votes in January–April on a cession no British instrument yet offers. (d) The Sultan is eighteen or nineteen and, on the record, deferential to the Resident; the two wazirs are the regents of 1924–31. The draft should say who actually decides.
*Fix:* The smallest change that stays inside M-1: Britain keeps the protectorate of 1888 and the Residency; by a British–Brunei agreement Labuan is retroceded to the Sultan (the retrocession of the Dindings to Perak in 1934–35 is a real precedent, from memory); the Sultan, with Britain's consent written into `C-1932-UK-RETRENCHMENT`, leases the harbour to the Concord. Brunei is then a party to the lease and not a new protectorate, Seria stays where it is, and "no flag" is literally true. If the author wants the protectorate to move, that is a new decision with its own questions (the Resident, the treasury, Limbang) and should be put to him as one. In either case: the British treaty, or a signed preliminary, comes first; Labuan votes on published terms after it; Brunei's and Tonga's instruments are initialled before and signed after. State what happens if Labuan votes no.

**B5. The island list does not match canon or the map of 1932.**
*Passage:* B4 ("the remaining British Line and Phoenix Islands (`LINEAT`—Fanning, Washington, Canton, Enderbury ...) and the Gilbert and Ellice Islands"); New parties.
*Problem:* (a) Canon: L700, "Hawaiian schooners raise the Confederation's flag over the remaining American guano bonds in the Phoenix group, at Kiritimati and on Swains" (1870), and L1139, "Britain recognizes Pacifican sovereignty over the equatorial islands from Palmyra to Baker." The American guano bonds in the Phoenix group covered Enderbury, McKean and Phoenix among others (from memory), so part of what the draft has Britain relinquish may already be Pacifican. The draft cites L700 only for Kiritimati. (b) *Hist.* Fanning and Washington were added to the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony on 27 January 1916, so they are not a separate "Line" party; the Phoenix group was not in the colony until 1937; the Union Islands (Tokelau) went to New Zealand's administration in 1926 and the draft does not say they are excluded. (c) *Hist.* Ocean Island was the colony's headquarters from 1908 to 1942 and phosphate its main revenue; the colony's administration existed in large part "to procure labour for phosphate mining on Ocean Island". Transferring the Gilberts "explicitly excluding Ocean Island" leaves Britain a seat of government without a colony and a phosphate island without its labour pool, and leaves the trust the people without the revenue. The draft does not notice. (d) Fanning is called "uninhabited/cable-station"; it and Washington had resident plantation labour. Passing them "on Britain's renunciation" needs a line on who lives there. (e) The stated purpose is the "Hawaii–Tutuila–Australia cable and air corridor". The Gilberts are not on it. The draft gives no Pacifican interest in the Gilberts and Ellice at all.
*Fix:* State which Phoenix islands canon already makes Pacifican and take only the rest. Treat Fanning and Washington as part of the colony Britain detaches them from. Decide the Gilberts and Ellice on their merits: either leave them British with Ocean Island (nothing "of importance" to the corridor is lost, which is the author's own test), or keep them optional and add a labour-and-revenue article for Ocean Island and Nauru that Australia and New Zealand, as phosphate commissioners, will demand. Exclude Tokelau in terms.

**B6. Japan's oil lever does not "survive" on the draft's own terms, and Japan's new command contradicts the renewal.**
*Passage:* C3.
*Problem:* L1720 (PROPOSED): "Julian threatens the resources Tokyo cannot easily replace: oil from Pacifica and the Gulf Republic". The draft gives Japanese firms "open-auction bidding and consortium entry" in Brunei and East Borneo and routes Seria crude to Asia, then says the lever survives "because Brunei/Indies crude flows through the Concord's common supply under common Finance (`L1597`)". L1597 lists Foreign Affairs, the Navy and "their Finance"; canon has no common oil supply and no power of the Finance minister over Shell's or a Japanese company's cargoes. A Japanese-owned field in Borneo is exactly a replacement for Pacifican and Gulf oil. Separately, L1734 says of the same renewal: "Japan's overseas commands passing to common control ... The naval party loses its reserved squadron arrangement as command is consolidated". Giving Japan "common-Navy operational command at the Labuan Harbour Lease" hands back in the south what the renewal takes away, without saying so.
*Fix:* Say the truth of the bargain: Japan's real gain is a first source of oil and bauxite not under Thalassia's hand, and Julian knows what it costs him. If the lever must survive, write the instrument: Japanese entry only in consortium with the trust, cargoes lifted in the common fleet's tankers, and the pledge's breach voiding the titles. For Labuan, either a mixed station under the consolidated command (and then it is not a Japanese gain) or an explicit exception to L1734, listed as a canon tension.

## Should fix

**S1. The "two figures" are misread.** *Passage:* A2. *Problem:* L1020: "two figures beside its price: the mouths it feeds or the people it brings, and by what year." The draft's figures are "mouths added to the Realm's roll: zero" and a list of strategic returns. *Fix:* Give the figure CR-082 asks for: tonnes of grain or rice that pass the straits to the Realm and its buyers under the century plan and the year they are needed (the Mekong "comes last", L1672), or an honest zero with the year, and put the strategic return in the prose beside it. Under Julian the figure is a target, so say what target.

**S2. Pacifica's own gain is thin and partly nominal.** *Passage:* A2. *Problem:* The Realm cancels its own claim (it also pays sixty-five percent of common costs, L1593) and receives: a halt to a base that *Hist.* was already suspended; "open, unfortified" straits that neither Britain nor the Netherlands closes in peace; rubber and tin it could buy in the 1932 glut without a treaty; and a harbour under Japanese command. "Saving Pacifica far more in western-Pacific fleet construction" is asserted, not sized, and is not in Part F's workbook tasks. *Fix:* Name gains that are the Realm's: (i) Fanning, the mid-Pacific landing of Britain's Pacific cable, in a trust the Realm sits in, with the cipher room already reading British traffic (L1534); (ii) the air stations of the corridor; (iii) the trust's Seria offtake and new Brunei blocks (the trust "has the canal and the Asian markets", L1096, and Borneo is a shorter haul to them than Texas); (iv) a fleet-cost line in the workbook, MODEL; (v) the remaining 38 to 43 annuities kept as the lever of L1754 ("Britain is managed through debt"). Say plainly that the Realm pays and the Concord receives, and why Julian accepts that.

**S3. CR-084 can merge alone, but collides with the CR-083 draft when both are in.** Both cancel a separately sized tranche of the same 53 annuities, so the total is unknown and M-0 (approving `C-1923-UK-FUNDING`) is needed by both. Both stage a Westminster debate in May–June 1932 with Churchill and Beaverbrook, and both stage the sufficers objecting in the Senate to cancelling British debt. CR-083 leases Bamfield and CR-084 leases Fanning, the two ends of one cable, to the same company. The header's "zero dependency" is true mechanically, but the impact notes say the lines were "verified against `main` / `drafts/cr083-caledonia`". *Fix:* One paragraph saying what is shared: one schedule of cancelled annuities with two purposes, one Westminster debate, one Senate debate, whichever merges second amends the first.

**S4. The moratorium and the annuity count.** *Passage:* B1. In canon the moratorium is Pacifica's own (L1334: "The later moratorium belongs to Thalassia too"), "and debt is restructured" (L1693). The draft narrows the restructuring to "short-term 1931 arrears" and counts 53 annuities left; if the 1931–32 instalment was suspended, say where it went. *Hist.* (from memory) the moratorium ran 1 July 1931 to 30 June 1932, which puts its expiry inside the Thalassia Conference; use it.

**S5. The British side.** *Hist.* (confirmed): from 5 November 1931 the Foreign Secretary is Sir John Simon, the Colonial Secretary Sir Philip Cunliffe-Lister, the First Lord Sir Bolton Eyres-Monsell, the Dominions Secretary J. H. Thomas; Hoare is Secretary of State for India. A treaty ceding a colony, releasing two protected states and capping the fleet is negotiated in the draft by MacDonald, Baldwin, Chamberlain and Hoare, without the three departments whose business it is. Hoare's presence needs a reason beyond the Petrograd file. The Admiralty is the natural refuser and has no voice. The "capital-ship cap east of Ceylon" costs Britain little in peace (the China Station had no capital ships, from memory); the base is the price. *Hist.* (confirmed, Hansard 13 November 1929): Hong Kong, the Federated Malay States and New Zealand had by then paid "£2,113,000" toward the base and the Straits Settlements gave the site. A permanent halt raises the question of that money; it is a ready-made price for New Zealand (S8). The reverter is good and should have a trigger someone can invoke.

**S6. Sarawak's shore of Brunei Bay and Limbang.** See the PROPOSED items. As drafted the refusal costs nobody anything.

**S7. Tonga's motive and the line.** *Passage:* B4, C4. Sālote's reason to change protectors is one phrase in a set piece ("refuses to be listed on a Whitehall schedule"). On the record she was loyal to Britain and Britain's hand in Tonga was light. Give her a gain (the consul's veto over finance gone; a treaty she signs as a principal) and a doubt. Japan's signature on a Tongan treaty needs a reason Tonga would accept. Geography: Tonga (about 175° W) and Tutuila (about 170° W) lie east of the 180th meridian, where the 10° S line ends, so "grandfathering" Tutuila under that line is incoherent and the line says nothing about Tonga. Either run the line east or drop the Tutuila clause.

**S8. Australia and New Zealand refuse nothing.** *Passage:* B4 ("removes Canberra's and Wellington's objections completely"), C4. They lose the Singapore strategy New Zealand paid for, see the Gilberts' labour pass to a trust, and acquire a Japanese-commanded harbour at 5° N, and their only recorded act is to "accept". Canon's dominions refuse the equality clause (L1752). Give Wellington the Singapore money and Canberra a stated demand (for instance that the pledge be Japan's own ratified act and not a declaration), and let one of them get less than it asked.

**S9. China is informed, not asked.** *Passage:* B2. China is a treaty ally (L1541) and the Concord here pledges to defend "Malaya/Indian Ocean commerce" for the power the Canton–Hong Kong strike was aimed at (L1534), and declares that "any Sino-British treaty dispute ... goes to the Hague arbitration court", which the Concord cannot decide for China. Nanjing should ask for something (a sentence that nothing in the treaty recognises the status of Hong Kong or the New Territories would do) and be seen to get or not get it.

**S10. Batavia.** (a) C1.1 says The Hague "declines a colonial defense guarantee" and accepts a pledge "to prevent external sea seizure", which is a guarantee. Choose. (b) "Private San Francisco and Tokyo banking syndicates extend a gold-dollar" credit in autumn 1932: L1695 has Takahashi asking that winter "for fresh dollars for Japan's villages" and being refused; Tokyo lending gold dollars to Batavia six months later hands the Restoration officers their case and is not plausible. A credit promised in a treaty is the state's promise whoever writes the cheque; say who stands behind it, and expect Giannini's objection again. (c) *Hist.* (not verified in this pass; from memory) India, Java's largest market, raised its sugar duties in 1931–32, and Java was bound by the Chadbourne agreement of 1931; "re-export to China, India, and the Middle East" by Japanese houses, which already dominated the Java trade, changes little. (d) A2 says Pacifica is "once again a major natural-rubber buyer" because Tacoma shifted; Tacoma made 25,000 tonnes in 1929 (L1551), a small part of what "the most motorized place on earth" uses. Pacifica never stopped being a buyer. (e) Bintan bauxite for the Concord pool displaces Arkansas bauxite (L1560: "Its bauxite comes from Arkansas ... and the Republic gets the alumina refinery and the royalty"); "preserving the Gulf Republic's alumina refinery royalty" is asserted without a mechanism. With the oil, that is two injuries to the Gulf in one treaty. (f) "The Union's NKPM Sumatra holdings" is a new canon fact (L1337 has Standard "a giant refinery without crude" abroad through Imperial and Peru); mark it PROPOSED. (g) No Indonesian has a voice. *Hist.* de Jonge's government kept the Boven-Digoel camp in New Guinea for nationalists; "Dutch New Guinea stays closed" has a meaning the draft does not use. The Philippine Cortes and Nanjing have reasons to dislike a Concord pledge that steadies Dutch rule.

**S11. The Canon tensions section presents no tension.** T1–T5 are each marked "Resolved", "Preserved" or "Decided". AGENTS.md asks for both cases and a recommendation. Real tensions to list: the ratification rules (B2); L1693 on tariffs (B3); L1738 "one last bargain" (B1); L1734 and L1720 (B6); L1885, which assigns "any southern renunciation" to "(the partners' CR)" and is taken here; L1385 and `C-1919-NAVAL-LIMITS`, both PROPOSED in canon and amended here; L700/L1139 (B5); the Brunei protectorate against CR-074 M1 (B4).

**S12. Fact changes.** The event patterns match only a sentence that begins "In June 1932 the British Pacific Retrenchment Treaty" and its like; integrated prose will rarely be written that way, so a wrong year will pass. Loosen them (treaty name within a sentence of a year). `batavia-compact-signed` covers initialling in June and ratification in autumn under one id. The Brunei pattern allows "May|June" while the calendar says spring. The forbidden pattern `guarantees (?:British )?Hong Kong` misses "guarantee of Hong Kong" and "Hong Kong is guaranteed"; the annexation pattern catches only the present tense "annexes". Missing: a guard for Pacifican "base rights at Singapore" as granted, for Sarawak signing, and for Fiji, the Solomons or Ocean Island transferred. `CT-AHMAD-TAJUDDIN` is given born 1912 while the draft's own date of majority (eighteen on 19 September 1931) implies 1913.

**S13. Labuan.** *Hist.* Labuan was ceded by the Treaty of Labuan of 18 December 1846 and made a Crown colony in 1848; the draft says Brunei "ceded it in 1848". Its people are British subjects; the Chinese traders in particular have reason to vote no. The draft gives no no-branch, so the vote reads as a formality. The Sultan of Sulu is "consulted on Brunei Bay navigation": *Hist.* (from memory) the bay shore of North Borneo came from Brunei's grants, not Sulu's; canon's lease is the Sultan of Sulu's (L764), so the consultation is harmless but should not be presented as his right. The sentence that Labuan "returned to direct British Straits Settlements rule in 1907 rather than staying under the Pacifican North Borneo company" implies the Pacifican company once ran Labuan; canon says only that Britain kept it.

**S14. Japan has no person and the factions are reversed.** No Japanese actor is named anywhere in the draft (Inukai, Takahashi, Shidehara, a navy minister, Chichibu; the impact notes list Chichibu and Takahashi for the character bible, and neither appears). *Hist.* (from memory) the southward advance was the navy's doctrine; a renunciation south of the Philippines costs the fleet faction its case for oil, and the army's eyes are on Manchuria. The draft has the navy pleased and the army "caged". The coup's manifesto (L1732) is said to cite the Java contracts and the pledge, but on the draft's calendar the pledge is agreed at Hayama in May and the Compact initialled in June. The officers can cite a leak, if the draft says so.

**S15. The Cortes vote is a formality.** Mariveles is already a chartered district (L1681) and a repair contract placed there needs no vote of the Cortes. If the Cortes votes, give it something to decide (the Philippine crown's "own budget and army", L1234; a Philippine share in the Labuan station; its view of a pledge to the Dutch).

**S16. Fanning and L1740.** The draft says the lease-back preserves L1740 ("two independent routes"). L1740 is about Pacifica's own cables "on Pacifican or allied ground through Oʻahu, Midway, Guam and Manila". The British cable is not one of them. What the lease-back actually ends is Britain's rule that its Pacific cable touches only British soil (from memory); with Bamfield in CR-083, both mid-ocean ends go. That is a larger British concession than the atolls and deserves the Admiralty's objection.

## The PROPOSED items

**1. Rajah Vyner Brooke's refusal.**

*Plausibility.* The refusal is plausible; the offer is not. *Hist.* (confirmed) under the agreement of 1888 Sarawak is "an independent State under the protection of Great Britain"; Britain conducts its foreign relations and must consent to any cession of territory; nothing gives Britain a right to assign its protection to another power. Britain cannot "offer to transfer its 1888 Sarawak protectorate" over the Rajah's head, and a National Government that keeps Malaya for the sterling bloc has no reason to try. So the draft sets up an offer that could not be made in order to have it refused. As to the man: Vyner Brooke ran a "hands-off" government, shared it in his early years with his brother Bertram, and in 1946 ceded the state to Britain. He is not obviously the dynast who "prizes autonomy"; a Rajah who asks what is offered and finds it too little is truer to the record than a flat no. "Sarawak owes no debt to anyone" I could not confirm for 1932. Miri: first oil on 22 December 1910, peak in 1929 at 15,211 barrels a day; it was past its peak in 1932, so "Shell's Miri oil field sits securely" overstates the prize.

*Cost.* As drafted, none. The Concord did not need Sarawak, Britain loses nothing, Brunei loses nothing. The draft says it gives "the book a real refusal", but a refusal that costs no one is decoration.

*What it leaves on the map, and the loose end.* This is where the refusal could matter. *Hist.* (confirmed) Sarawak annexed Limbang in 1890; Brunei never accepted it ("Brunei is Limbang and Limbang is Brunei"); Limbang lies "between the two halves of Brunei", and Sarawak's Limbang, Lawas and Trusan districts front Brunei Bay. With North Borneo Pacifican (L764) and Labuan leased to the Concord, the bay's southern shore remains a British-protected Rajah's. The anchorage the draft makes the southern anchor is therefore overlooked from ground outside the treaty, and Brunei remains cut in two. The draft does not mention Limbang.

*Recommendation: keep, and change.* The approach comes from Thalassia through London, as an enquiry London forwards without recommending, and concerns something specific: Limbang for Brunei (the Sultan's real price), or a non-fortification undertaking for the Sarawak shore of the bay. Brooke refuses Limbang. The cost falls on Brunei, which signs for Labuan and without Limbang, and on the Concord's navy, which takes an anchorage with a foreign shore. London declines to press him, which is the one thing Britain refuses the Concord in Borneo. That is a refusal with a price, and it stands at the end of 1932. Drop "Britain offers to transfer its protectorate".

**2. The five-year Gulf crude volume guarantee.**

*What canon has.* L1697: "The compact holds a floor under the price rather than cutting output: the Realm cuts none of its own wells, and its purchases of the Republic's crude help hold the floor." L1096: the trust "buys crude at posted prices; it earns its share as the best buyer, because it has the canal and the Asian markets", and "no refiner may refine more than fifty-five percent of the Republic's crude ... the Pacifican trust's share is a treaty limit". The ledger's `C-1932-GULF-OIL` has parties GULF and PAC, floor and term not stated (the workbooks use $0.95). L1620: the trust's own oil abroad (Lago, Peru, Bahrain's first oil in June 1932, Kuwait, the Mesopotamian share) is "the 1930s' oil bought at 1920s prices". L1600: Pacifica supplies the Concord's "oil products".

*Plausibility.* That the Gulf asks for it is entirely plausible: after "a year of ten-cent East Texas oil" a price floor without a buyer is paper, and a compact that sends Borneo crude into the trust's Asian markets takes the outlet the Gulf's barrels travel to. That the Realm's Senate grants it at "Houston's" demand is not (B1). The draft's claim that Borneo and Indies barrels will "displace outside European/Union sellers in Asia rather than Gulf barrels" does not hold: the sellers of oil in Asia are Shell's own Indies fields and, in canon, the trust selling Gulf and home oil. Seria crude displaces the trust's Gulf-sourced sales or it displaces Shell's own.

*Who is bound, to what.* The draft says "Pacifica guarantees its minimum annual purchases". The buyer in canon is the trust, a champion, not the Treasury. A state guarantee of a private refiner's liftings is a new kind of instrument: if the trust lifts less, does the Treasury buy and store, or pay? At what price, the floor or the posted price? Is the minimum inside the 55 percent ceiling? None of this is stated, and "the floor price and term" are already open in the ledger.

*What it costs, and whom.* The trust: a floor under its liftings of dear Gulf crude in the years its cheap Bahrain, Peruvian and (now) Seria barrels arrive; it must place those in Asia and Japan instead. The Realm: a contingent liability in a slump in which it "raises neither tariffs nor taxes". Japan: nothing directly, but if the trust's cheap barrels go to Japan while Gulf barrels come home, Japan's supply under the Hayama annex shifts from Gulf and Californian oil to Borneo and Bahrain oil, which bears on the lever of L1720 (B6). The Union: it is the Gulf's largest customer (about 270 million barrels in 1929, L1337); a Realm minimum tightens what is left for it, mildly.

*Contradiction with canon?* Not with the floor: a minimum purchase is the mechanism L1697 already names ("its purchases ... help hold the floor"), made a number and a term. It is not an output cut. It does sit awkwardly with L1096's ceiling and with the trust's freedom to buy as "the best buyer".

*Recommendation: keep the substance, change the form and the route.* It is the Gulf government's price in the 1932 compact, asked when it learns of Seria and Bintan, and granted by the Realm because the same treaty costs the Gulf oil outlet and bauxite. Write it as an undertaking by the trust, countersigned by the Realm, that its liftings of Gulf crude will not fall below a stated share of its average liftings of 1929–31 for five years, at the posted price and never below the compact's floor, inside the 55 percent ceiling, with the Realm's navy fuel contract as the backstop. A share is safer than a volume while the slump lasts. Add a parallel line for Arkansas bauxite or say that Bintan is additional tonnage only. The Senate then votes one thing, the Realm's countersignature, and Giannini can object to that with reason. Its five years should end with the Concord's term in 1937 on purpose, and the draft should say so.

## Literary effect

*What works.* Britain has a spine: it keeps Fiji, the Solomons, Malaya, Hong Kong and Ocean Island, and takes a counter-signature and a reverter against the day Japan leaves. That is a counterparty, not a donor. De Jonge is the right antagonist, a former oil-company director defending the oil company's colony, and he wins things. Churchill held one sentence short of a crisis by a sheet of paper he saw once is the draft's best use of canon. "One yes, one no" at Kuching and Brunei Town is a scene. The Labuan headman and the Gilbertese elder are the right viewpoints for the rule that no people are taken by decree.

*What is too convenient.* Everyone signs within nine months and no one who signs loses anything he minds. Shell "accepts". Australia and New Zealand "accept". Nanjing is "informed". The Sultan has "compelling reasons". The Labuan vote has no losing side. Brooke's refusal costs nothing. The sugar wall, the Gulf guarantee and the private credit each remove an objection in the paragraph that raises it. The Senate passes it "narrowly" but every objector is paid.

*Where agency is missing.* Japan: no named person, a gain that is rights to bid, and a price that is permanent. The navy, which in canon has just stood with the Emperor against the coup, gives up its southern doctrine without a voice. China: an ally told afterwards. The Brunei court: the wazirs who were regents until September 1931 and the Resident who governs are the real parties and neither acts. The Philippine crown: a vote on a shipyard. Hawaii: its court are sufficers (L1020) and its schooners raised the flag on these islands in 1870 (L700); it has more to say about the Line and Phoenix atolls than about sugar. Indonesians and Malayans: one mill clerk. The common Foreign minister, who makes every treaty here, has no name. Julian acts once, at Nukuʻalofa.

*Pacifica's interest.* The draft knows it must show it (A2) and then lists goods that are mostly the Concord's or nobody's. The author's objection to drafts in which Pacifica pays and others gain applies here: the Realm cancels its own gold claim, the harbour's command is Japanese, the concessions are open to all bidders, and the straits were open before. S2 lists what would make the Realm's gain visible.

*Flat.* The Senate set piece, as cast. Australia and New Zealand. The Batavia credit. The Thalassia signing, which adds a fourth and fifth treaty to a conference canon already fills with the Union's settlement (L1746).
