## Verdict

One more revision. The new parts (`A2a`, `B5`, the tranche schedule) are the best of the draft and nearly hold, and the canon citations I checked are accurate. Three things stop integration: the 10° S protocol is built on a geographical error that changes Canberra's bargain, Britain decides to renounce without anyone in the room asking who else signs while `CR-086` has France stay out, and the 30 June deadline that is said to drive London is switched off by the draft's own April accord.

## Blockers

**B1. Rabaul and Nauru are not south of 10° S.**
*Passage.* Line 213: "The former German Samoa, German New Guinea with Rabaul, and Nauru are Pacifica's after 1919 ... All three lie inside the zone. Their stations as they stood on 1 January 1932 go on the annex with Tutuila and Swains, so the annex has five entries. The line is worth that much less to Canberra, which has had Rabaul to its north since 1919 and says so: what it gains is that nothing is added and nothing is fortified further."
*Problem.* The zone is "south of 10° S between Timor and 155° W". Rabaul is at about 4° S, the whole of former German New Guinea lies between about 1° S and 8° S, and Nauru is half a degree south of the equator. Only Samoa (about 13.5° S) is inside. So the annex has three entries, not five; and the protocol leaves Pacifica free to add to and fortify Rabaul, the one station Canberra fears most, which is the opposite of "nothing is added and nothing is fortified further". The author's "6 Pacifica" made this station Pacifican an hour before the revision, and the line was not redrawn for it. A Canberra that accepted 10° S with a Pacifican Rabaul at 4° S unbound has won nothing, and line 214 already has it lose everything else.
*Fix.* Decide what Canberra gets about Rabaul and say it: either a named standstill clause for the former German New Guinea and Nauru beside the 10° S line (no new works, no capital ships stationed), or Canberra asks for it and is refused, which then needs a reason why Canberra still accedes. Correct the annex to Tutuila, Swains and the station in former German Samoa. Bring line 245 ("Former German Pacific territories unaffected by the 10° S line"), line 243, the M-4 row at line 538 ("Tutuila and Swains excepted") and the ledger line 311 into agreement with whatever is chosen. See also S1.

**B2. Britain renounces without asking who else signs, and France does not.**
*Passage.* Lines 164 to 184 (`B5`), especially line 168 ("A signatory outside the Concord would undertake ...") and the three requests at lines 179 to 182.
*Problem.* No one in the committee of six asks whether Germany and France sign. For a cabinet of 1932 that is the first question, before the Exchequer's. `CR-086` `C4` then has France sign the Act and Article II and reserve on Article I, keeping "its own hands free" with "a school, gold and a reason to look". So Britain decides in late May, France declines after Herriot's government forms in June, and Westminster still ratifies in the autumn a text that binds Britain and Germany and leaves France, across the Channel, unbound. `B5` gives no request, no condition and no loser on this point, and Lindemann, whose case is "the second rank for good", is not given the obvious sentence. As written the decision is too convenient at exactly the place the suite's ending rests on.
*Fix.* Add a fourth request to Simon's instructions and give it an outcome. The simplest that fits the siblings: London asks that its undertaking fall if another European power builds a separating plant; Thalassia refuses the condition and answers with what it can offer (the court, and the fact that the pledge is withheld from a non-signatory), and Simon reports that Germany's signature is what London needed and France's ore and heavy water are not in French hands. Whatever is chosen, say in `B5` what the cabinet knew about France when it decided and what it does when France reserves, and tell `CR-086` `C4`.

**B3. The 30 June deadline has no force after April.**
*Passage.* Line 119: the moratorium's expiry "falls *inside* the Thalassia Conference ... creating the concrete deadline". Line 131: "Both become payable when the moratorium ends on 30 June 1932 unless the conference disposes of them. The accord of April carries the suspension forward to the day of signature, and on 1 August they are written off. Between those dates Britain owes nothing ... That is why 30 June matters to London."
*Problem.* The same paragraph says the April accord already extends the suspension to signature. From April onward 30 June is not a deadline and does not matter to London. The pressure the draft relies on in May, when the cabinet decides, has been given away in the preliminary accord. A second gap sits beside it: the two instalments are "written off on signature", whatever Westminster does later, while request 3 at line 182 is told as only "half won".
*Fix.* Choose one. Either the April accord does not extend the suspension, and London negotiates through June with two instalments falling due on 1 July (then say who waives them day by day, and on what authority before the chambers vote); or it extends it only to 1 August and conditionally, so that a conference without a signature leaves both instalments due at once, and 1 August, not 30 June, is London's date. Then correct lines 119, 131, 225 ("Hoover moratorium expires 30 June") and 295 together, and say whether the write-off survives a refusal at Westminster.

**S5. Chamberlain's answer to default leans on a lever that went in September 1931.** Line 173: "The gold credit that has stood behind sterling since 1925 (`L1566`) is Thalassia's to withdraw".
