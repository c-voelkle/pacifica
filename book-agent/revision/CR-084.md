**A2a. The cabinet's statement of the Realm's own interest (Thalassia, winter 1931–32; PROPOSED).**
The Realm alone gives up the gold, and the Concord shares what the gold buys. Before the Foreign Minister opens the talks, Julian's cabinet therefore writes down, in one minute, what the Realm is buying for itself. Like the conclusions drawn from the Office's tables (`L1020`), the minute is a cabinet paper and is never published. It makes four statements.
1. **The straits.** A decade on, part of the Realm's bread comes through four straits it does not hold. A treaty that binds the two powers on their shores to keep them open in peace is worth more to the Realm than a base at Singapore, which would make the Realm the policeman of Malaya and the enemy of Nanjing at Hong Kong.
2. **Britain's signature.** The Works reckoned in 1928 that Britain could have a device about 1933–1935 (`L1614 ("fully committed from 1928")`). A Britain that stops for a price it is glad to take costs the Realm less than the plant it would otherwise build to stay ahead of a Britain that does not stop. The minute says only that the annuities buy "Britain's signature under the first article". It records nothing of what the Works holds.
3. **The cable and the corridor.** Fanning, the Phoenix titles and the air stations between Hawaii, Tutuila and Australia come under a trust in which the Realm sits.
4. **Japan's oil.** A Japan with oil of its own in East Borneo is a Japan that stays in the Concord because it gains by staying. The cabinet records that this spends part of the lever of 1931 (`L1720 ("oil from Pacifica and the Gulf Republic")`) and that it does so knowingly.

**The oil trust objects and loses.** The cabinet hears the president of Pacifica's oil trust (name **OPEN**) before it settles the minute. His case is that the trust pays twice: the Batavia Compact gives Japanese houses crude of their own in the trust's best market, and the Gulf's undertaking (`B3.4`) binds the trust for five years to lift dear Gulf crude that the Seria offtake would otherwise displace. He asks for one of two things, a share for the trust in every Japanese concession in East Borneo or release from the Gulf undertaking. Julian refuses both. The first would keep the lever the fourth statement has decided to spend, and the second is the Gulf's price and is not the Realm's to waive. The trust is left with the Seria offtake and the right to bid on Brunei's new blocks at auction like anyone else, and it carries its objection to the Senate, where it loses again (`A3`).

**B2. Singapore, Hong Kong and the China Alliance.**
- *Hist.* anchor: On **13 November 1929**, Britain's Labour government told the Commons that work on the **Singapore Naval Base (Sembawang)** *"shall be slowed down as much as possible"* and *"all work that can be suspended shall be suspended"*, with a floating dock already provided and **£2,113,000** contributed by Hong Kong, the Federated Malay States, and New Zealand (Hansard, 13 Nov 1929).
- **Exact terms:**
  1. **Permanent halt of the Singapore capital-ship base & peacetime capital-ship cap east of Ceylon:** Britain permanently halts capital-ship fortification and heavy dry-dock work at Singapore (converting or withdrawing the floating dock; PROPOSED) and accepts a peacetime capital-ship cap east of Ceylon (amending `L1381 ("Naval limits count the combined")`; cruisers and sloops stay unrestricted), subject to Britain's **reverter clause**. **New Zealand's share of the £2,113,000 Singapore contribution** is rebated/credited by London against New Zealand's wartime debt to Britain (giving Wellington a concrete settlement for losing the Singapore strategy; PROPOSED).
  2. **Hong Kong excluded & Nanjing's reservation (`L1534 ("Hong Kong strike runs from June 1925")`, `L1541 ("Treaty of Nanjing with the Concord")`, `L1706 ("Britain holds no Weihaiwei")`):** Because China is a guaranteed treaty ally, **Hong Kong is excluded from all Concord guarantees**. The Concord's maritime pledge covers strictly external third-party sea attack on Malacca Strait and Malaya/Indian Ocean commerce, excluding internal colonial order.

**B4. Fanning, Washington, Residual Phoenix Claims, and the Kingdom of Tonga.**
- **What stays British / New Zealand:** **Fiji** (Crown colony 1874, Great Council of Chiefs 1876), the **British Solomon Islands**, the **New Hebrides** (Anglo-French Condominium, 1906), **Pitcairn**, the **Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony proper with Ocean Island** (headquarters 1908–42 and Australia/NZ phosphate hub—leaving Britain's administration and labour pool intact), and **Tokelau / Union Islands** (under New Zealand administration since 11 Feb 1926, *Hist.*, explicitly excluded).
- **What Britain detaches (`OPEN-019-05`, strictly on the Hawaii–Tutuila–Australia cable/air corridor):**
  1. **Fanning Island and Washington Island (`FANNING`):** Formally detached by Britain from the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony (*Hist.:* added to GEIC 27 Jan 1916). Because neither atoll has an indigenous polity—both are private copra estates worked by imported Gilbertese contract labour alongside cable staff—this is **not** a "people's consent" vote, and is handled honestly as **three commercial/labour instruments**:
     - **Plantation buyout/re-lease (`FANNING-ESTATE`):** *Hist.* in 1930–32, Fanning and Washington's copra plantations were owned by Father **Emmanuel Rougier's** syndicate (*Fanning Island Limited*) and heavily mortgaged to **Burns Philp (South Sea) Company** as managing agents (which bought them outright in 1935). Ka Lōkahi buys out or re-leases the mortgaged estate from **Fanning Island Limited and Burns Philp** at published valuation (`CR-073` pattern), making the plantation owner an explicit party;
     - **Contract-Labour & Repatriation Compact (`FANNING`):** The Gilbertese contract workers receive Pacifica's 1887 labour-article protections (`CR-071`) plus a free, funded choice between paid repatriation home to the Gilberts or continued free-wage employment under Ka Lōkahi's trust charter (land leased never sold; annual Office of Public Measures accounts; naval/cable stations by lease only; ten-year review petitions, `T-K1` Case B approved);
     - **Cable Lease:** **Fanning's Pacific Cable station** (`L1139 ("British Fanning as the station")`) is leased back to Imperial and International Communications Ltd. (whose payment bore interest from 1 April 1928, *Hist.*), breaking Britain's all-British Pacific cable rule over Admiralty protest.
  2. **Residual Phoenix quitclaims:** Canon already made **Kiritimati, Swains, and the former American guano-bond Phoenix islands** (`L700 ("Kiritimati")`, `L1139`) Pacifican in 1870–99; Britain quitclaims its remaining overlapping British claims in the Phoenix group (Canton, Gardner, Hull, Sydney) to confirm the air-corridor titles.
- **Kingdom of Tonga (`C-1932-TONGA-FRIENDSHIP`):** Under Britain's preliminary treaty (`April 1932`), Britain agrees to release its **18 May 1900 Treaty of Friendship and Protectorship** (and 1905 financial-veto agreement, *Hist.*) with Tonga's consent. **Queen Sālote Tupou III** (*Hist.:* born 13 March 1900, reigned 1918–65) wins the **end of the British consul's veto over Tongan finance** and signs as a sovereign principal—while distrusting Japanese military presence and insisting that the **Concord–Tonga Treaty of Friendship and Maritime Security** (`TO`, `PAC`, `JP`) name **Julian as Mōʻī of Hawaii** as ceremonial guarantor, bar any foreign garrison or base on Tongan soil, and take effect only *after* Britain's formal release. **B5. The cabinet's decision to renounce (London, April – June 1932; PROPOSED).**
This is the largest single decision any foreign government makes in the endgame suite. It is taken in London before the conference closes, so that the Final Act seals what is already won. `CR-086` owns the article Britain decides to sign: its text, how it is checked, who else signs and the date. This section owns Britain's reasons, its price and its losers. The Works' reckoning of a British device for "about 1933–1935" is overtaken here; the canon tension is `CR-086`'s first, decided by the author on 23 November 2026 at 19:32.

- **What is put to London.** In April 1932, with the preliminary accord, the Concord's Foreign Minister hands Simon a second paper: the draft of the first article of an act to be made at the conference's close. A signatory outside the Concord would undertake to build no plant for separating the light uranium, to make no test, and to deliver to the Works all fuel that has been through a pile. Atomic power stays lawful. Two things come with it. The Realm pledges in the same article never to use the weapon against a signatory, and the annuity schedule carries a share for Britain's signature (`B1`). The paper discloses nothing about what the Works has made or can make.
- **What Britain has to give up.** Britain has been "fully committed from 1928" (`L1614`), four years by the spring of 1932. Its separation works copies the halls, "the method Pacifica is about to abandon" (`L1640 ("copies the method Pacifica is about to abandon")`), and its pile work waits on heavy water, of which the Works "holds most of the world's supply" (`L1643 ("holds most of the world's supply")`). The ore is under contract elsewhere (`CR-083` for Great Bear, `CR-086` for the rest). No figure for Britain's progress is given on the page; the scene needs only that Britain is years away and knows it.
- **The committee.** MacDonald forms a committee of six: himself, Baldwin, Chamberlain, Simon, Eyres-Monsell and Hoare. It hears two scientists, both already in canon.
  - **Henry Tizard** (`CT-HENRY-TIZARD`), who read the Sierra tables correctly in 1922 and reopened his file in 1924 (`L1575 ("Tizard reopens his file")`), gives the estimate. On the money voted since the crisis of 1931 he cannot promise a device before the later 1930s. With the money doubled he would still need ore he does not have. He cannot say what Thalassia will have built in the same years, and he says that this is the figure that matters.
  - **Frederick Lindemann** (`CT-FREDERICK-LINDEMANN`), who called the separation factory impossible (`L1572 ("Oxford's professor of experimental philosophy")`) and was shown to be wrong in 1927, argues for going on with a convert's force. His case is the best one made against signing: the third clause takes away the material itself, so a signature given in 1932 cannot be taken back in 1942, and Britain would be signing itself into the second rank for good.
- **Chamberlain** answers with the Exchequer's account. Sterling is off gold, the annuities are owed in gold dollars, and a separation works on the scale of the Sierra's published power tables is beyond any estimate he can lay before the House. The tranche offered for the signature is money Britain receives now for giving up something it cannot afford to finish.
- **Eyres-Monsell**, who has just lost Sembawang, sides with Lindemann. A navy that can no longer hold the East with ships has, in his view, the more need of the one weapon that might. He loses twice in one season.
- **Simon** makes the case for signing as a matter of what Britain keeps and gains. It keeps atomic power and its laboratories. It gains, for the first time since the autumn of 1927, a ratified sentence saying that the weapon will not be used against it, and a court to which a breach can be taken.
- **Hoare** says little. His reason for being in the room is the India route, and his reason for silence is the Petrograd account (`L1609 ("Samuel Hoare")`): he knows better than his colleagues how much Thalassia holds that it has not used, and he tells MacDonald privately that the offer will not improve.
- **Churchill** is out of office. Baldwin tells him on Privy Council terms what is proposed. He was shown one sheet in June 1927 and saw the column of water that autumn, and he does not raise a revolt. What he says in private is left to the set piece.
- **The decision.** In the last week of June 1932, before the moratorium runs out on the 30th, the cabinet decides to sign. It asks three things of Thalassia and gets one and a half.
  1. **Won:** the Realm's pledge stands in the article itself and is ratified with it, and is not a separate declaration. (The pledge and the Navy's objection to it at Thalassia are `CR-086`'s.)
  2. **Lost:** Britain asks to keep the small separating plant it has built, as a laboratory. Thalassia refuses. The plant is dismantled, and the manner of checking is `CR-086`'s.
  3. **Half won:** Britain asks that its share of the annuities be cancelled on signature. It is cancelled on ratification at Westminster, with the suspended instalment of 1931–32 written off on signature.
- **Who loses.** Lindemann and the Admiralty lose in the committee room. The British programme's own people lose their work: the staff of the separating plant are stood down in the autumn of 1932, and Tizard is asked to turn the pile work to power.
- **The dominions.** Britain's signature does not cover them. Canada signs for itself (author, 23 November 2026, 19:55: "I would say Canada signs unless there is some pressing historical reason why it shouldn’t"); `CR-086` `C3` has the terms. The committee notes that this closes the last imperial source of ore: a Canada that has itself renounced cannot be asked to divert Great Bear.

## C. The Batavia Compact (Rung B: Concessions and Leased Ports), Japan's Southern Gain and the 10° S Line

**C1. The Batavia Compact: Concessions, Two Leased Commercial Ports, and de Jonge's Damage Limitation (`C-1932-BATAVIA-COMPACT`; restores author's 22 Nov Rung B decision).**
- **Dutch motives (*Hist.*):** Prime Minister **Charles Ruijs de Beerenbrouck's** cabinet (1929–33) faces collapsing Indies colonial revenues and estate exports (Java sugar under the 1931 Chadbourne scheme and rising Indian duties, Sumatra rubber, Bangka/Belitung tin) while Britain permanently halts Sembawang. With no European navy left east of Ceylon, The Hague prefers a ratified treaty setting fixed legal fences over an uncontrolled confrontation.
- **Governor-General B. C. de Jonge's stand — limiting the damage (PROPOSED):** **Bonifacius Cornelis de Jonge** (*Hist.:* 1875–1958, Governor-General 12 Sept 1931 – 16 Sept 1936, former Minister of War and **BPM board member** via Hendrikus Colijn) fights the Compact hard. Because The Hague concedes two fenced commercial ports with Japanese guards and wide new mineral blocks, Batavia gains no new sovereign rights—de Jonge limits the damage by holding four lines the Netherlands already possessed:
  1. **No annexation, no protectorate, no military guarantee:** The Hague declines any military guarantee as incompatible with Dutch neutrality. The Concord pledges strictly **non-annexation and respect for Dutch sovereignty outside the leased fences**, with **zero internal colonial policing** (`L1109 ("foreign invasion, not from their own people")`, `C-1888-COMPANIES-ACT`);
  2. **Royal Dutch Shell's (BPM) existing Tarakan, Balikpapan, and South Sumatra fields/refineries—and the Union's NKPM Sumatra holdings (PROPOSED, reconciling `L1337 ("giant refinery without crude")`)—stay completely untouched**;
  3. **The Dutch KPM inter-island shipping monopoly stays closed** (defeating Japanese shipping lines' commercial demand);
  4. **Dutch New Guinea stays closed** (protecting de Jonge's internment camp for Indonesian nationalists at **Boven-Digoel**, *Hist.*).
- **What the Concord and Japan win (Rung B — concessions plus leased commercial ports, `T-NF1`):**
  1. **Two Fenced Commercial Oil and Bauxite Leased Ports (50-year commercial leases under a ratified Dutch treaty):** One **bauxite loading port on Bintan Island** (*Hist.:* bauxite found in the Riau Islands in 1924, mined in the 1930s mostly for Japan; ramping post-1935) and **one new East Borneo oil loading port** (serving newly allocated East Borneo blocks outside Shell's Balikpapan/Tarakan titles—preventing any clash with London/Shell). Inside the fenced loading grounds, **Japanese company/consular guards maintain security**; **no naval fortification is permitted** (answering Canberra, `C3`), and Dutch sovereignty and Dutch courts govern everywhere outside the fences.
  2. **Oil & Mineral Concessions:** Open-door exploration and production concessions on published royalties in **unallocated East Borneo oil blocks**, **Bintan Island bauxite** (development from 1932, post-1935 deliveries strictly as *additional* tonnage on top of Arkansas bauxite, preserving the Gulf's alumina refinery royalty at `L1560 ("bauxite comes from Arkansas")` and respecting the 1937 Hayama Annex bar at `L1600 ("builds no aluminium or heavy electrical industry")`), and **Sulawesi nickel**. Because Shell's existing fields remain untouched and the new East Borneo blocks produce nothing in 1932, **no guaranteed Indies crude tonnage is promised in 1932**; Japanese lifting of Indies crude grows only as the newly awarded East Borneo concessions come into production (with ordinary commercial spot and bunkering purchases on published terms in the interim).
  3. **Open Straits & Commercial Bunkering:** Sunda, Lombok, and Makassar Straits kept open for peaceful commerce. The clause is scheduled to Article II of the Final Act, with Malacca (`B2`). The Netherlands signs the Final Act for these straits; whether it also signs Article I is `CR-086`'s.
  4. **No tariff concession on sugar (`L1693 ("Pacifica raises neither tariffs nor taxes")`):** No new tariff wall is raised; existing duties and Formosa's contracts (`L1541 ("sugar and rice go to Japan on long contracts")`) stand unchanged, while Japanese houses buy Java sugar strictly **in bond for third markets** (China and the Middle East) alongside **Sumatra rubber** and **Bangka tin** contracts.
  5. **Post-Ratification Commercial Indies Treasury Bond (Autumn 1932 — no Tokyo gold lending):** Because Japan is still repaying San Francisco's 1927 rescue loan through 1933 (`L1589 ("five instalments from 1929 to 1933")`) and Takahashi was refused dollars for Japan's villages (`L1695`), Tokyo banks do not lend gold dollars to Batavia. After the Netherlands **States General** ratifies the Compact in autumn 1932, the Indies colonial treasury places an ordinary **commercial bond issue in San Francisco and Amsterdam** with **no Concord or Realm state guarantee** (obeying `L892 ("treaty negotiation with Pacifica")`). Indonesian nationalist circles (and critics in Manila and Nanjing) denounce any settlement that steadies de Jonge's treasury.

**C2. What the Batavia Compact and the Labuan lease themselves give Japan (`T-L1` Case A, `T-O1` Case A, `T-R1`).**
The renewal of 1932, the May Hayama Council, the Navy seat, who carries Japan's terms and how Tokyo's factions receive them are `CR-085`'s. This section lists only what the two instruments of this CR give Japan by their own text.
1. **By the Batavia Compact:** two leased commercial loading ports in the Indies (Bintan and the new East Borneo port) with Japanese company and consular guards inside the fences, and long concessions for Japanese consortia in East Borneo oil, Sulawesi nickel and, after 1935, Bintan bauxite. These are held by Japanese houses under a Dutch treaty. They do not depend on the Concord's command arrangements.
2. **By the Labuan lease:** the lessee is the Concord, `PAC` and `JP` jointly, so Japanese ships of the common Navy use the harbour by right of the lease. The lease reserves no squadron and no commandant to Japan (`T-L1`, Case A approved). How command at Labuan is arranged inside the common Navy is `CR-085`'s.
3. **By neither instrument:** an Imperial renunciation of the south. `CR-082`'s proposed southern renunciation (`L1885 ("southern renunciation")`) is withdrawn. Japan is bound in the south only by the Compact's respect for Dutch sovereignty outside the fences, the bar on fortifying the piers and the 10° S non-basing line (`C3`).
- **Julian's real cost (`T-O1`, Case A approved):** direct concessions and a leased East Borneo loading port give Japan its first oil foothold outside Pacifica and the Gulf. Julian accepts the gradual erosion of his lever of 1931 (`L1720`) as the price of a Japan that gains by staying, and says so in the cabinet's minute (`A2a`). Pacifica's oil trust objects in cabinet and on the Senate floor and loses both times (`A2a`, `A3`).

**C3. Australia and New Zealand: The 10° S to 165° W Line (`C-1932-ANZ-DEFENSE`).**
Canon (`L1752 ("Australia and New Zealand agree")`) keeps `C-1932-ANZ-DEFENSE` bilateral (`ANZ`, `PAC`) with New Zealand's 1931 crop guarantee (`C-1932-NZ-CROP-GUARANTEE`) and Australia's crop guarantee waiting on Ottawa (`L1752 ("imperial preference agreed at Ottawa")`).
- **What Wellington and Canberra win—and what Canberra loses (PROPOSED):**
  1. **Wellington's win:** Britain rebates New Zealand's share of the £2,113,000 Singapore contribution (`B2`), and **Fiji, Ocean Island, and NZ's Tokelau remain untouched**.
  2. **Canberra's two wins:** (a) The **10° S Non-Basing Line from Timor across the Arafura Sea, Torres Strait, and Coral Sea to 165° W** (covering Fiji, Tonga at ~175° W, and Tutuila at ~170° W—grandfathering Tutuila as a pre-1932 light station without capital-ship fortification, `L1127 ("Tutuila through the Samoan settlements")`); (b) an explicit treaty clause (mirrored in `C-1932-BATAVIA-COMPACT`) forbidding **naval fortifications or warships permanently berthed at the leased Bintan and East Borneo commercial loading ports** (both sitting well north of the 10° S line).
  3. **Canberra's loss:** Australia demanded a total ban on Japanese commercial port leases in the Indies and a shipping cap north of Timor; Pacifica and Tokyo refuse both, offering the 10° S line and the non-fortification clause instead.
