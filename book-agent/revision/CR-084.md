**A2a. The cabinet's statement of the Realm's own interest (Thalassia, winter 1931–32; PROPOSED).**
The Realm alone gives up the gold, and the Concord shares what the gold buys. Before the Foreign Minister opens the talks, Julian's cabinet therefore writes down, in one minute, what the Realm is buying for itself. Like the conclusions drawn from the Office's tables (`L1020`), the minute is a cabinet paper and is never published. It makes four statements.
1. **The straits.** The Realm's oil from the concessions of the 1920s, its rubber and its tin already come through four straits it does not hold, and a decade on part of its bread will. A treaty that binds the two powers on their shores to keep them open in peace is worth more to the Realm than a base at Singapore, which would make the Realm the policeman of Malaya and the enemy of Nanjing at Hong Kong.
2. **Britain's signature.** The Works reckoned in 1928 that Britain could have a device about 1933–1935 (`L1614 ("fully committed from 1928")`). A Britain that stops for a price it is glad to take costs the Realm less than the plant it would otherwise build to stay ahead of a Britain that does not stop. The minute says only that the annuities buy "Britain's signature under the first article". It records nothing of what the Works holds.
3. **The cable and the corridor.** Fanning, the Phoenix titles and the air stations between Hawaii, Tutuila and Australia come under a trust in which the Realm sits.
4. **Japan's oil.** A Japan with oil of its own in East Borneo is a Japan that stays in the Concord because it gains by staying. The cabinet records that this spends part of the lever of 1931 (`L1720 ("oil from Pacifica and the Gulf Republic")`) and that it does so knowingly.

**The oil trust objects and loses.** The cabinet hears the president of Pacifica's oil trust (name **OPEN**) before it settles the minute. His case is that the trust pays twice: the Batavia Compact gives Japanese houses crude of their own in the trust's best market, and the Gulf's undertaking (`B3.4`) binds the trust for five years to lift dear Gulf crude that the Seria offtake would otherwise displace. He asks for one of two things, a share for the trust in every Japanese concession in East Borneo or release from the Gulf undertaking. Julian refuses both. The first would keep the lever the fourth statement has decided to spend, and the second is the Gulf's price and is not the Realm's to waive. The trust is left with the Seria offtake and the right to bid on Brunei's new blocks at auction like anyone else, and it carries its objection to the Senate, where it loses again (`A3`). The aluminium champion's president is heard on the same day about Bintan: a Japanese bauxite port for fifty years is his loss after the first review of 1937 (`CR-085` `B2`), and he too is overruled.

## C. The Batavia Compact (Rung B: Concessions and Leased Ports), Japan's Southern Gain and the 10° S Line

**C1. The Batavia Compact: Concessions, Two Leased Commercial Ports, and de Jonge's Damage Limitation (`C-1932-BATAVIA-COMPACT`; restores author's 22 Nov Rung B decision).**
- **Dutch motives (*Hist.*):** Prime Minister **Charles Ruijs de Beerenbrouck's** cabinet (1929–33) faces collapsing Indies colonial revenues and estate exports (Java sugar under the 1931 Chadbourne scheme and rising Indian duties, Sumatra rubber, Bangka/Belitung tin) while Britain permanently halts Sembawang. What brings The Hague to sign is first what it is offered in a slump: Java sugar bought in bond, rubber and tin contracts, and a market for an Indies bond (figures when the workbook has them). The second reason is not spoken by either side: with no European navy left east of Ceylon, the only power that could take the Indies is the Concord itself, and a ratified treaty with fixed fences is the best protection against the partner that signs it.
- **Governor-General B. C. de Jonge's stand — limiting the damage (PROPOSED):** **Bonifacius Cornelis de Jonge** (*Hist.:* 1875–1958, Governor-General 12 Sept 1931 – 16 Sept 1936, former Minister of War and **BPM board member** via Hendrikus Colijn) fights the Compact hard. Because The Hague concedes two fenced commercial ports and wide new mineral blocks, Batavia gains no new sovereign rights—de Jonge limits the damage by holding four lines the Netherlands already possessed:
  1. **No annexation, no protectorate, no military guarantee:** The Hague declines any military guarantee as incompatible with Dutch neutrality. The Concord pledges strictly **non-annexation and respect for Dutch sovereignty outside the leased fences**, with **zero internal colonial policing** (`L1109 ("foreign invasion, not from their own people")`, `C-1888-COMPANIES-ACT`);
  2. **Royal Dutch Shell's (BPM) existing Tarakan, Balikpapan, and South Sumatra fields/refineries—and the Union's NKPM Sumatra holdings (PROPOSED, reconciling `L1337 ("giant refinery without crude")`)—stay completely untouched**;
  3. **The Dutch KPM inter-island shipping monopoly stays closed** (defeating Japanese shipping lines' commercial demand);
  4. **Dutch New Guinea stays closed** (protecting de Jonge's internment camp for Indonesian nationalists at **Boven-Digoel**, *Hist.*).
- **What the Concord and Japan win (Rung B — concessions plus leased commercial ports, `T-NF1`):**
  1. **Two Fenced Commercial Oil and Bauxite Leased Ports (50-year commercial leases under a ratified Dutch treaty):** One **bauxite loading port on Bintan Island** (*Hist.:* bauxite found in the Riau Islands in 1924, mined in the 1930s mostly for Japan; ramping post-1935) and **one new East Borneo oil loading port** (serving newly allocated East Borneo blocks outside Shell's Balikpapan/Tarakan titles—preventing any clash with London/Shell). Inside the fenced loading grounds, **unarmed Japanese company watchmen keep the grounds, and Dutch field police stand at the gate. The guards are the point de Jonge half wins: Tokyo asked for armed consular guards and the Japanese navy for a fortified pier, and both are refused**; **no naval fortification is permitted** (answering Canberra, `C3`), and Dutch sovereignty and Dutch courts govern everywhere outside the fences.
  2. **Oil & Mineral Concessions:** Open-door exploration and production concessions on published royalties in **unallocated East Borneo oil blocks**, **Bintan Island bauxite** (development from 1932, post-1935 deliveries strictly as *additional* tonnage on top of Arkansas bauxite, preserving the Gulf's alumina refinery royalty at `L1560 ("bauxite comes from Arkansas")` and respecting the 1937 Hayama Annex bar at `L1600 ("builds no aluminium or heavy electrical industry")`), and **Sulawesi nickel**. Because Shell's existing fields remain untouched and the new East Borneo blocks produce nothing in 1932, **no guaranteed Indies crude tonnage is promised in 1932**; Japanese lifting of Indies crude grows only as the newly awarded East Borneo concessions come into production (with ordinary commercial spot and bunkering purchases on published terms in the interim).
  3. **Open Straits & Commercial Bunkering:** Sunda, Lombok, and Makassar Straits kept open for peaceful commerce. The clause is scheduled to Article II of the Final Act, with Malacca (`B2`). The Compact is signed in July, so on 1 August the Netherlands signs the Final Act's schedule for these straits as a party to an annexed treaty, with ratification pending; whether it also signs Article I is `CR-086`'s. Scheduling adds one thing to clauses already in two treaties: a complaint about the straits can be taken to the arbitration court by any signatory of the Final Act.
  4. **No tariff concession on sugar (`L1693 ("Pacifica raises neither tariffs nor taxes")`):** No new tariff wall is raised; existing duties and Formosa's contracts (`L1541 ("sugar and rice go to Japan on long contracts")`) stand unchanged, while Japanese houses buy Java sugar strictly **in bond for third markets** (China and the Middle East) alongside **Sumatra rubber** and **Bangka tin** contracts.
  5. **Post-Ratification Commercial Indies Treasury Bond (Autumn 1932 — no Tokyo gold lending):** Because Japan is still repaying San Francisco's 1927 rescue loan through 1933 (`L1589 ("five instalments from 1929 to 1933")`) and Takahashi was refused dollars for Japan's villages (`L1695`), Tokyo banks do not lend gold dollars to Batavia. After the Netherlands **States General** ratifies the Compact in autumn 1932, the Indies colonial treasury places an ordinary **commercial bond issue in San Francisco and Amsterdam** with **no Concord or Realm state guarantee** (obeying `L892 ("treaty negotiation with Pacifica")`). Indonesian nationalist circles (and critics in Manila and Nanjing) denounce any settlement that steadies de Jonge's treasury.

**C2. What the Batavia Compact and the Labuan lease themselves give Japan (`T-L1` Case A, `T-O1` Case A, `T-R1`).**
The renewal of 1932, the May Hayama Council, the Navy seat, who carries Japan's terms and how Tokyo's factions receive them are `CR-085`'s. This section lists only what the two instruments of this CR give Japan by their own text.
1. **By the Batavia Compact:** two leased commercial loading ports in the Indies (Bintan and the new East Borneo port) with unarmed Japanese company watchmen inside the fences and Dutch police at the gate, and long concessions for Japanese consortia in East Borneo oil, Sulawesi nickel and, after 1935, Bintan bauxite. These are held by Japanese houses under a Dutch treaty. They do not depend on the Concord's command arrangements.
2. **By the Labuan lease:** the lessee is the Concord, `PAC` and `JP` jointly, so Japanese ships of the common Navy use the harbour by right of the lease. The lease reserves no squadron and no commandant to Japan (`T-L1`, Case A approved). 3. **By neither instrument:** an Imperial renunciation of the south. `CR-082`'s proposed southern renunciation (`L1885 ("southern renunciation")`) is withdrawn. - **Julian's real cost (`T-O1`, Case A approved):** direct concessions and a leased East Borneo loading port give Japan its first oil foothold outside Pacifica and the Gulf. Julian accepts the gradual erosion of his lever of 1931 (`L1720`) as the price of a Japan that gains by staying, and says so in the cabinet's minute (`A2a`). **C3. Australia and New Zealand: The 10° S Line (`C-1932-ANZ-DEFENSE` and the Concord's protocol).**
Canon (`L1752 ("Australia and New Zealand agree")`) keeps `C-1932-ANZ-DEFENSE` bilateral (`ANZ`, `PAC`) with New Zealand's 1931 crop guarantee (`C-1932-NZ-CROP-GUARANTEE`) and Australia's crop guarantee waiting on Ottawa (`L1752 ("imperial preference agreed at Ottawa")`).
- **What Wellington and Canberra win—and what Canberra loses (PROPOSED):**
  1. **Wellington's win:** Britain rebates New Zealand's own contribution to the Singapore base (`B2`; sum OPEN), and **Fiji, Ocean Island, and NZ's Tokelau remain untouched**.
  2. **Canberra's two wins:** (a) The **10° S non-basing line**. It is written as a protocol to `C-1932-UK-RETRENCHMENT`, signed by the Concord (`CROWN` and `JP`) and by Britain, and Australia and New Zealand accede to it; a treaty of the Realm alone could bind neither Japan nor the common Navy (`L1597`). The Concord stations no warship and builds no base south of 10° S between Timor and 160° W, which takes in Fiji, Tonga and the Cook Islands, and it undertakes to seek none in Portuguese Timor, which lies north of the line. Tutuila's existing wireless and coaling station and Swains, both Pacifican before 1932 (`L1127 ("Tutuila through the Samoan settlements")`), are named as exceptions. (b) An explicit treaty clause (mirrored in `C-1932-BATAVIA-COMPACT`) forbidding **naval fortifications or warships permanently berthed at the leased Bintan and East Borneo commercial loading ports**. Who holds the former German New Guinea and Rabaul is not settled in canon (`L1316 ("Pacific islands and Tsingtao")`, PROPOSED); if the Concord holds Rabaul, the line is worth less to Canberra, and this is left **OPEN**.
  3. **Canberra's loss:** Australia demanded a total ban on Japanese commercial port leases in the Indies and a shipping cap north of Timor; Pacifica and Tokyo refuse both, offering the 10° S line and the non-fortification clause instead. Canberra and Wellington have two further grievances and get nothing for them. With Fanning here and Bamfield in `CR-083`, both ends of the first span of the Pacific cable pass out of British hands in one season.
