**B3. Borneo: Britain keeps Brunei's Residency, Labuan votes on retrocession, and Vyner Brooke refuses Limbang.**
- **The legal baseline (*Hist.* and `CR-074` M1).** Under the protectorate agreements of 1888 Brunei and the Raj of Sarawak are British-protected states (`CR-029` A42 and `CR-074` M1: "Britain keeps Labuan, Sarawak and Brunei"). Labuan was ceded by Brunei under the Treaty of Labuan of 18 December 1846, made a Crown colony in 1848 and joined to the Straits Settlements on 1 January 1907. **B4. Fanning, Washington, the remaining Phoenix claims, and the Kingdom of Tonga.**
- **What stays British or New Zealand's.** Fiji, a Crown colony since 1874 with its Great Council of Chiefs since 1876; the British Solomon Islands; the New Hebrides, an Anglo-French condominium since 1906; Pitcairn; the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony proper, with Ocean Island, its headquarters from 1908 to 1942 and the phosphate hub of Australia and New Zealand, so that Britain's administration and its pool of labour are left whole; and Tokelau, the Union Islands, under New Zealand's administration since 11 February 1926 (*Hist.*), which is expressly excluded.
- **What Britain detaches (`OPEN-019-05`), all of it on the cable and air corridor from Hawaii by Tutuila to Australia.**
  1. **Fanning Island and Washington Island (`FANNING`).** Britain formally detaches them from the Gilbert and Ellice Islands Colony, to which they were added on 27 January 1916 (*Hist.*). Neither atoll has an indigenous polity. Both are private copra estates, worked by Gilbertese contract labourers brought in for the purpose, beside the cable staff. So there is no vote of a people to take, and the matter is handled for what it is, in three commercial and labour instruments.
     - *The plantation is bought out or re-leased (`FANNING-ESTATE`).* In history Father Emmanuel Rougier bought the two islands in 1907 and sold them in October 1911 to a Canadian syndicate, Fanning Island Limited; Burns Philp bought them in 1935–36 (*Hist.*). Whether Burns Philp held a mortgage or an agency before then is not verified. Ka Lōkahi buys the estate from Fanning Island Limited, or re-leases it, at a published valuation, on `CR-073`'s pattern. The plantation's owner is thus a party in its own name.
     - *A compact on contract labour and repatriation (`FANNING`).* The Gilbertese contract workers receive the protections of Pacifica's labour article of 1887 (`CR-071`). They also get a free choice, paid for, between passage home to the Gilberts and staying on at free wages under Ka Lōkahi's trust charter. Under that charter land is leased and never sold; the Office of Public Measures publishes accounts each year; naval and cable stations are held by lease only; and petitions are heard at a review every ten years (`T-K1`, Case B, approved).
     - *The cable is leased back.* Fanning's Pacific Cable station (`L1139 ("British Fanning as the station")`) is leased back to Imperial and International Communications Ltd., whose payment for the cable bore interest from 1 April 1928 (*Hist.*). That breaks Britain's rule of an all-British Pacific cable, over the Admiralty's protest.
  2. **The remaining Phoenix claims are quitclaimed.** Canon already made Kiritimati, Swains and the Phoenix islands of the former American guano bonds Pacifican in 1870–99 (`L700 ("Kiritimati")`, `L1139`). Britain quitclaims its remaining overlapping claims in the Phoenix group, to Canton, Gardner, Hull and Sydney, which confirms the titles along the air corridor.

## C. The Batavia Compact (Rung B: Concessions and Leased Ports), Japan's Southern Gain and the 10° S Line

**C1. The Batavia Compact: concessions, two leased commercial ports, and how de Jonge limits the damage (`C-1932-BATAVIA-COMPACT`; restores the author's Rung B decision of 22 November).**
- **Why the Dutch sign.** The slump is history, and so is the cabinet: Prime Minister Charles Ruijs de Beerenbrouck's, of 1929 to 1933 (*Hist.*). It faces collapsing revenues in the Indies and collapsing estate exports: Java sugar, under the Chadbourne scheme of 1931 and rising Indian duties; Sumatra rubber; the tin of Bangka and Belitung (*Hist.*). Meanwhile Britain halts Sembawang for good. What follows is PROPOSED. The first thing that brings The Hague to sign is what it is offered in a slump: Java sugar bought in bond, contracts for rubber and tin, and a market for an Indies bond (figures when the workbook has them). The second reason is spoken by neither side. With no European battle fleet left east of Ceylon, the only power that could take the Indies is the Concord itself, and a ratified treaty with fixed fences is the best protection against the partner that signs it.
- **Governor-General de Jonge's stand (PROPOSED).** Bonifacius Cornelis de Jonge (1875–1958; Governor-General from 12 September 1931 to 16 September 1936; a former Minister of War, and on BPM's board through Hendrikus Colijn; *Hist.*) fights the Compact hard. The Hague concedes two fenced commercial ports and wide new mineral blocks, and Batavia gains no new sovereign right. De Jonge limits the damage by holding four lines the Netherlands already had.
  1. **No annexation, no protectorate, no military guarantee.** The Hague declines any military guarantee as incompatible with Dutch neutrality. The Concord pledges only that it will not annex and that it respects Dutch sovereignty outside the leased fences. It does no policing inside the colony (`L1109 ("foreign invasion, not from their own people")`, `C-1888-COMPANIES-ACT`).
  2. **Shell's fields are not touched.** Royal Dutch Shell's existing fields and refineries at Tarakan, Balikpapan and in South Sumatra, held through BPM, stay exactly as they are. So do the Union's NKPM holdings in Sumatra (PROPOSED, which reconciles `L1337 ("giant refinery without crude")`).
  3. **The KPM's monopoly of inter-island shipping stays closed.** That defeats what the Japanese shipping lines asked for.
  4. **Dutch New Guinea stays closed.** That protects de Jonge's internment camp for Indonesian nationalists at Boven-Digoel (*Hist.*).
- **What the Concord and Japan win (Rung B: concessions and leased commercial ports; `T-NF1`).**
  1. **Two fenced commercial ports, leased for fifty years under a ratified Dutch treaty.** One is a port for loading bauxite on Bintan Island. In history bauxite was found in the Riau Islands in 1924 and mined in the 1930s, mostly for Japan, with output rising after 1935 (*Hist.*). The other is a new port for loading oil in East Borneo. It serves newly allocated blocks outside Shell's titles at Balikpapan and Tarakan, so there is no clash with London or with Shell. Inside the fences unarmed watchmen of the Japanese companies keep the grounds, and Dutch field police stand at the gate. The guards are the point de Jonge half wins: Tokyo asked for armed consular guards, and the Japanese naval party for a fortified pier, and both are refused. No naval fortification is permitted, which answers Canberra (`C3`). Outside the fences Dutch sovereignty and Dutch courts govern everywhere.
  2. **Concessions for oil and minerals.** They are open-door concessions to explore and produce, on published royalties, in three places: unallocated oil blocks in East Borneo; the bauxite of Bintan Island; and the nickel of Sulawesi. Bintan is developed from 1932 and delivers after 1935, and its tonnage is additional to Arkansas bauxite. That keeps the Gulf's alumina refinery its royalty (`L1560 ("bauxite comes from Arkansas")`) and respects the Hayama annex's bar to 1937 (`L1600 ("builds no aluminium or heavy electrical industry")`). Shell's existing fields are untouched and the new East Borneo blocks produce nothing in 1932, so no tonnage of Indies crude is promised in 1932. Japan's lifting of Indies crude grows only as the new concessions come into production. Until then Japanese houses buy on the spot market and bunker on published terms, as any merchant does.
  3. **Open straits and commercial bunkering.** The Sunda, Lombok and Makassar straits and the Dutch shore of Malacca are kept open to peaceful commerce. The schedule binds the powers on the shores and the Concord. Any signatory of the Final Act may complain of a closure to its court, and none but those four is bound by it. The clause is scheduled to Article II of the Final Act, with Malacca (`B2`). The Compact is signed in July, so on 1 August the Netherlands signs the Final Act's schedule for these straits as a party to an annexed treaty, with its ratification pending; whether it also signs Article I is `CR-086`'s. Scheduling adds one thing to clauses that are already in two treaties: a complaint about the straits can be taken to the arbitration court by any signatory of the Final Act.
  4. **No tariff concession on sugar (`L1693 ("Pacifica raises neither tariffs nor taxes")`).** No new tariff wall is raised. Existing duties and Formosa's contracts (`L1541 ("sugar and rice go to Japan on long contracts")`) stand as they are. Japanese houses buy Java sugar in bond only, for third markets in China and the Middle East, and with it take contracts for Sumatra rubber and Bangka tin.
  5. **A commercial bond of the Indies treasury after ratification, in the autumn of 1932, and no gold lent from Tokyo.** Japan is still repaying San Francisco's rescue loan of 1927, through 1933 (`L1589 ("five instalments from 1929 to 1933")`), and Takahashi was refused dollars for Japan's villages (`L1695`). So Tokyo's banks lend Batavia no gold dollars. After the Netherlands **States General** approve the Compact in the autumn of 1932, the Indies colonial treasury places an ordinary commercial bond issue in San Francisco and Amsterdam. Neither the Concord nor the Realm guarantees it, as `L892 ("treaty negotiation with Pacifica")` requires. Indonesian nationalist circles, and critics in Manila and Nanjing, denounce any settlement that steadies de Jonge's treasury.
