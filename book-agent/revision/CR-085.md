## A. May 1932: the Turn, and a Renewal Without Term

**A1. The winter's warning (1931–32; PROPOSED).**
In 1931 Julian stopped the Manchurian plot by threatening three things Tokyo could not easily replace: "oil from Pacifica and the Gulf Republic, transport by the common navy and the yen's convertibility through common finance" (`L1720 ("oil from Pacifica and the Gulf Republic")`). In the winter that follows, the sufficers in the Senate's aliʻi seats count what is left of them. Their reason for counting is their own doctrine: a Realm that means to buy its bread abroad must not also depend on a partner who can walk away. The Batavia talks are offering Japanese houses oil of their own in East Borneo (`CR-084`; the Compact is not initialled until June). Takahashi, refused dollars that winter, is asking for an adjustable yen (`A7`). If both are granted only the transports remain. Their warning to the cabinet is that a Japan which needs neither Pacifica's oil nor its gold, and which "may refuse in 1937" (`L1734 ("Japan may refuse in 1937")`), will refuse. Julian has no answer that winter except that a partner held only by levers is not a partner. His cabinet writes its answer down in March (`CR-084` `A2a`), and May gives a better one.
- **What a refusal in 1937 would have meant (PROPOSED).** Canon gave the union no term; what had terms was the executive trust (the ledger's note on `C-1927-CONCORD` says so). In law a refusal in 1937 would have ended only Pacifica's hold on the executive. Both capitals take it to mean more. A union with no agreed executive has no one to name the common ministers, order the fleet or answer for the reserve compact, so a refusal is exit in fact though not in law. That is the sense in which Japan's right to leave, dissolution and what would have been revocable are meant here.
- **Japan's consent in principle to the first article (winter; PROPOSED).** The draft article is shown to Tokyo before it is shown to London. Inukai's cabinet, formed in December 1931 (*Hist.*), consents in principle through Prince Chichibu. It costs Japan nothing it has: under the fuel lease it returns its fuel already and has no use for a separating plant; the bar itself is written in 1932 (`C2`). The army ministry's objection is to the other article and comes in June.

**A2. The Kansai station: decided in the winter, announced on 29 April 1932 (APPROVED, item 85-3; Wigner's condition as reworded here APPROVED, `R-7`).**
The Tokyo station "is opened on 29 April 1932, the Emperor's birthday" (`L1634 ("station is opened on 29 April 1932")`). A second station for Osaka and Kobe is Julian's to give, as Tokyo was his to announce at the demonstration of 1927, and he decides it in the winter with the consent of Langmuir's seat and the Council of the Seal (`C2`).
- **Who objects, and loses.** **Eugene Wigner** (`CT-EUGENE-WIGNER`) holds that a second station in an earthquake country should wait until the first has run five years. He loses on the decision and wins one thing. Its cost is the rule for restarting and the published shutdowns, more than the year itself, which the building calendar would nearly have given anyway: no ground is broken at Kansai until Tokyo has run a full year at rated power from its opening, with every shutdown published. A shutdown of more than thirty days restarts the year; shorter ones are published and counted. Ground is therefore broken no earlier than May 1933.
- **The announcement.** At Tokyo's opening the Emperor's household and Julian announce the second station, by radiotelephone, on the link Thalassia has had to Tokyo since 1928 (`L1625 ("from 1928 Thalassia telephones Tokyo")`). The world sees what the atom gives a partner two weeks before it sees whether the partnership holds. The Restoration officers see it too: a second station tied to a lease from Thalassia is, to them, a second chain.
- Kansai is therefore no part of what is traded at Hayama. The Council only charters the Board that will build it.

**A3. May 1932: the coup fails, and Japan proposes a renewal without term (the two cabinets and the dates APPROVED, `P-0d`; the items of rows R-7 to R-10 APPROVED; the rest PROPOSED).**
The officers strike "to stop the Concord's first renewal", and "Emperor Shōwa and the navy stand against them, and the coup fails" (`L1732 ("merchant houses that share in its champions")`). Canon leaves Inukai's fate open; here Inukai is killed on 15 May 1932, as in history (`T-I1`, decided). **Takahashi** is acting prime minister from that night, and on 26 May Admiral **Saitō Makoto** forms a cabinet in which Takahashi keeps Finance (*Hist.* for both). The proposal that follows is resolved by the first cabinet and confirmed by the second. (`L1597` requires the prime minister's countersignature only when Julian appoints the three common ministers; `CR-084` `A1` reads it the same way.)
- **The dates (APPROVED, `P-0d`; the particular days about the cable and the sailing PROPOSED).** The coup is on 15 May (*Hist.* for the day; canon gives the month). The arrests are made within the week; the trials come later. Takahashi's caretaker cabinet resolves on the offer in the week after the coup and leaves it for its successor. Saitō's cabinet confirms it on 26 May, the day it is formed, and it is cabled to Chichibu on about 28 May. Julian sails in the first days of June and the Council sits at Hayama from 12 to 15 June, a week later than first planned. Canon's proposal that Vance is killed in May by headquarters guards (`L1736 ("Vance dies in May")`, PROPOSED and open) would fall in these weeks; if it stands, Julian leaves Thalassia days after burying his foster brother, and that is not decided here.
- **Three institutions of Japan carry the renewal.**
  - *The sovereign.* The Emperor's stand in May is his own. He is not asked by Thalassia, and Julian sends no ship.
  - *The navy.* Canon is read here as giving Japan no separate navy after 1927 (PROPOSED reading): the Navy is the Concord's (`L1597`), with the western squadron reserved to the Japanese naval party until 1932 and overseas commands that are Japan's until then (`L1734`). Two parts of it stand against the officers: the stations and yards in Japan, which Japanese officers command (APPROVED, `R-7`), and the Japanese units of the western squadron, which Kiyoshi's son holds loyal (`L1732`). The naval party that is about to lose its reserved squadron is the party that saves the Concord, and its claim to the Navy seat rests on that (`A4`).
  - *The Diet.* In June it votes its quota (`L1593 ("Diet votes its quota")`) and chooses its sixty delegates. It does not do so in silence: a deputy, **Mori Kaku** of the Seiyūkai (*Hist.*), chief secretary of the cabinet whose premier was shot and out of office since 26 May, says from the floor that a Concord without term is the bakufu made permanent, and is voted down (PROPOSED).
- **Who makes the offer.** The officers had a date to strike at, the renewal of 1932, and their successors would have the next one. **A7. The yen's adjustable parity at Thalassia, and what it costs (`L1562`, `L1589`, `L1693`, `L1695`, `L1720`; `C-1932-ADJUSTABLE-PARITY`).**
Canon already has the monetary climax. Pacifica's falling prices drag Japan's down while rural debts stay fixed (`L1562 ("At gold parity Japan's prices must follow Pacifica's down")`). Japan stays on gold through the winter of 1931–32 under common finance (`L1693 ("Japan remains on gold through common finance")`) while the Reserve Bank refuses to print (`L1695`), and that feeds the manifesto of May's coup (`L1732`). Then Tokyo wins "the adjustable parity it wins at Thalassia" (`L1695 ("adjustable parity it wins at Thalassia")`). Canon states the conference's adjustable gold-dollar order for the Union's currency.

- **Where Takahashi is.** At Hayama on 12–15 June; in Tokyo for the Diet and the army ministry until mid-July, while his vice-minister negotiates the instrument at Thalassia; at Thalassia from about 27 July, with the Emperor's instruments, to sign it and to stand in the hall on 1 August (APPROVED, `R-7`).
- **The instrument.** It is negotiated in July 1932 with the common Finance minister. John U. Calkins, the Reserve Bank's governor, and Kashevarov, the head of its Russian department (`L1607`), advise on the gold cover. In history Japan embargoed gold on its own on 13 December 1931, and the yen fell by about 60 per cent in disorder (*Hist.*). Here Takahashi secures an orderly reset of the yen's parity, 20 to 25 per cent downward (`C-1932-ADJUSTABLE-PARITY`; the range is an estimate, PROPOSED, until the workbook sizes it), inside a consultation band of 5 per cent either way under Common Finance (PROPOSED). `C-1927-CONCORD-RESERVES` (`L1597 ("Japan's gold in San Francisco is earmarked")`) is not touched: Japan's earmarked gold can still be withdrawn without notice, and its dollar balances in San Francisco keep their 50 per cent gold backing.
- **What it costs (PROPOSED).** There are eight costs, and nobody escapes them.
  1. *The quota costs more yen.* If Japan's 35 per cent quota is payable in gold dollars (PROPOSED; canon does not say in what money it is paid), a parity 20 to 25 per cent lower raises the Diet's yen appropriation for defence and the Concord by about 25 to 33 per cent. The Diet fights over it, rural relief against naval taxes.
  2. *The banks' debt costs more yen (`C-1927-JP-RESCUE`).* Under `L1589 ("five instalments from 1929 to 1933")` it is the rebuilt Japanese banks that owe the fourth instalment, in 1932, and the fifth, in 1933, in gold dollars; they owe them through the stabilization agency, and the sovereign budget does not owe them directly. The reset raises their load in yen by a quarter to a third. The stabilization agency and the Bank of Japan have to lend them yen so that they pay San Francisco on schedule and there is no new run.
  3. *The pool's inputs are dearer.* The Concord pool's aluminium and oil stay priced at gold parity (`L1691 ("on Arkansas bauxite at gold parity")`), so they cost more yen in Japan's shipyards and on the airframe lines at Nagoya and Ōta.
  4. *The Bank of Japan has a ceiling.* In history the yen floated. Here, inside a gold-exchange band of 5 per cent either way, the Bank of Japan's underwriting of bonds for rural relief drains Japan's earmarked gold in San Francisco if Takahashi issues too much. That is a strict ceiling on money, and Common Finance watches it.
  5. *Rural relief is incomplete.* Nagano's silk districts get part of what they need and no sudden prosperity, because the Union's depression has collapsed American demand for raw silk (`L1689 ("Japan's silk districts lose income")`).
  6. *China objects (`C-1930-TARIFF-NOTES`).* China's exchange of notes of 1930 binds its duties on Japanese cotton goods until May 1933 (`L1706`). A yen 20 to 25 per cent cheaper undercuts Shanghai's Chinese cotton mills. Nanjing and the Shanghai mill-owners protest at once, and in 1932 it becomes likely that China will not renew the notes in May 1933; canon says only that no renewal is promised.
  7. *One of Julian's levers of 1931 is loosened (`L1720`).* Adjustable parities loosen, on purpose, one of the three things Julian threatened Tokyo with in 1931: "the yen's convertibility through common finance". The oil lever is spent knowingly in `CR-084` `A2a`. Of the three levers of 1931 only the common Navy's transports remain; the fuel lease is a fourth hold, made since. Julian concedes the parity because rigid deflation on gold was handing recruits to Tokyo's Restoration officers.
  8. *The Realm pays too.* If the reset is 20 per cent, Japanese cloth and consumer goods become a fifth cheaper in the Realm's and the Concord's markets and Pacifican machinery a quarter dearer in Japan; if it is 25 per cent, a quarter and a third. The Union and Britain have a precedent made under their eyes at the conference.
- **The yen and the conference's rule.** `CR-086` `B1` states the conference's rule for every member: it names its parity and consults before changing it. The yen's band is the Concord's stricter case of that rule, and `C-1932-ADJUSTABLE-PARITY` is the only parity instrument between the two crowns.

## B. Closing `CR-054`'s Open Items and the Pool-Entry Protocol

**B1. The four open items of `CR-054`, settled (`L85`, `L1560`, `L1600`, `L1742`, `L1877`).**

Until that review it also keeps to the single party toward outsiders in the European nitrogen convention (`L1600 ("European nitrogen convention")`, `C-1930-NITROGEN-CONVENTION`).

**B2. The Pool-Entry Protocol (`T-H1`, `C-1932-HAYAMA-GRADUATION`; APPROVED with the 49% ventures and the first review as the day of entry, item 85-2; the share formula and where the ventures refine are OPEN).**
- **Entry does not wait on agreement.** Japanese smelters enter the pool on the day the first review opens, whether or not that review agrees on anything else. Of the annex of 1928 only the bar on Japanese aluminium and heavy electrical industry ends that day; its other terms continue and are open at the review like the rest (PROPOSED).
Canon's bar (`L1600`: Japan "builds no aluminium or heavy electrical industry against Pacifica's before 1937") was written for a Concord with a second term ending in 1937. What the protocol gives is the pool's inside price and a share of the Concord's orders on a fixed day.
- **1932 to the first review (mid-1937):** primary aluminium smelting in Japan stays barred, as `L1600` says. ## C. The Tokyo Atomic Station (1927–28 Financing), Nishina's Bond, and the Kansai Stake

**C1. How the 80 MW Tokyo atomic station is paid for, which canon left open (`L75`, `L1634`, `L1867`; `C-1928-TOKYO-STATION-FINANCING`, `C-1931-TOKYO-FUEL-LEASE`).**
Ground is broken in 1928 (`L1634 ("station is opened on 29 April 1932")`), so the financing is settled in late 1927 and early 1928 (`C-1928-TOKYO-STATION-FINANCING`, PROPOSED). That is just after Japan's banking panic of 1927 (`L1589`), when Japanese banks have no gold dollars to spare for buying a station outright. The arrangement has three parts.
1. **Japanese capital, in yen (`TOKYO-DENTO`).** The Tokyo Electric Light Company (*Tokyo Dentō*) and a syndicate of Japanese utility bonds finance and own the inland site on rock, above the reach of a tsunami and built to the Polytechnic's earthquake code (`L1634`), the civil containment, the cooling works, the steam turbines, the alternators and the ties to the grid. All of it is paid in yen, so that no gold leaves Japan at the start.
2. **The Works' part, on a lease of twenty years (`C-1931-TOKYO-FUEL-LEASE`, 1931–1951).** Pacifica's Light and Matter Works supplies the pile, the purified graphite, the control gear and the uranium charge, which is leased. It recovers its outlay through a lease of fuel and core charged by the kilowatt-hour for twenty years, from first power in 1931.
3. **What the lease depends on.** The lease runs only while Japan is a member of the Concord. If the union were ever dissolved, the Works' duty to deliver fresh cores would end, and all irradiated fuel would still return to the Works (`L1634 ("the bred elements never stay on its soil")`). Under a renewal without term this describes the dependence canon names and is no longer a cliff in 1937. It stays in force after the second record opens in the autumn of 1947 (`L1612`).

**C2. The Concord Atomic Power Board, Nishina's Divided Duty, and the Kansai 80 MW Station (`T-A1`, `C-1932-ATOMIC-POWER-BOARD`; APPROVED, 20:11 and item 85-3; the tariff's discount is OPEN).**
- **Authority inside Pacifica.** The Works' rules are in the Works' charter and the Polytechnic's statutes and in no treaty (`CR-050`). Pacifica's consent to the Board therefore comes from Langmuir's seat and the Council of the Seal (`L1643 ("Council of the Seal")`). Hayama does not override the Works.
- **No audit inside the seal of 1947 (`L1612`).** The Board never sees what uranium costs to mine, enrich or fabricate inside the walled Works. It publishes an external benchmark formula for the tariff, indexed only to Tokyo's published cost of generating from delivered steam coal, less a discount the Concord agrees. That answers the Restoration's charge that the fuel is priced at will, and it opens nothing behind the seal (`T-A1`).
- **No separating plant in Japan (a new term, PROPOSED).** The Board's charter, and from 1932 the fuel lease, forbid any plant in Japan for treating irradiated fuel. Canon's lease has only the return of the fuel (`L1634`); Japan's place in Article I rests on this term, so it is written down and not assumed.
- **Nishina Yoshio's divided duty (`CT-NISHINA-YOSHIO`).** He co-chairs the Board for Japan, beside a Works engineer of Eugene Wigner's school of reactor safety (`L1637 ("Wigner becomes the master of the reactors")`). He has signed his bond again, for twenty years to the autumn of 1947 (`L1612`). He sits as the civilian Tokyo trusts, and he is bound by oath never to tell his own government what lies inside the walled college or what Pacifica recovers from Japan's returned fuel.
- **The Kansai station (`KANSAI-UTILITIES`).** Julian decides it in the winter with the consent of Langmuir's seat and the Council of the Seal. It is announced at Tokyo's opening on 29 April 1932 (`A2`) and built by the Board chartered at the renewal. It is a second civilian atomic station, for Osaka and Kobe, of Tokyo's size: 80 MW (the Tokyo station's size is itself PROPOSED in canon). It is built from 1933, when Wigner's year has run (`A2`), to about 1938. It is financed as Tokyo was: a syndicate of Kansai's electric utilities owns the civil works and the turbines in yen, and the Works leases the pile and the fuel under its own radiation-safety code and the Polytechnic's earthquake code (`L1634`, `L1869`).
  - *For Japan.* Kansai's industrialists gain cheap power without coal, on a fuel lease that runs while Japan is in the Concord. Wigner's objection is overruled, and no ground is broken at Kansai until Tokyo has run its year (`A2`).
  - *For Pacifica.* Every Japanese station returns its spent fuel to Pacifica (`L1634`). Two Japanese power reactors of 80 MW therefore breed elements that go back into the Works' hands, and they draw on the Realm's existing uranium reserve of 4,600 tonnes (`L1646 ("lie in reserve in 1932")`).

## D. The Partners' Deferred Dossiers: Rice, Hokkaido, Warnings, Cable, Korea, and Kamchatka

**D1. Japan's rice crop in 1910, 1920 and 1932, Hokkaido early, and what it costs the Ainu (`CR-082` `R-6`, `L729`, `L735`, `L1541`, `L1706`).**
`CR-082` (`L1885`) asks the partners' CR to raise Japan's home rice crop above history's (MODEL; the percentages are `CR-082`'s, lines 203 and 291 of CR-082.md):
- by 5 per cent in 1910, about 2.5 million koku above 50.1 million;
- by 8 per cent in 1920, about 4.6 million koku above 57.5 million;
- by 13 per cent in 1932, about 7.8 million koku above 60.3 million, which keeps Japan within about 1 per cent of the 68.4 million koku its 62.2 million residents need, without annexed colonies.

It also asks this CR to carry the point that no shortage of rice brings riots in 1918. Each date needs means that exist in its own decade.

1. **1891 to 1910 (5 per cent, about 2.5 million koku).**
   - *Nitrogen and bean cake before 1910.* Long before Pacifica's Columbia ammonia of 1915, and before the Hayama annex of 1928 reserves Japan's fertilizer market, two things raise crops from the 1890s: Chilean nitrate, carried on Pacifica's Iquique contracts of 1891 (`CR-082` §D), and Manchurian soybean cake, moving at open rates (`CR-081`). The bean cake and fish meal go on the paddy. The nitrate goes on upland crops and mulberry, since nitrate is lost in flooded fields. What carries the cake to Japan before the rate rules of the 1920s is ordinary trade through the treaty ports.
   - *Hokkaido's advisers change their minds, and breeding starts early (`C-1893-HOKKAIDO-RICE`, PROPOSED).* Canon already links Pacifican advisers to Kuroda's Hokkaido (`L735 ("Kuroda Kiyotaka's Hokkaido development brings Pacifican advisers north")`) and ties Sapporo's agronomists to Sitka (`L729 ("Sapporo's agronomists and Sitka's assayers trade their methods")`). In history Hokkaido's development agency opposed rice on its American advisers' counsel until it opened a test station in 1893, after the pioneer Nakayama Kyūzō had proved *Akage* rice near Kitahiroshima in 1873 (*Hist.*), and local breeding in Hokkaido began only in 1915 (*Hist.*). In canon Pacifica's food table of 1890 (`L1020`) turns its Hokkaido advisers round by 1893. Hokkaido's test station and Sapporo Agricultural College work with Pacifica's stations and use winter nurseries in Hawaiʻi, which halves `CR-082`'s breeding cycle of 12 to 15 years to 6 to 8. The college's acting head is Satō Shōsuke (1856–1939), an agricultural economist and later the first president of Hokkaido Imperial University, from 1918 to 1930 (*Hist.*). Pure-line *Bōzu* selections, and cross-bred Hokkaido cultivars equivalent to *Hashiri-bōzu* and *Fukoku* (*Hist.*), therefore reach the fields of Hokkaido and Tōhoku from 1901 to 1903 onward and not before 1901. Hokkaido's paddy expands early, to roughly 100,000 to 130,000 chō, about 1.5 to 1.8 million koku by 1910 (MODEL); selection in Tōhoku and bean cake supply the rest. Only Hokkaido's excess over its historical crop counts toward the 5 per cent, because history's figure already contains Hokkaido's historical crop. The split between Hokkaido and the rest is **OPEN** until the series is in `population/provisioning.py`. Canon says only that Pacifican advisers went north. That they first opposed rice, as Capron did in history, is new here and PROPOSED.
   - *What it costs the Ainu (`L729`).* Paddy pushed across the Ishikari and Kamikawa plains a generation ahead of history speeds Japanese settlement on Ainu land. It deepens the quarrel Sitka's assayers already name at `L729`: "Sapporo settles its soldiers on Ainu land without asking". Pacifica's sufficers point to the contradiction. The Realm's own rule is that no land is granted on a nation's country without its compact (`CR-070`), and the Realm is subsidizing Hokkaido's expansion on ground the Ainu never ceded.
2. **1910 to 1920 (8 per cent, about 4.6 million koku; no riots over a shortage in 1918).**
   - Noguchi Shitagau (1873–1944, *Hist.*) founds Nippon Chisso (*Nichitsu*) in 1908 (*Hist.*) to make calcium cyanamide and ammonium sulfate on mountain water power, and buys the Casale ammonia process in 1921 (*Hist.*). From 1915 other Japanese firms join him, buying Pacifican hydro-turbines (`L1560`) and ammonia technology at the open royalty (`L863`). With Hokkaido's paddy, about 2.5 to 3.0 million koku by 1920 (MODEL), and Manchurian bean cake, Japan reaches 8 per cent by 1920. That prevents a physical shortage of rice from July to September 1918, so no cabinet in Tokyo falls to riots over a shortage of rice in 1918; whether prices and the army's buying still bring unrest that summer is not decided here.
3. **1920 to 1932 (13 per cent, about 7.8 million koku), with Formosa and Korea reconciled.**
   - Mori Nobuteru (1884–1941, *Hist.*) founds Showa Fertiliser in October 1928, and it makes ammonium sulfate by 1931 (*Hist.*) on Pacifican hydro-turbines (`L1560`). Korea is never annexed (`L1196 ("Korea remains an independent guaranteed state")`), so Japan never launches history's drive after 1920 to requisition colonial Korean rice (*Hist.*), which crushed Japanese farm prices and caused the stagnation of the 1920s that Hayami and Ruttan identified (*Hist.*). A crop 13 per cent above history's in 1932 is about 68.1 million koku. From the crop of 1920 given above that is growth of about 0.8 per cent a year, with Hokkaido contributing about 3.5 to 4.0 million koku (MODEL), and it is within about 1 per cent of the 68.4 million koku canon's Japan needs (`CR-082`).
   - *Formosa (`L1541`) and Korea (`L1706`).* Japan's home crop covers ordinary table rice in normal years. Formosa's long contracts (`L1541`) therefore carry mainly sugar, with specialty and reserve rice. Korea's commercial rice (`L1706`) is far smaller than history's colonial drain. It fills demand in Japan's cities and sake breweries and serves as a buffer in cold summers, which matters in a chill year such as 1931, and Korean merchants sell the rest of their surplus into North China and Manchuria. Korean landlords and Seoul's treasury still feel the narrower Japanese market, and that adds a real economic grievance to relations between Korea and Japan.
4. **The Pacific kelp and kombu research compact (`C-1932-KELP-RESEARCH`, `L1672`, PROPOSED).** In July 1932 Hokkaido Imperial University and Hokkaido's *kombu* growers sign a joint compact on marine agronomy with Pacifica's marine stations. It builds on what Hercules Powder's wartime potash and acetone plant at San Diego learned about harvesting kelp by machine (*Hist.*). It starts `CR-082`'s Work 5 and changes nothing in the diet tables of 1932.

**D2. The warnings compact of 1897: typhoon warnings northward, tsunami warnings outward (`C-1897-WARNINGS`, `L1131`, `L1631`, `L1659`).**
Typhoons form in the western Pacific and strike Guam, Manila and the Visayas first, before they recurve north toward Japan. Japan and Kamchatka sit on the Pacific trench, which sends tsunamis across the ocean toward Hawaiʻi. So the warnings run two ways.
- **Typhoons, from the Realm and the Philippines to Japan.** The Manila Observatory (`MANILA-OBS`) anchors the Realm's western typhoon network with Guam and Chichijima (`C-1892-TOKYO-CABLE`). In history the Jesuits founded it in 1865; Father Federico Faura gave the first recorded typhoon warning in the Far East on 7 July 1879; it was made official by decree in 1884; and from 1897 its director was Father José Algué, who invented the barocyclonometer (*Hist.* for all four). It gives Guam, Manila and the Visayas a day's warning (`L1131`) and wires storm tracks north to Japan. From 1923 Okada Takematsu (1874–1956; head of Japan's Central Meteorological Observatory, `CMO-JP`; *Hist.*) combines Manila's reports and ships' wireless reports with the Tokyo electronic calculator of 1931 (`L1631 ("one at Tokyo")`) to compute the tracks on which typhoons will strike Japan's coast.
- **Earthquakes and tsunamis, from Japan and Kamchatka to the Pacific.** News of the Sanriku tsunami of 15 June 1896 reached Honolulu by wire before the waves, when nobody there knew what it meant (`L1131`). After it Ōmori Fusakichi (1868–1923, *Hist.*; Omori's law and the Bosch–Omori seismograph) and `CMO-JP` wire warnings of offshore earthquakes and tide-gauge readings outward over both `C-1892-TOKYO-CABLE` and `C-1903-KURIL-CABLE`, including Petropavlovsk's seismic wires (`L1659`). The warnings go to Honolulu's Volcano Observatory and serve Hawaii's Harbour Act of 1913 (`C-1913-HARBOUR-ACT`).

**D3. Manila's direct submarine wire to Shanghai (1906; `C-1880-HK-MANILA-CABLE`, `C-1906-MANILA-SHANGHAI-CABLE`, PROPOSED).**
In history the Manila–Shanghai cable of 1906, laid by the *CS Silvertown* (*Hist.*), belonged to the Commercial Pacific Cable Company, which was half British Eastern Telegraph's, a quarter the Danish Great Northern's and a quarter Commercial Cable's. The reason was that Eastern Extension and Great Northern held exclusive concessions to land cables on China's coast, under agreements of 1899 and 1900 (*Hist.*).

In canon Edmund Fong heads the Chinese Maritime Customs Inspectorate (`L790 ("Hart takes the British ministership")`) and China leans toward Pacifica. The Qing Imperial Telegraph Administration refuses to extend the foreigners' exclusive right on the coast against a line that ends in Chinese hands. Under `C-1906-MANILA-SHANGHAI-CABLE`, signed and landed in 1906, the Realm's telegraph estate and the Philippine administration lay a sea cable of Carquinez gutta-percha (`L1131`) from Manila to a landing near the mouth of the Yangtze (the place is OPEN), where China's Imperial Telegraph Administration owns and operates the shore end and the Shanghai office. Traffic between Manila, Thalassia and China's treaty ports then passes round British Hong Kong, and no new foreign cable enclave is carved out of Shanghai. Britain's Bolinao cable of 1880 stays open as a commercial circuit (`L1131`).

**D4. Korea, Kamchatka and the deferred southern question (`L1196`, `L1674`, `L1885`).**
- **The Kamchatka Company (`C-1927-KAMCHATKA-CO`, still PROPOSED).** Its ledger entry is annotated with a renewable charter of twenty years, 1927 to 1947 (PROPOSED), given with the consent of the Republic of Kamchatka (`KAM`); one-third Pacifican equity; paid crews managed directly (`CR-031-R1`); and oversight of Korean contract workers by Tokyo, Seoul and Thalassia together (`L1674`).
- **The southern question (`L1885 ("Japan's yields, Hokkaido and any southern renunciation")`).** It is settled by `CR-084`, which merges first. Japan signs no renunciation of the south; it has leased piers and concessions under the Batavia Compact, unfortified, and no base south of 10° S. This CR recites that and decides none of it.
