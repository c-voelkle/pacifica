## Verdict

One more revision. The design is sound and the ending can be believed: a renunciation bought before the conference, a food pledge extracted by the sufficers, one open refusal and one sealed dissent. Six things stop integration: two canon lines read against their meaning (the food lever of 1919 and the prize of 1911), France both inside and outside the monetary settlement, a calendar that has London shown the article before Hayama agrees it and calls the act "ratified" on a day it is only signed, a chambers' bargain built on bills that the sibling drafts and this draft's own Part B say are not bills, and an ore check that is blind exactly where Part A says the ore was refused. None needs a new idea; most make the ending stronger when fixed.

## Blockers

**B1. "It has not used food as a weapon" is false in canon.**
- *Passage:* D2, line 175: "It has not used food as a weapon. In 1931 Julian threatened Tokyo with oil, transport and the yen, and 'Food is not among them'."
- *Problem:* `L1379`, under the comment "CR-051: the food lever is the ore lever": "At The Hague the man who decides where Pacifican food and credit go is Hoover, and starving Vienna and Bohemia and ruined Belgium negotiate with him. Told only that the Works values the uranium side of the two concessions and that Julian wants it secured, he secures it without asking why." Joachimsthal and Shinkolobwe, half of Part A's ore, were got in 1919 with food as the lever. Belgium "understands what it signed in 1927" (`L1379`), so at least one government knows. D2 is the section titled "Why it is believed", and its second bullet is contradicted by the storyline.
- *Fix:* Use it. The Realm did use food once, to get the ore that now makes Article I possible, and Article II is the promise never to do it again. That is a better reason to believe the article than a clean record, and it gives Hoover ("the rescue's Pacifican face", `L1750`) a private reason to be in the room. Add `L1379` to the impact notes. Keep the 1931 example; drop the general claim.

**B2. Herriot's second reason reads the prize of 1911 backwards.**
- *Passage:* C4, line 141: "Its school took the prize of 1911 for 'the elements beyond uranium that neutrons produce' (`L1281`) and will not hand its piles' fuel to a foreign works."
- *Problem:* In canon that prize rewarded an error. `L1281`: "the transuranic chains of 1902–1907 look settled"; Hillebrand "withdraws it from consideration pending verification, and the prize goes to Paris alone. The laboratory most likely to break the error becomes the one most committed to defending it." `L1612` then has the opening of 1927 publish "Hillebrand's withdrawal from the prize of 1911". The words quoted are in the line; what they are cited for is the opposite. After 1927 the prize is Paris's public humiliation, not a credential in bred elements. Separately, "its piles' fuel" gives France piles; canon has "A European heavy-water pile, aimed at power, runs about 1930–1931" (`L1614`), one, with no country.
- *Fix:* Make the humiliation the motive: Paris was let take a prize that Carquinez knew was wrong, was exposed by the corrections column in 1927, and will not now hand a foreign works the one thing that would let it find real elements beyond uranium. That is stronger than pride and is already in canon. Add the security motive the draft misses: Article I binds Germany whether or not France signs, so France gets Germany's renunciation free and keeps its own hands untied. Say whether France has a pile, or mark it OPEN.

**B3. France both enters the monetary settlement and stays out of it.**
- *Passage:* B5, line 98: France "will not put the Bank of France under the consultation rule and signs only a bilateral gold-clearing protocol." E3 entry 3, line 234: "Germany, Britain and France enter the monetary settlement." B1, line 81, quotes the same canon words as its basis.
- *Problem:* `L1748`: "Germany, Britain and France enter the monetary settlement". B5 departs from it and the departure is not under `## Canon tensions`; the draft then recites the canon sentence unchanged in E3. `C-1932-THALASSIA` lists `FR` as a party.
- *Fix:* Either list it as a tension (canon line is PROPOSED, so the author can decide quickly) and rewrite E3 and the impact note for `L1748`, or keep France inside the settlement and let its refusal be Article I alone. The second is cleaner: one refusal, on the one thing that matters.

**B4. The calendar does not hold together, and 1 August is called "ratified" when it is signed.**
- *Passage:* C2, line 119 (the proposal is Julian's "in the winter of 1931–32" and "needs the consent of Langmuir's seat and the Council of the Seal before it goes to the common Foreign minister"); line 45 ("promises in a ratified act"); E1 and E3; set piece 4 ("The Senate, July 1932").
- *Problem:* (a) `CR-084` `B5` has the common Foreign minister hand Simon the article, pledge included, "In April 1932". `CR-085` `A5` has the crowns agree only "At Hayama", late May, "what the Concord will bring to the conference's close", including Japan's countersignature of the pledge. A Concord minister therefore offers London a Concord pledge a month before the Council of the Two Crowns has agreed it. (b) `CR-084` cancels Britain's share "on ratification at Westminster" and has the States General ratify the Batavia Compact in "Autumn 1932"; `L1750` has Young "campaign for acceptance" to November. On 1 August nothing outside the Concord is ratified. (c) The Ottawa conference opened on 21 July 1932 under Bennett's chairmanship, with Baldwin, Bruce and Coates present (see History checked), and `L1752` keeps Ottawa in canon. On 1 August the heads of the Canadian, Australian and New Zealand delegations, and Britain's second minister, are in Ottawa. `CR-083` has Bennett sign the Trilateral Treaty in person "Weeks before hosting" it. (d) `CR-084` puts the chambers' votes in "July – Aug 1932 (after signature)"; this draft puts the Senate scene in July, before the signature.
- *Fix:* Add a calendar table as the siblings have. Suggested: winter, cabinet and Council of the Seal; April, the paper goes to London *ad referendum* with Japan's consent already given through Chichibu (or move the first sounding to after Hayama); late May, Hayama; last week of June, London decides; July, chambers vote the authorising bills; 1 August, signature and promulgation by the Concord; ratifications named as pending. Say who signs for Britain and the dominions (Simon and high commissioners, with the Empire's other ministers meeting in Ottawa that week to raise a tariff wall, which is a gift to the scene). Replace "ratified" in line 45 with what is true on the day.

**B5. The sufficers' condition is attached to bills that are not bills.**
- *Passage:* D5, line 202: the aliʻi seats "make the article their condition for the rest of the season's bills: the British annuities, the renewal without term and the stabilization credits."
- *Problem:* Three conflicts. The renewal: `CR-085` `A6` has it ratified by the sixty-and-sixty delegations, and has the Common Tribunal rule against Tate that the term "is a matter of the renewal and within the delegations' competence"; `L1734` says "ratified by both crowns and delegations". The Senate has no vote to withhold. The credits: B1, line 84, says they "are placed with private San Francisco syndicates" and Giannini "loses, because the credits are private money"; there is nothing to vote. The annuities: these do go to both chambers (`CR-084` `A1`), but `CR-084` `A3` already states the sufficers' conditions for that vote ("zero people taken without vote or compact, zero colonial policing, no annexation in the Indies, and no tariff concession on sugar") and does not mention Article II. The same vote is decided twice with different terms.
- *Fix:* Rest the condition on the one real bill, the annuities, and on the eight aliʻi seats' margin in a vote `CR-084` says passes "narrowly". Add Article II to `CR-084` `A3`'s list by a cross-reference agreed between the two drafts, or state here that this CR adds a fifth condition to that vote. If the renewal is to be part of the leverage, it must be through the thirty delegates the Senate chooses (`L1604`: "delegates chosen half by each chamber"), which is a different and playable scene.

**B6. The ore check in C5 cannot see the ore that matters, and using it would open the Works' books.**
- *Passage:* C5, line 147: "The Works already keeps the ledgers of nearly all the world's uranium ore, because it buys it. A signatory's purchases outside those ledgers are visible as gaps."
- *Problem:* A2 lists exactly the ore the Works does not buy: Cornwall, Saxony, Madagascar, and Urgeiriça after August 1929, where "In canon London is behind them" (line 57). These are the signatories' and France's own soil or their nationals' property; nothing is purchased, so no gap appears. Line 62 says Cornwall "in time would feed a first British device". Article I does not regulate ore at all. And a complaint to the court founded on the Works' ledgers would require producing them, which shows the Works' purchases mine by mine: the heading promises a check "without the Realm opening its books". The same applies to the cipher room: line 151 keeps it out of the article, but a complaint founded on it reveals it. Note also that `L1534` gives the room "British official traffic in China and the Pacific", not Whitehall's home traffic.
- *Fix:* Say plainly that ore cannot be checked and the article does not rely on it. Rest verification on what canon already gives: heavy water ("holds most of the world's supply", `L1643`; every European pile needs it and the Works is the contract holder, yet C5 omits it); power (canon's halls draw hundreds of megawatts and Britain itself reads "the power tables", `L1640`; a separating works shows in any country's published load); the restricted goods; the pile register. Add one sentence on how the Realm complains without showing its sources, or accept that it cannot and that the pledge's lapse is the only sanction. Decide what happens to Urgeiriça and South Terras after Britain signs: an offtake to the Works, paid for, would close the hole and pay Britain again.

## Literary effect

The structure is right: the fights happen before the conference and Thalassia seals them. Parts A and B read as term sheets, which is acceptable in a CR, and each has a named defeat (Urgeiriça, Belgium's royalty, Giannini). Part C has real conflict inside the wall: Langmuir's argument from the British date, Wigner's honest support of a clause whose purpose he knows, Szilárd's "the signposts made into law", and the foresight that "in 1947 ... every signatory will learn what it signed, as Belgium did in 1927". That last sentence is the strongest in the draft because it puts a dated fuse under the triumph without naming any cost.

Outside the wall the conflict thins. The German delegate who says the article is one-sided and is told that it is has a line but no stake. Britain's refusals are all in `CR-084`. Mexico, China, Canada and the two dominions want nothing and lose nothing on the page. Article II has no foreign party who bargains (S9). The C3 table is a term sheet; it needs at least one signatory who signs angry.

On protected knowledge: no foreigner cites a figure for the stock, here or in the siblings (`CR-084` has Tizard say he "cannot say what Thalassia will have built"; the cabinet minute "records nothing of what the Works holds"). Nothing in the draft opens the second record; the clause's stated ground is true and its purpose is spoken only inside the wall. The clause is consistent with `L1614` ("an idea in Europe, with no production pile, no chemistry and no weapon route"). The article does tell every chemist in Europe that irradiated fuel is worth a treaty; the author accepted that at 19:32 and C4 turns it into France's reason to look.

On T-6: outside quotations of the author and of the line to be cut, I find no sentence in CR-083 to CR-086 that says the moral cost is unresolved or settled. Three sentences come near a verdict and should be watched when they become storyline prose: "The cabinet's aim is reached in the act of renouncing its abuse" (line 205), "The others' signatures were therefore given and not extorted" (line 144), and "Thalassia seals what is already won" (line 45).

Does the ending earn its triumph? Nearly. It earns it where the victor binds himself at his mother's school's insistence, where one dissent is sealed and one power walks away. It does not yet earn the word "believable" for verification (B6), for the day itself (B4) or for the food order's honesty (B1). Fixing B1 turns the food order from a boast into an atonement that never has to be called one, which is the reading the author asked to leave to the reader. The image of Victoria leaving the set on is the right last picture and costs nothing.
