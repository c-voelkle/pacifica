## Verdict

One more revision. The design holds: the bargain of two articles is believable in outline, the calendar in `E1` agrees with the three siblings and with `L1746`–`L1758`, no foreigner cites a figure for the Works' stock, and nothing in the draft opens what canon seals to 1947. Three things would make integration wrong as the draft stands: `C5` gives the Realm a unilateral way out of the pledge that the approved text and CR-084 deny it; the route by which the Realm and the Concord consent to the two articles is told three ways and disagrees with CR-084 `T-S1`; and three ledger lines record something other than the body. Beyond those, the draft still carries a dozen leftovers from the day's rebuilding, and the finale has no act by Julian in it.

## Blockers

**B1. The pledge can be ended by the Realm alone in `C5`, and only by the court everywhere else.**
- *Passage:* `C5`, "What the Realm cannot use" (line 173), against `C5` "The court" (line 174), `C1` (line 128), `T-5` (line 381), `F-4c` (line 479) and CR-084 `B5`.
- *Problem:* line 173 says: "What it learns otherwise it can act on only by saying that the pledge has lapsed, without giving its reason." The next bullet says "a bench of the accused's peers decides when the Realm is released from its pledge" and has Langmuir answer "that a pledge the Realm could cancel by its own finding would be worth nothing to London." `T-5` says "the pledge lapses toward a signatory the court finds in breach", and `F-4c` records "its lapse on the court's finding (86-4)" as approved. CR-084 `B5` has Simon sell the signature to his cabinet on "a court to which a breach can be taken". If line 173 stands, Britain has signed for a sentence the Realm can withdraw in silence, which is the thing Langmuir says would make it worthless.
- *Fix:* cut the unilateral lapse. The honest version is harsher and better: what the cipher room or the Works' ledgers show, the Realm cannot act on at all under the article; it can only watch, or contrive a public ground. Say that, and let the cabinet know it as the weak joint.

**B2. How the Realm and the Concord consent to the two articles is told three ways and disagrees with CR-084 `T-S1`.**
- *Passage:* `D5` (lines 228–229), `C8` (line 187), `D1` (line 194), `E1` (lines 259, 264–265); CR-084 `T-S1` and `A1`.
- *Problem:* `D5` says "Article II is voted by the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies, as a guarantee of the Realm, and by the Concord's delegations." CR-084 `T-S1`, Case A (decided), says "a treaty or guarantee of the Realm needs the consent of two-thirds of the Senate; a money act ... needs a majority in both chambers." A guarantee therefore does not go to the Chamber, and it does need two-thirds. But `D5` then says "The Senate has two votes that season in which two-thirds is needed", and names the annuity guarantees and the striking of the term; Article II would be a third. `C8` calls Article I "a treaty of the Concord", which by CR-084 `A1` goes to both crowns and the delegations; yet the pledge is what "the Realm undertakes" (`C1`) and the ledger line makes `CROWN` the obligor, and no vote on Article I is stated anywhere. `E1` has the common Foreign minister sign on 1 August "with both crowns' ratification already given in July", that is, ratification of an act a month before it is signed, while the July row of the calendar lists no vote on either article. The Realm's most consequential undertaking in the book has no stated organ.
- *Fix:* decide once, in `E1`, and recite it in `C8` and `D5`. The simplest reading that fits the siblings: both articles are treaties of the Concord (the common Foreign minister signs; the delegations vote the two texts in July with the component treaties; the crowns ratify after signature, on 1 August or the days after); Article II is in addition a guarantee of the Realm and takes the Senate's two-thirds, which makes three such votes, the third being the one the aliʻi seats want and the provisioners' senators resist. If the pledge of Article I is also an undertaking of the Realm alone, it too needs the Senate, and the draft should say whether the Senate is told anything about clause 3 (see S6). Then correct "two votes" and drop the Chamber from the guarantee.

**B3. Three ledger lines do not record what the body decides.**
- *Passage:* `## I`, New commitments, lines 348–350; `D1` and `D5`; `C3`; CR-083 `A11`.
- *Problem:* (a) `C-1932-ARTICLE-II` reads "grain and its means move in peace at published prices and no signatory is embargoed in peace". That is the sufficers' first draft. `D1` says the first draft "said that 'grain and its means move to every signatory'" and was narrowed, and `D5` says of the passed text: "It does not promise the Realm's own crop to anyone." The ledger line would commit the Realm to the promise Mead got struck. (b) `C-1932-FINAL-ACT` and `C-1932-ARTICLE-I` list `UK`, `CA`, `ANZ`, `US`, `DE`, `CN`, `MX`. Caledonia is in `C3`'s table of signatories, and CR-083 `A11` says "Caledonia signs the Final Act and both articles" (party `CLD`). It is missing from both lines. (c) The Final Act annexes "the Union's settlement" (`C-1932-THALASSIA`, whose ledger parties include `CSA` and `GULF`) and schedules the Gulf's compact; neither is a party to the Final Act line and the draft does not say whether the Gulf or the Confederacy signs anything. The Netherlands is named without a party code.
- *Fix:* rewrite the Article II line from `D1`'s three undertakings; add `CLD` (noting it is CR-083's party) to the Act and to both articles; state in `C3` whether the Gulf Republic and the Confederacy sign, and for the Netherlands use the code CR-084 assigns.

## Should fix

**S1. `D2` says Article II forswears what was done in 1919; `D1`'s text does not forbid it.** `D2`: "Article II is the promise never to do it again." What `L1379` records is that "starving Vienna and Bohemia and ruined Belgium negotiate with" the man who decides "where Pacifican food and credit go". That was conditioning relief and credit on a concession at a peace table. `D1` promises "to embargo none; to honour the scheduled contracts; and to keep the means and the passage open at published prices", and only "in peace". None of the three undertakings reaches relief credit granted on terms. Either add a fourth undertaking (no political condition on food or food credit), which Mead and the provisioners would fight and which gives `D5` a harder argument, or reduce `D2`'s claim to what the text does. Note also that the three states the lever was used on, Belgium, Austria-Hungary and Bohemia, are not at the conference (`C3`, line 155), so nobody in the room can say it. That absence is either a flaw or a scene; at present it is unremarked. And if the Realm lets it be understood in public that it used food in 1919, someone must decide to say so; the draft leaves it in narration with Hoover's "private reason".

**S2. `C5`'s checks are weaker than stated, and one of them uses the Works' books.** (a) Heavy water: "Who receives heavy water, and how much, is known to the Works as a customer". A customer knows what it buys, not what the seller delivers to others. `L1643` gives the Works "most of the world's supply", not all, and no right to see Norsk Hydro's other sales; and `L1614` says heavy hydrogen is public and "Norway's cheap electrolysis can concentrate it", so a signatory with cheap power can make its own. (b) The same bullet list says the Realm "can complain to the court only on what is public: power, heavy-water deliveries and the register", after saying a complaint founded on the Works' ledgers "would reveal it". Heavy-water deliveries are the Works' commercial knowledge, not public. The draft must choose: either the contract of 1930 is disclosed and gives the Works a right to Norsk Hydro's delivery list (a new term, PROPOSED), or heavy water is not a ground of complaint. (c) Graphite is named among "the restricted goods", but canon's obstacle is different: Europe's graphite "absorbs too many neutrons ... and Pacifica does not correct the error" (`L1614`). The regime's quiet dependence on an uncorrected error, which any good laboratory could remove, is the real weak joint and is not mentioned. (d) "a load of that size shows in any country's published figures" is asserted of countries that do not publish the Office's county tables. None of this exposes the cipher room; the cipher room is handled correctly.

**S3. The ore argument that Part C "depends on" has no quantities and a hole the draft itself opens.** `A2` (line 72): Britain's sources are "enough in time for one device, late and small, and not for a second", while "Their quantities are OPEN". The conclusion is fixed and the numbers are to follow; it is the sentence CR-084 `B5` gives Tizard. The ratio offered, "some sixty tonnes of uranium to a device (`L1646`)", is not on `L1646`; it is derived (250 tonnes by 1927 for about four devices), and the same line with `L1640`'s "some fifteen devices in all" gives about thirty-seven. Radium Hill: `A2` says "The Commonwealth closes the door after the demonstration", which leaves the mine open to Britain, yet "Britain's own sources after 1929 are Cornwall's dumps and the Portuguese mine", and `C3` says the paid offtake "closes the one hole Part A leaves". Saxony (on a signatory's soil) and Madagascar are further holes that `C5` itself concedes. Say "one of the holes", account for Radium Hill, and give the workbook task for Cornwall's and Urgeiriça's tonnage the same standing as Great Bear's.

**S4. Custody and King's objection exist only inside `## Canon tensions`, and who decides the pledge is unclear.** The header says "Custody of the devices is stated once, here (`T-5`)". A tension is a note for the author; what is integrated is the body, and `C1`–`C2` carry one line ("The Realm's General Staff objects to the pledge, through King, and loses"). Move the custody statement and the two things King obtains into Part C. Then answer three questions the present text raises. Where is custody "written down": in the article (so every signatory reads the Realm's chain of command), in a protocol of the Concord, or in the Works' charter? King "is heard by the Council of the Seal" and "loses both"; but the Council's competence in canon is production against science, with "secrecy and publication to the keys" (`L1362`), and a pledge about use is the cabinet's and the sovereign's. CR-085 `A4` sends the same paper "to the cabinet in the winter". And King's second argument, against a Navy seat "fixed by law to one nation", is made in the winter, while CR-085 `A4` gives that seat as "recognition of May". He objects to a concession not yet thought of. Also: CR-085 `A4` says King obtains one thing (custody written down); this draft says two.

**S5. The second key in the summer of 1932.** Canon: the Polytechnic chief's key is "from 1915 Vance's" (`L1256`), and `L1736` (PROPOSED) has Vance killed in May 1932 by "headquarters guards sympathetic to the Restoration". `C2` notes that this "would leave the key to his successor by the summer". The draft makes that key one of two that command the world's only atomic weapons, and passes over the fact that its holder may be murdered by sympathisers of the coup ten weeks before the Act. Either the custody statement is written because of that death (which would give King's paper a far stronger occasion than the winter), or the draft should say plainly that the successor is unnamed and that on 1 August the second key is held by someone the reader has not met.

**S6. Who on the Pacifican side knows what clause 3 is for.** `L1503` sets the practice: the foreign ministry "is told the truth it needs: that the Works buys the salt and uranium for industry." In this draft the common Foreign minister hands the article to Simon, answers the German and the French delegates, and signs. The Senate, the delegations, and Japan's Navy minister who countersigns all act on the article. The draft never says what any of them is told. `G3` has "One Pacifican in the room" who knows; name the office (Hoover, bonded since 1920 and "the rescue's Pacifican face", is the obvious candidate) and say that the Foreign minister defends the clause on its stated ground because that is all he has. This costs a sentence and closes a who-knows-what gap in the book's last act.

## Literary effect

What works. The bargain is stated as a bargain, and the one-sentence ending is honest about its reach ("the powers at Thalassia"). The decisive fights are placed before the conference with named losers: Lindemann in London, Hahn in Berlin, Szilárd inside the wall, Mead before the cabinet, King in his paper, the French delegate in committee. Szilárd's dissent is the best thing in the draft: a man who is right, loses, is entered in the roll and "does not resign". France is a real refusal with reasons that are not pride, and it costs the Realm something. `D5`'s turn, that it is "Victoria's school that writes the limit into her son's triumph" and that "The cabinet's aim is reached in the act of renouncing its abuse, and no one says so in the chamber", is the kind of irony the book's ending needs, and it carries the cost without naming it.

What does not yet work.

- **Two of the named losers lose nothing.** Giannini: "There is no vote for him to lose, since the money is private." The interior senators over the Pampas: "They lose because the money is the houses' own and no vote is needed". An objection that cannot affect the outcome is commentary. Give each a lever (Giannini's refusal makes the syndicates smaller or dearer; Tate's bloc trades its silence on the Pampas for something in the annuity bill) or cut them to a line.
- **The Foreign minister's two answers are one answer.** To the German: "it is [one-sided]". To the Frenchman: "it is the price list, and ... France has read it." Both are good; together they are a mannerism, spoken by a man with no name. The Concord's voice in its greatest act should be a person. If the name must stay OPEN, give the second answer to someone else.
- **Julian is absent from his own finale.** The proposal "is Julian's, made in his cabinet", and after that he sails to Hayama in a sibling draft. In `E3` and `G5` he signs nothing, says nothing, and is offered a telephone call. Victoria has a gesture (the set left on); Szilárd has his roll; the protagonist has a decision reported in one clause. The winter cabinet in which he proposes to bind himself (after 1919, after 1927, with `L1672`'s plan on the table) is the missing scene, and it is where his motive must be shown: at present nothing says why this man gives up the two levers, beyond the reasoning the narrator supplies in "The ending in one sentence".
