## The ending in one sentence

On 1 August 1932 the powers at Thalassia give up the weapon, and the Realm binds itself never to withhold bread.

A renunciation alone is a monopoly dictating terms, and no proud power signs it. A food pledge alone is charity. Together they are a bargain: the one state that could starve or destroy any other promises in a signed act to do neither, and the others stop trying to become what it is. The fights, refusals and prices happen before the conference, each with a loser who is named. Thalassia seals what is already won.

## A. Why the others cannot get fuel (1909–1932)

Canon left three questions for this CR: "how Pacifica mines and buys more uranium than its four sources" (`L1867 ("how Pacifica mines and buys more uranium than its four sources")`), "thorium (a later CR)" (`L1869 ("thorium (a later CR)")`) and "the dependence strategy after 1927" (`L1871 ("the dependence strategy after 1927")`). Part A answers them as one thing: by 1932 a power that wanted a weapon could not find the fuel for it.

**A1. What was under contract before anyone knew why.**
The rule was "to buy what nobody values" (`L1262 ("The rule is to buy what nobody values")`), and to buy it "for glazes, glass and alloy steel" without a false word (`L1259 ("for glazes, glass and alloy steel")`). By 1921 the Works holds the Western Slope, Joachimsthal, Shinkolobwe and Fergana, and from about 1912 Great Bear, found from "cobalt bloom and copper near Echo Bay" (`L1265 ("cobalt bloom and copper near Echo Bay")`). In 1927 "Ottawa keeps the contract" when London asks for it (`L1617 ("Ottawa keeps the contract")`).
- **Great Bear's open details (`L1881 ("Great Bear's year, company, volumes and route")`; PROPOSED):** the Canadian-chartered company is the Mackenzie Radium and Silver Company (name PROPOSED). Its ore goes out by river barge in the summer. Its contribution to 1932 is put at 200 to 350 tonnes of uranium; no workbook yet carries Great Bear, so the figure is a placeholder and **OPEN**. The railhead it used in the 1910s is not verified and is left OPEN. The contract's term of fifty years from 1932 is `CR-083`'s; it is why the Empire's own North American ore stays out of British reach after 1932.

**A2. What the Works bought after Hoover's remark, and where it was refused (PROPOSED).**
About 1926 Hoover, who "knows of it in Saxony, Cornwall and Portugal" (`L1580 ("knows of it in Saxony, Cornwall and Portugal")`), backed Szilárd's five years against the Chief's twenty. The ore office acts on the remark, and the demonstration of 1927 then closes most of what it reaches for.
- **Portugal.** In 1926–27 the Works, through the estates, buys the dumps of the idle Urgeiriça mine and a short contract for its concentrate (*Hist.*: the mine stood idle from 1926 to 1929). In August 1929 two British subjects buy the mine and found the Companhia Portuguesa de Radium (*Hist.*). In canon London is behind them. The Works keeps what it has carted away and loses the mine. This is the sweep's dated defeat.
- **South Australia.** One purchase of dumps and stock at Radium Hill in 1926–27, a few tonnes of uranium (MODEL). The Commonwealth closes the door after the demonstration.
- **Cornwall, Saxony, Madagascar.** Refused outright. After the autumn of 1927 Whitehall bars foreign purchase of Cornish pitchblende and dumps (*Hist.*: South Terras was the one British mine extracting the salt on site in the 1920s). The Saxon government keeps the Erzgebirge districts for German use. France keeps Madagascar's ores inside its colonial administration.
- **Belgium's price (`C-1929-SHINKOLOBWE-REVISION`).** "Belgium understands what it signed in 1927" (`L1379 ("Belgium understands what it signed in 1927")`). The concession is the Works' company's for ninety-nine years and Union Minière no longer holds it, so the lever is the Belgian state's: its sovereignty, its royalty and its seats on the board. In 1928–29 Brussels asks for a higher royalty and a larger free allowance of the salt for its hospitals, and gets both. It asks for a share of the uranium and is refused; "all its output going to Pacifica" stands. This is the one place where the Works pays to keep what it has.
- **The totals (MODEL).** Canon's "about 5,800 tonnes" mined to 1932 (`L1646 ("lie in reserve in 1932")`) did not count Great Bear. With Great Bear, Urgeiriça's dumps and Radium Hill the figure would be about 6,000 to 6,200 tonnes; it is **OPEN** until the workbook carries Great Bear.
- **What the sweep buys.** "The ore is time and leverage, not a guarantee" (`L1503 ("The ore is time and leverage, not a guarantee")`). Britain's own sources after 1929 are Cornwall's dumps and the Portuguese mine. Their quantities are **OPEN**; on canon's own ratio, some sixty tonnes of uranium to a device (`L1646`), they are enough in time for one device, late and small, and not for a second, which is what Tizard tells the British cabinet (`CR-084` `B5`). The ore does not make a rival's weapon impossible. It makes it late, small and dear, and that is the position from which a rival will take a price to stop. Part C depends on this sentence.

**A3. Thorium (`C-1926-BRAZIL-MONAZITE`; PROPOSED).**
Szilárd and Wigner's breeder proposal of 1925–26 says that "one day thorium, can be turned into fuel" (`L1637 ("one day thorium, can be turned into fuel")`). From 1926 the Works, through the estates' chemical houses, buys Brazilian monazite sand on ordinary commercial contracts (*Hist.*: the monazite trade was slack in the 1920s as electric light replaced the gas mantle). The cerium and the rare earths are in fact used by the champions, and nothing false is said about the purchase. The thorium is stored. Travancore, a princely state under its own government, restricts bulk export to foreign buyers after 1928 on London's advice (PROPOSED). London does not know what thorium is for; by 1928 it distrusts anything the Works buys. Thorium breeding stays an experiment in 1932, and the stock is insurance for the 1940s.

**A4. Heavy water and the tools (canon, recited).**
In 1930 the Works contracts for Norsk Hydro's heavy water and "holds most of the world's supply from then on" (`L1643 ("holds most of the world's supply")`; PROPOSED in canon by `CR-055`). The halls' valves, pumps, graphite and beryllium are restricted goods (`L1640 ("Exports of these goods are restricted")`), and "what a rival needs most after 1927 is not a fact but ten years of practice" (`L1640`).

**A5. What a foreign delegate knows in 1932.**
No foreigner in this CR cites a figure for the Works' stock. What the delegates know is what was shown or published: that in 1927 "three exist, and one is spent" (`L1612 ("Three exist, and one is spent")`); five years of published power tables since; and a second record "sealed for twenty years from 1927" (`L1612`). They argue from an arsenal whose size the Realm will not state. How many devices have been assembled since 1927 stays open in canon (`L1867`), and this CR does not decide it.

**A6. The dependence strategy (`L1871`).**
Inside the Concord, dependence is the fuel lease and the return of the bred elements (`CR-085`). Outside it, in 1932, dependence rests on the ore, the heavy water, the restricted goods and Article I. Leasing Works fuel to states outside the Concord is argued inside the Council of the Seal and deferred past 1932; it is out of this suite.

## B. What each power is paid (1930–1932)

A power signs away a weapon only if it cannot get one soon, cannot afford one, is paid, and is less afraid afterwards than before. Part A is the first condition. This Part is the third.

**B1. The monetary settlement and who lends (`L1746`, `L1748`).**
Canon has the Union's currency join "an adjustable gold-dollar order" at "the Thalassia Conference in June–July 1932" (`L1746 ("Thalassia Conference in June–July 1932")`), and "Germany, Britain and France enter the monetary settlement" (`L1748 ("Germany, Britain and France enter the monetary settlement")`).
- **The anchor.** The Realm's gold dollar stays at the Reserve Bank's charter cover. The Bank still "refuses to issue beyond its cover" (`L1695 ("refuses to issue beyond its cover")`).
- **The rule.** A member names its own parity and consults before it changes it.
- **The lender (PROPOSED).** The stabilization credits are placed with private San Francisco syndicates. The Reserve Bank discounts for them only on its usual rule, at a penalty rate and on good security. The Treasury does not borrow from the Bank, and the Patrimony lends to no government "in treaty negotiation with Pacifica" (`L892 ("treaty negotiation with Pacifica")`).
- **Giannini refuses.** He is the largest private banker in San Francisco and is asked into the syndicates. He declines, and says why in public: his bank's money is for the mortgages he was refused credit for in the winter. The syndicates form without him. There is no vote for him to lose, since the money is private.
- **The Concord's case.** The yen's band in `C-1932-ADJUSTABLE-PARITY` (`CR-085` `A7`) is the Concord's stricter instance of this rule.

**B2. Britain.** Britain's payment is `CR-084`'s: the annuities cancelled for the retrenchment, for Caledonia and for its signature under Article I. Sterling enters the settlement at a parity Britain names. Nothing more is decided here.

**B3. The Union.** Canon pays it with "debt and gold rescue" (`L1746`), "partial recovery through the German debt settlement, Atlantic primacy and a founding seat in the renewed court" (`L1748`). The Union comes to the weapon "later still, its best people being in Pacifica" (`L1614 ("Germany comes later")`). It signs Article I as part of the settlement Dawes negotiates and Young campaigns for (`L1750 ("Owen D. Young wins the Union election")`). The margin and date of its Senate's vote are not in canon and are not invented here.

**B4. Germany: the debt settled (`C-1932-GERMAN-DEBT`; PROPOSED in detail).**
Germany is a parliamentary monarchy. It "comes later, starting from power reactors" (`L1614`), and it wants its debts settled more than it wants a weapon a decade off.
- **What it is paid.** The settlement of its debts to the Union's banks, which take a cut in principal. That cut is the Union's "partial recovery" and it is why the Union's banks need the Realm's rescue.
- **What it keeps.** The right to build piles for power. Canon gives Europe one small heavy-water pile by 1930–31 and does not say whose it is; that is left **OPEN**, and this draft adds no others.
- **What it gives.** Its signature, and with it every charge of fuel any pile of its own will irradiate. Germany's scientists see what the third clause costs sooner than its ministers do. Hahn (`CT-OTTO-HAHN`), the second finder of fission, says so in Berlin and is overruled by a finance ministry that has what it came for.

**B5. France: inside the settlement, and paid nothing more (PROPOSED).**
France enters the monetary settlement with Germany and Britain, as canon has it (`L1748`). It sits on gold in 1932 (*Hist.*, from memory) and asks for no credit. Its debt to the Realm is not cut, because it gives nothing for a cut. Its one refusal is Article I (`C4`).

**B6. China: the dual track (`C-1932-CHINA-GOLD-EXCHANGE`; `T-C1`; PROPOSED).**
"In 1929 a Pacifican commission proposes a gold-exchange standard linked to Pacifica's dollar" (`L1714 ("a Pacifican commission proposes a gold-exchange standard")`), and "silver's fall is Pacifica's loss" because the Realm's loans for works in China are in silver.
- **When.** The customs side comes first, in 1930 (*Hist.*: China's customs gold unit dates from 1 February 1930). The protocol is negotiated through 1931 and signed at Thalassia in July 1932 by **T. V. Soong** (*Hist.*: Minister of Finance 1928–33) for Nanjing.
- **The two tracks.** Customs assessments and new foreign borrowing move to a gold-exchange unit linked to the Realm's dollar. The soybean compact's contracts are already written in that dollar. The interior stays on silver, because Shanghai's native banks, the silver merchants and Canton will not give it up.
- **What China is paid.** The Realm's existing silver loans stay in silver, so the loss on silver's fall stays where canon puts it, with the Realm. China gets a reserve compact of its own in San Francisco on the terms the other holders have (`L1597 ("compacts of their own")`). The Concord signs as one party, `CROWN` and `JP`.
- **What the Realm gets.** China's new borrowing abroad is in the Realm's dollar, so the next generation of loans carries no silver risk for the lender.
- **Who objects.** Shanghai's mill-owners, who face a cheaper yen in the same summer (`CR-085` `A7`). Soong's answer is that the dollar link governs customs and borrowing and not the price of cloth, and that the tariff notes with Japan run out in May 1933.
- **China and the two articles.** China has no atomic programme. It signs Article I and receives the Realm's pledge, and Article II writes the wheat credit of 1931 and the soybean compact into a ratified promise.

## C. Article I: the renunciation

**C1. The article (wording PROPOSED; the author approved a renunciation agreement, a spent-fuel clause to the Realm's exclusive advantage, and the pledge as item 5 of his list).**
The signatories outside the Concord undertake:
1. to build no plant for separating the light uranium;
2. to make no test;
3. to deliver to the Light and Matter Works all fuel that has been through a pile, and to keep none.

Atomic power stays lawful for them. The Realm discloses nothing: no design, no figure, and its second record stays sealed to 1947. The Realm undertakes never to use the weapon against a signatory that keeps the article (`T-5`). Complaints under the article go to the Hague Arbitration Court as renewed in 1932 (`L1748`).

**C2. Who decides it at Thalassia (PROPOSED).**
The proposal is Julian's, made in his cabinet in the winter of 1931–32, when Britain has left gold and every rival is short of money at once (`L1687 ("Britain leaves gold in September")`). The Works' rules sit in its charter and not in any treaty, so the article needs the consent of Langmuir's seat and the Council of the Seal, which hears Wigner, Szilárd and King. Japan's consent in principle comes that winter through Prince Chichibu, and the Council of the Two Crowns confirms it at Hayama in early June (`CR-085`).
- **Langmuir** argues for it. The lead "is defended by the next plant" only until a rival's first device, and the Works reckoned Britain's for "about 1933–1935" (`L1614 ("about 1933–1935")`). An article that stops the rivals is worth more than a plant that stays ahead of them.
- **Wigner** supports the third clause on its stated ground. Irradiated fuel is dangerous, and only the Works has a code for handling it.
- **Szilárd** objects, and his objection is in `C7`.
- **The Realm's General Staff** objects to the pledge, through King, and loses (`T-5`).
- **The two keys.** A dispute about secrecy goes to the archive's two keys, the sovereign's and the Polytechnic chief's. The second is Vance's; canon's open proposal that he dies in May 1932 would leave the key to his successor by the summer.

- **The dominions.** Canada signs Article I for itself (author, 23 November 2026, 19:55). No historical reason stands against it: after the Statute of Westminster a dominion makes its own treaties, and in the same weeks Canada signs the Trilateral Treaty of Thalassia, its first treaty with Pacifica in its own name (`CR-083`). Its signature matters because it holds Great Bear, and a Canada that has itself renounced cannot be asked by London to divert the ore. Australia and New Zealand, already treaty partners of Thalassia in 1932 (`L1752 ("can still find one another")`), sign for themselves on the same footing (author, 23 November 2026, 20:11).

Their accession is left OPEN for the coda. The sentence at the head of this file therefore says "the powers at Thalassia".
- **Britain's remaining ore.** When Britain signs, the Works offers to take the output of the Portuguese mine and of Cornwall under a paid contract (PROPOSED). It closes the one hole Part A leaves and pays Britain a second time.
- **The Union on 1 August.** Its signature is unratified on the day and rides on the election of November; its revanchists remain "the unresolved threat of the next decade" (`L1756 ("the unresolved threat of the next decade")`).

**C4. France reserves (PROPOSED).**
France signs the Final Act and Article II and enters a reservation on Article I. Herriot's government (*Hist.*: formed 3 June 1932, from memory) has two reasons, and neither is pride.
- **1927.** In 1911 Paris was allowed to take the prize for "the elements beyond uranium that neutrons produce" (`L1281 ("the elements beyond uranium that neutrons produce")`) when Carquinez had withdrawn its own work as unverified, and in 1927 the corrections column showed the world that the prize had rewarded an error. France will not now hand a foreign works the one material that would let its own school find the real elements beyond uranium.
- **Germany.** Article I binds Germany whether or not France signs. France gets its neighbour's renunciation for nothing and keeps its own hands free.
- **What the reservation costs France.** The Realm's pledge runs only to signatories of Article I, so France is the one great power at Thalassia to which no pledge is given, and its debt is not cut. A French delegate says in committee that this is punishment. The common Foreign minister answers that it is the price list, and that France has read it.
- **What it costs the Realm.** One holdout, with a school, gold and a reason to look. The third clause has told France where to look. Whether France has a pile is **OPEN** (canon gives Europe one and does not say whose). This is the coda's threat and it is left to the coda. No sentence in this CR says how it ends.

**C5. How the article is checked without the Realm opening its books (PROPOSED).**
- **Ore cannot be checked, and the article does not rely on it.** Cornwall, Saxony and Madagascar are the signatories' or France's own soil, and nothing is bought there that a ledger could show. Part A's ore makes a weapon late and dear; it does not police a signature.
- **Heavy water.** Every pile in Europe needs it, and since 1930 the Works holds the contract for most of the world's supply (`L1643`). Who receives heavy water, and how much, is known to the Works as a customer and needs no intelligence.
- **Power.** A separating works draws hundreds of megawatts. Britain itself read the Sierra's published power tables (`L1640`), and a load of that size shows in any country's published figures.
- **The restricted goods.** A separating plant needs the valves, pumps and graphite of `L1640`, and orders for them or attempts to copy them are known to the champions that make them.
- **Fuel accounts.** A signatory that runs a pile registers each charge of fuel with the court's registrar when it goes in. The Works collects it when it comes out. The court's officers count; they do not inspect designs.
- **Plants.** A separating plant already built is dismantled before the court's observer. Britain's is the only one (`CR-084` `B5`).
- **What the Realm cannot use.** "The cipher room reads British official traffic in China and the Pacific" since 1925 (`L1534 ("cipher room reads British official traffic")`), and the Works has its ledgers. A complaint founded on either would reveal it. The Realm can complain to the court only on what is public: power, heavy-water deliveries and the register. What it learns otherwise it can act on only by saying that the pledge has lapsed, without giving its reason. The cabinet knows this is the article's weak joint.
- **The court.** France, a non-signatory, sits on the bench that hears complaints and reads the register, and a bench of the accused's peers decides when the Realm is released from its pledge. King says so in the General Staff's paper. Langmuir's answer is that a pledge the Realm could cancel by its own finding would be worth nothing to London.
- **The asymmetry is in the text.** The Realm is not inspected, and the article says so plainly. A German delegate says in committee that the article is one-sided. The common Foreign minister answers that it is, and that the pledge and Article II are what the other side receives for it.

**C6. The spent fuel, on the true ground of its danger (`T-7`).**
The third clause makes general the rule of the Tokyo station, whose fuel "is leased from the Works and returned to Pacifica" so that "the bred elements never stay on its soil" (`L1634 ("the bred elements never stay on its soil")`).
- **What the text says.** Fuel that has been through a pile is dangerous to those who handle it, and the Works alone has a code and a works for handling it. Both statements are true.
- **What the text does not say.** What the fuel is worth. The Works carries it away at its own cost, charges nothing and pays nothing (PROPOSED).
- **Why it is too late for the clause to help a rival.** Europe already suspects that piles breed something: by 1932 it is "an idea in Europe, with no production pile, no chemistry and no weapon route" (`L1614 ("an idea in Europe")`). The clause confirms a suspicion. A rival would still lack ore, heavy water, a production pile, the chemistry and the years of practice, and a signatory that keeps its word never holds irradiated fuel long enough to work on it.
- **What the Realm gains.** In 1932 almost nothing: Europe has one small pile. The clause's value is in the piles that will be built, each of which will enlarge the Works' stock.

**C7. Szilárd's objection inside the wall.**
Szilárd called the Works' signposts "lying with true sentences" (`L1580 ("lying with true sentences")`). He tells the Council of the Seal that the third clause is the signposts made into law. Its stated ground is true and its purpose is hidden, and in 1947, when the second record opens, every signatory will learn what it signed, as Belgium did in 1927. ## D. Article II: the food order

**D1. The article (text PROPOSED).**
The Realm and Japan, as the Concord, undertake three things toward every signatory in peace: to embargo none; to honour the scheduled contracts; and to keep the means and the passage open at published prices, the means being seed, nitrogen, machines, credit and carriage, and the passage the scheduled straits. Any signatory may buy at the prices the Realm's own houses pay.

This is the text as the Senate passes it. The sufficers' first draft said that "grain and its means move to every signatory"; `D5` says how it was narrowed.

Canon's rule is: "Control means being indispensable, never denying: the Realm refuses food to no partner and embargoes no neighbour in peace. Like the Republic's further purpose in the Pacific, the aim is never stated" (`L1672 ("the aim is never stated")`). Article II publishes the rule. The aim, to be indispensable, stays unstated (`T-2`).

**D2. Why it is believed.**
- The Realm already sells "3 to 4 million tonnes of grain or its equivalent a year to Asia" (`L1672`), and the Entente ate its bread on credit from 1915.
- The Realm used food once. At The Hague in 1919 "the man who decides where Pacifican food and credit go is Hoover, and starving Vienna and Bohemia and ruined Belgium negotiate with him" (`L1379`), and that is how Joachimsthal and Shinkolobwe, half of Part A's ore, were secured. Belgium has understood it since 1927. Article II is the promise never to do it again, made by the state that knows what the lever is worth because it pulled it. Hoover, "the rescue's Pacifican face" in 1932, has a private reason to be in the room.
- It did not use it in 1931. Julian threatened Tokyo with oil, transport and the yen, and "Food is not among them" (`L1720 ("Food is not among them")`).
- Japan is fed by its own crop and signs as a guarantor (`CR-085` `A5`).

**D4. The Pampas, restored as private houses' purchases (`C-1932-PAMPAS-HOUSES`; PROPOSED).**
`CR-082` P-4 is approved: "The Realm buys there by contract through its own houses, never through Chile, and it keeps out of the frontier quarrel" (line 273 of CR-082.md).
- **Who buys.** Private Pacifican grain houses, as merchants. They carry, finance and insure Argentina's wheat, maize and linseed to third markets, and sell it the Columbia's nitrogen and the champions' machines. They do not import it into the Realm, which sells wheat in 1932.
- **Who does not.** The Crown's granary. It "buys the home counties' wheat and feed first" and takes contracts abroad only for beans and rice (`L1695`), and the Patrimony behind it lends to no government in negotiation (`L892`).
- **Why Buenos Aires deals.** President Justo's government (*Hist.*: in office from 20 February 1932) is watching the preferences being prepared for Ottawa, which threaten Argentina's British market (*Hist.*).
- **What Argentina refuses.** Any political tie, any coaling right, any arbitration of its waters. Britain remains its first buyer of chilled beef.
- **Who objects at home.** The interior counties' senators, Harlan Tate's bloc among them (for his age see `CR-085` `A6`), ask why Pacifican houses finance the Pampas' crop in the winter the home counties' wheat farms are foreclosed. They lose because the money is the houses' own and no vote is needed, and the cabinet's reason is not given in the chamber: it is the unstated aim, to be present "at every step of the world's grain trade".
- **In the schedule.** Argentina is not at the conference. The Realm names the houses' contracts in the schedule as contracts it will not interrupt. It puts no money behind them.
- **A foreign objection.** Britain imports most of its bread, and the schedule is a list of its granaries passing under Pacifican contract. Its Board of Trade asks in committee what published prices are worth when one buyer holds the crop. The answer is the last sentence of `D1`: any signatory may buy at the prices the Realm's houses pay. Britain gets that sentence and nothing more.

**D5. The pledge binds the Realm: the chambers' vote (PROPOSED).**
Article II is voted by the Senate and the Chamber of Deputies, as a guarantee of the Realm, and by the Concord's delegations.
- **The sufficers carry it.** The Senate has two votes that season in which two-thirds is needed (`CR-084` `T-S1`): its consent to the guarantees that go with the British annuities (`CR-084` `A3`), and the amendment of its conditions of 1928 that strikes the Concord's term (`CR-085` `A6`). The eight aliʻi seats and their allies make Article II their condition for both. It is the fifth of the conditions `CR-084` `A3` lists. Their case is the one they made in 1930 against Julian's table. His course "holds only while the sellers sell and the sea lanes are open", and a ratified promise never to withhold is what makes the sellers go on selling. It is Victoria's school that writes the limit into her son's triumph. Victoria does not enter the chamber.
- **Who objects.** The provisioners in the cabinet would keep the rule as a practice and not as law. The cabinet hears Elwood Mead (`CT-ELWOOD-MEAD`), seventy-four and holding no office (his being heard is PROPOSED; his career after the school is open in canon). Mead's case is the year the Realm's own margin closes, which the Office puts about 1940–42 (`L1672`): a law made in a year of surplus binds a successor in a year of want.
- **How it is settled.** Mead loses on the form and wins the wording. The sufficers' draft promised grain. The text of `D1` promises no embargo, the scheduled contracts, and the means and the passage. It does not promise the Realm's own crop to anyone.
- **What the provisioners get.** Their century's plan "closes only by buying three-quarters or more of what the world's grain sellers sell abroad" (`L1672`). The sellers now have a ratified reason to sell to the Realm's houses. The cabinet's aim is reached in the act of renouncing its abuse, and no one says so in the chamber.

**E2. Guaymas's chartered district (`C-1932-GUAYMAS-DISTRICT`; PROPOSED).**
Guaymas has had its port and railway since 1867: "A Guaymas railway concession and free port go with it" (`L687 ("A Guaymas railway concession and free port")`). By 1913 Sonora "runs on Pacifican copper, the Guaymas railway and the Sonora customs" (`L1342 ("the Guaymas railway and the Sonora customs")`), and in the 1920s there is "a casino resort on the Sonoran shore at Guaymas" (`L1024 ("a casino resort on the Sonoran shore at Guaymas")`). What "waits on Mexico" (`L1681 ("waits on Mexico")`) is the industrial district beside the port, which only Mexico can charter.
- **Why now.** Mexico has waited since 1920. In 1932 Calles is still the power in Mexico, and he is one of the Sonorans whose state "runs on Pacifican copper, the Guaymas railway and the Sonora customs"; silver and oil revenues have collapsed.
- **The charter.** In the summer of 1932 Mexico charters the district by its own act, under Mexican courts, customs and labour law and with Article 27 untouched. **Alberto J. Pani** (*Hist.*: Treasury Secretary in 1932; the day he took office is not confirmed) signs for Mexico.
- **What Mexico gets.** Smelting and canning wages in Sonora in a year when silver and oil revenues have collapsed, and bonds for the port placed privately in San Francisco.
- **What Pacifica gets.** Sonoran copper smelted beside its own railway.
- **What Mexico refuses.** Any Pacifican customs officer, court or guard inside the district. None of canon's chartered districts has them; Mexico has it written down all the same.
- **In the Act.** The charter is annexed, and Mexico signs both articles. Its signature under Article I costs it nothing and is worth little; the draft says so. `C-1867-GUAYMAS` is not amended.
