Step 4 of the plan of 25 November 2026, 14:36. Six questions for the author, each with a recommendation. The answers become D-18 onwards in `CR-088.md`. Below them is a list of corrections that follow from canon and are made without asking. Nothing here changes canon.

## Q1. The Treaty of Edo's sunset: a fixed date or conditional on Japan's codes? (`A1.2` item 3, `A1.3`)

**The problem.** The draft says both that the sunset is a "twelve-year transition" and that it ends "automatically… once Japan publishes modern commercial and maritime codes". In our timeline Japan's Criminal Code dates from 1880 (in force 1882), the Constitution from 1889, the Commercial Code from 1890 (partly in force 1893, replaced in 1899) and the Civil Code from 1896–98. A sunset conditional on the codes cannot end in 1870, but canon's alliance of 1870–71 is already equal (`L747 ("The 1870–1871 Japanese alliance is equal")`).

- **A (recommended): a fixed date.** The 1858 treaty names the date. Japan undertakes to publish codes, but the end does not wait for them. Elias's reason is capability, not fairness: a Japan with its own courts and tariff is a stronger partner against the Atlantic powers, and a date kept is the one proof of Pacifica's word that Tokyo can plan around for the next sixty years. The panels also matter less to California's merchants each year, as their trade passes to Japanese-staffed houses.
- **B: conditional on the codes.** The end moves to the 1890s, contradicts `L747` and puts Pacifica in Britain's company.

## Q2. Who judges Californians in Japan from 1858 to 1870? (`A1.2` item 3; `L306`, `L747`)

**The problem.** Canon says the treaty strengthens Japan's options "without openly ceding the country's sovereignty" (`L306 ("without openly ceding the country's sovereignty")`), and that the 1870–71 alliance has "no consular courts" (`L747`). The draft gives mixed Californian–Japanese panels in designated ports and then has Pacifica "abolish consular courts" in 1870.

- **A: no special jurisdiction at all.** Californians are under shogunal law from 1858. Canon is untouched, but in 1858 (no codes, interrogation by torture) California's merchants and captains would refuse to trade on those terms, and the date of 1870 loses its meaning.
- **B (recommended): mixed panels with a Japanese presiding judge, in two treaty ports, ending in 1870.** One Californian and one Japanese judge sit with a Japanese president. That is shared jurisdiction, not a cession, so it fits `L306`'s words. It also fits `L747` exactly: Pacifica never had consular courts at all, which is the sharpest contrast with the Atlantic powers. `A1.3`'s "abolition of consular courts" becomes "the end of the mixed panels". I would record this as canon tension `T-EDO-1`, with no fact change (no linter pattern tracks it), so that the reading of `L306` is yours rather than mine.

## Q3. The convention of 1881: whose, and what does it do? (`A3.1`, `A4.1`)

**The problem.** Canon secures family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans across the Realm only in the renewal of 1907 (`L1220`; `L1223 ("With Japanese families admitted under the compact")`). It also has an earlier Japanese emigration scheme behind Wakamatsu (`L783`, PROPOSED), and the population model has about 35,000 Japanese-origin residents in 1870 and 107,000 in 1880 (MODEL; `CR-089` `A13`). A Realm-wide convention in 1881 would make 1907 redundant. In our timeline Japan allowed contract emigration to Hawaii only from 1885, after Kalākaua's request of 1881, under the convention of 1886 (*Hist.*).

- **A (recommended): the Kingdom of Hawaii's own convention.** Family passages to Hawaii; no penal contracts (Hawaii's law of 1856, `L367`); wives and children travel with or after the men. It binds neither California nor the Confederation. On the mainland the earlier scheme and the assisted passages carry on. The 1907 renewal makes family entry and civic equality a treaty right across the Realm, as canon has it. The convention starts the family pattern in Hawaii, not in the Realm.
- **B: a Realm-wide convention in 1881.** Needs a canon tension against `L1220` and `L1223`, and takes from Tokyo its win of 1907.
- **C: no convention.** 1881 is only the visit and its honours.

## Q4. Kalākaua in Tokyo, 1881: the marriage proposal (`A3.1`)

**Our timeline (*Hist.*).** Kalākaua reached Yokohama on 4 March 1881. In a private audience he proposed to the Meiji Emperor that his five-year-old niece Kaʻiulani marry the young Prince Higashifushimi Yorihito, and that Japan lead a union of Asian nations. Both proposals were declined politely in letters of 1882; the prince pleaded a prior engagement. The Emperor gave him the Grand Cordon of the Order of the Chrysanthemum, Japan's highest order.

**In canon.** Kalākaua is not king. He is Victoria's minister of the image and her envoy (`L848`), and dies in San Francisco in 1891. Kaʻiulani is his niece (`L850`), lives (CR-043), becomes common Foreign minister from 1931 (CR-084) and signs the Final Act last, seated (CR-086). The one dynastic marriage between the courts that happens is Chiyo's, in 1907. Kahekili's betrothal is a grievance of 1932.

- **A (recommended): keep the proposal as Kalākaua's own.** He makes it beyond his instructions. Tokyo declines courteously in 1882. Victoria, told afterwards, says nothing in public and makes him answerable, as she does after the mutiny (`L818`). The first dynastic idea between the courts thus comes from Hawaii and is refused; the one that happens, in 1907, comes from the Kuroda side. No sentence on the page links 1881 to Kaʻiulani's ministry of 1931, by the same rule as for 1919 (CR-086).
- **B: leave it out.** 1881 keeps the visit, the honours and (per Q3) the convention.

Under either option, three historical details change because Kalākaua is an envoy and not a sovereign:
- He receives the Grand Cordon of the Order of the Rising Sun, not the Chrysanthemum.
- "The first reigning foreign monarch to visit Japan" is dropped.
- No federation of Asian nations is proposed, since Hawaii is already a member of the Confederation.

The band's music at Yokohama is left unnamed. *Hawaiʻi Ponoʻī* was written by Kalākaua as king in 1874, and canon says nothing of an anthem.

## Q5. The rescue of 1927: the figure (`A2.1` item 5)

**A correction to my review of 25 November.** "$600 million" is not invented. It is the workbook's committed credit: $496.861m drawn within $600m committed, of which $165.6m is public and $331.2m private (`finance/CR-031-review.md`). The Treasury's $166m is repaid in five instalments of $33m from 1929 to 1933 (`finance/CR-083-086-workbook.md`). The storyline gives no amount, and CR-024's amounts are MODEL.

- **Recommended:** keep the figure, labelled MODEL, as "a foreign credit of about $600 million committed, about $497 million drawn (MODEL)". Keep canon's division of labour: Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money, and Pacifica supplies the foreign credit (`L1607`).

## Corrections made without asking (canon, steps 5–7)

Option A (approved) reverses `L300`'s "his failure to reach Japan". The impact note lists that sentence, and the forbidden pattern is corrected to catch canon's actual wording.

5. **Mixed residence (*naichi zakkyo*)** stays a live question in the European treaty revisions of 1894–99. Pacifica not demanding it softens the debate; it does not remove it (`A1.3`, `A5.2`).
6. **1905:** "southern gains at Urajio" becomes canon's wording. Witte signs at Urajio and recognises Japan's southern gains (`L1213`).
