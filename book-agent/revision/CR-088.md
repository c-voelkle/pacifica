## A. The change

### A1. The founding trust: Perry, Shimoda, Edo and equal law (1853–1871)

*Closes Unresolved #14 (`L2002 ("Japanese alternate treaties")`) and anchors why Pacifica is never filed under the unequal-treaty powers in Japanese memory.*

**A1.1. Perry's reshaped 1853–54 mission (APPROVED direction; operational detail PROPOSED).**
Canon leaves Perry's altered mission OPEN and rejects a coaling embargo as its explanation (`L300 ("Matthew Perry")`). In this timeline Commodore Matthew Perry **does** reach Uraga and Edo Bay in 1853 and 1854, sailing via the Atlantic, Cape of Good Hope, Macao, and Naha (*Hist.* for the Atlantic/Indian Ocean route). His route and his lack of a Pacific base are what they were in our timeline; the real Perry had no Californian harbour behind him either. What changes is what waits for him:
- Without California (no San Francisco, no Mare Island, and a sharper North–South sectional struggle in Washington after 1850), Washington has no Pacific coast of its own to argue from, and nothing behind the East India Squadron but the long Atlantic road.
- Crucially, **Edo is not caught blind, and this is the difference that matters.** California's deniable Satsuma/Ryukyu channel—opened through Nakahama Manjirō (1851), Hamada Hikozō, and Shimazu Nariakira—has already delivered charts, intelligence on Western navies, and California's modest, non-missionary technical offer before Perry's black ships anchor.
- **Abe Masahiro** (`CT-ABE`) uses California deliberately as a diplomatic counterweight. Rather than facing a monolithic Western gunboat demand, the Bakufu grants Perry limited shipwreck and provisioning terms while quietly authorizing Satsuma-brokered technical exchanges with California in 1854, maturing into the **1855 Shimoda agreement** (`C-1855-SHIMODA`). In Japanese political memory, the opening of the country is tied to an immediate distinction: **the Atlantic powers arrived with threats; California arrived first with charts, castaways, and no priests.**

**A1.2. The 1858 Treaty of Edo (`C-1858-EDO`; clauses PROPOSED).**
Concluded in 1858 (`L306 ("Treaty of Edo")`) during the Ansei crisis after Abe's and Nariakira's deaths, the treaty is built against the model Townsend Harris imposes for the United States the same summer (*Hist.* for Harris's 1858 treaty). Elias's reason is capability, not fairness (D-29, PROPOSED). He does not hold that nations are owed equal terms. He holds that Japan can be made strong, that a strong Japan is worth more to the Republic than a dependent one, and that every clause should make Japan stronger in ways that bind it to California. Each article below therefore has two halves: what Japan gains, and what the Republic gains by giving it.
- **What California gets** is in the treaty too: coal, silk, copper and tools on stated terms (`L302 ("Coal, provisions, silk, copper, tools")`); Japanese shipwrights and naval students in California's yards; and a partner across the ocean that is not Britain's.
1. **The bullion clause:** Elias warns shogunal and Satsuma officials of the gap between Japan's domestic gold–silver ratio, about 1:5, and the world's, about 1:15 (*Hist.* for Japan's pre-1859 disparity; canon, `L304 ("he warns his partners and offers financial expertise")`). California's coin is exchanged by assayed weight and fineness.
   - *What Japan gains:* its gold stock, which would otherwise drain abroad.
   - *What the Republic gains:* the gap pays whoever holds silver, and in 1858 that means the Mexican-dollar houses of Hong Kong and Shanghai, which are British and American. California holds gold, so the warning costs it little and denies its rivals a windfall. The assay itself is a product: a Japan that has learned to trust Californian weights brings its old coinage to Ralston's bank to be refined for the new yen in 1872 (`L755 ("his bank contracts to refine Japan's old coinage for the new yen")`).
   - Japan still loses gold to the European traders, and so learns the difference by measurement, not by assurance. The rule behind the clause is the Republic's own: a falsified account "robs everyone who must build on it" (`L242 ("falsified account robs everyone who must build on it")`), and a false weight in a treaty is the same.
2. **The no-missionaries article:** California renounces any state-backed or treaty-protected mission on Japanese soil. It is the Republic's general rule, kept with China too (`L762 ("policy of no missionaries")`).
   - *What Japan gains:* relief from its deepest ideological fear since the Shimabara rising of 1637–38.
   - *What the Republic gains:* it gives up a lever it never wanted, and the duty that comes with it. A protected mission is a pretext. Honolulu learned that in 1839, when a French frigate took a toleration edict and a $20,000 bond in the missions' name (`A1.2` item 3). A murdered missionary is a casus belli that his protector must answer or lose face. The Republic will owe Japan no such answer, and Japan learns that California will never come with priests and then with ships. The clause costs the Republic nothing and is worth more to Edo than any other.
3. **Mixed panels and a fixed sunset (APPROVED, D-18, D-19; details PROPOSED):** Instead of consular courts and a tariff schedule Japan cannot change (in our timeline Harris's treaty fixed duties, mostly 20 per cent, and the uniform 5 per cent came with the convention of 1866, *Hist.*), the treaty sets a **fixed date, twelve years on, in 1870**. Until then a case involving a Californian at Nagasaki or Kanagawa is heard by a **panel of one Californian and one Japanese judge under a Japanese president**, with reasons written in both languages; outside those ports Japanese law applies. On the date the panels end and the tariff is Japan's own. Japan undertakes to publish commercial and maritime codes, but the end does not wait for them (in our timeline the Criminal Code came in 1880, the Commercial Code in 1890 and the Civil Code in 1896–98, *Hist.*). Pacifica never has a consular court in Japan, and this is meant to be noticed.
   - **Why California can do what the Atlantic powers will not (PROPOSED).** Four reasons, none of them kindness:
     - *Hawaii's memory.* The Confederation's Hawaiian half has lived under the system from inside. In 1839 the French frigate *L'Artémise* extracted the Edict of Toleration and a $20,000 bond from Kamehameha III; in 1843 a British captain seized the islands (`L360 ("the British seizure of 1843")`); and the treaty with France of 1846 had every Frenchman tried by a jury of natives or of foreigners proposed by the French consul until a new treaty was signed in October 1857 (*Hist.*; whether the jury clause survived it is to verify). The Confederation's negotiators, Hawaii's among them, will not have the Confederation do to Japan what Honolulu still endures.
     - *No gunboats to spare.* A consular court rests on a squadron that can enforce it. In 1858 the Republic's ships are few and needed at home, and a court it could not enforce would be a court the shogunate would learn to ignore.
     - *The panel is a school.* Elias wants Japanese judges who can try a Californian merchant by 1870, and the only way to get them is to have them try Californians from 1858. The Japanese judges of the panels are the first commercial judges of Meiji (PROPOSED).
     - *The comparison is the point.* Every Japanese official who deals with Harris's consular court and with the Nagasaki panel in the same year learns which partner treats Japan as a state. Elias builds that comparison on purpose.
   - **Who objects.** California's Nagasaki merchants petition for Harris's terms in 1859, and the Council refuses them (PROPOSED).
4. **Technical and student reciprocity:** two-way shipyard and foundry training, leading directly to the March 1860 *Kanrin Maru* engineers' request for Japanese admission to Carquinez (`L308 ("Kanrin Maru")`).
   - *What Japan gains:* the methods.
   - *What the Republic gains:* the people. Japanese engineers become the Polytechnic's competitors and colleagues, so each side improves the other's work (`CR-031-R1`; `L760 ("Japanese and Pacifican engineers learn from one another and compete")`). Carquinez recruits its talent from every background (`CR-034`), and Japan is the deepest pool across the ocean.

**A1.3. Honoring the sunset in 1870–1871 (`C-1870-JP-ALLIANCE`; PROPOSED connective detail).**
Following Pacifica's restraint during the 1868 Edo transition (`L745 ("negotiated transition at Edo")`), Elias keeps the 1858 date in the **1870–1871 Japanese alliance** (`L747 ("1870–1871 Japanese alliance")`). The mixed panels end, the tariff is Japan's, and there is **no demand for unrestricted foreign landownership in the Japanese interior** (*naichi zakkyo* stays under Japanese domestic law; see `A5.2`).
- **The trade, not the gift (PROPOSED reading of canon):** the same alliance gives the Republic dockyard rights at Yokosuka, a coaling lease at Hakodate and Takashima coal (`L747`). Equality is what Japan gets; a western Pacific base system without a single sovereign foothold is what the Republic gets. A sovereign base would be a grievance Japan would one day fight to end. A lease from an equal is a right Japan will keep granting as long as it profits from it. The partnership is renewed by compact from 1887, every ten years (`L991`).
- **Why no interior land:** the Republic's merchants trade at the ports and do not need the interior. Demanding it would make Pacifica's treaty the template the Atlantic powers cite, and would aim Japan's grievance at Thalassia instead of London. Canon's "no consular courts" (`L747`) stands as written: there were never any. Mixed residence stays a live question all the same, because Britain and the Atlantic powers make it their price in the revisions of 1894–99 (*Hist.*), so the Japanese debate is softened, not removed. Pacifica achieves legal equality with Japan **twenty-four to forty-one years ahead** of Britain (1894/1899) and the Atlantic powers (*Hist.* for OTL's 1894 Anglo-Japanese treaty taking effect in 1899 and full tariff autonomy in 1911).

### A2. Why Japan accepts Pacifica: its own interest first, the broken ladder second (1853–1932)

Japan accepts the partnership because it wins by it, and the Japanese who say so are reasoning people with names. Pacifica's good conduct is the second reason, not the first. Japanese in our timeline grew xenophobic in a world where they were humiliated and robbed of the fruits of their wars. Here they are neither, and they can count the difference. *(Research behind this section: `outputs/sessions/20261125-cr088-japan/research-gains.md`.)*

**A2.1. The bargain on Japanese grounds (synthesis of canon; APPROVED direction, 25 November 2026, 14:36; wording PROPOSED).**
A Japanese statesman can defend the partnership in the Diet without one word of gratitude. Five sentences carry it, each resting on canon:
1. **Japan wins its wars with Pacifica, and is paid for the ones Pacifica stops.** In 1894 Victoria cuts the war short with Korea independent, an indemnity and Japan's economic predominance (`L1084`). In 1898–99 the army gets the land war it was denied and keeps the Ussuri region, Vladivostok, the Razdolnaya and all Sakhalin (`L1092 ("Japan gains the Ussuri region")`). In 1905 Witte comes to Urajio to sign (`L1213`). In our timeline Japan won its wars and lost their fruit, to Europe in 1895 and to its own exhausted treasury in 1905 (*Hist.*).
2. **Japan's territory grows without colonies of rice-growers.** Enkaishū, Urajio and Karafuto, then Kamchatka and the Koryak coast under Japanese protection from 1921 (`L1515 ("a republic under Japanese protection")`), are cold lands, settled and worked; the Marshall atolls from 1914 (`A2.6`) are coral, copra and fish, not rice. Korea stays independent, "so Japan runs no drive to draw a colony's rice into its home market, and the Japanese farmer's price is not broken by one" (`L1704 ("Korea is independent, so Japan runs no drive")`), and its rice keeps pace with its people from the 1890s (`L1188 ("Japan's home rice crop keeps pace with its people from the 1890s")`). The denial the army most resents, Korea (`A5`), is the reason Japan's villages do not starve.
3. **Japan's standing rises in every treaty.** Equal in 1870–71 (`L747`); its subjects equal in the Realm from 1907, because "a more confident Japan bargains hard" (`L1220 ("A more confident Japan bargains hard")`); its racial-equality clause in the peace of 1919 (`L1396 ("Japan's racial-equality clause")`); the combined fleets at parity with Britain (`L1403 ("Naval limits count the combined Pacifica–Japan fleets at parity with Britain")`, PROPOSED in canon); a co-equal party in 1927 (`L1615 ("Japan's Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council's conditional advice")`); the Navy seat by treaty law in 1932 (`L1619 ("the Navy seat is Japanese by treaty law")`).
4. **Japan's money is rescued and never conquered.** It receives credit in 1870–71, in 1873, for the gold transition of 1897 (`L1088 ("Pacifican gold loan support Japan's gold transition")`, PROPOSED in canon), after the earthquake of 1923 and in 1927. Each time the money is lent, never collected by a customs administration or a debt commission. In 1927 Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money and Pacifica supplies the foreign credit (`L1607`).
5. **Japan keeps what it cares most about, and refuses what it does not want.** The Emperor in Japan, the home army, the *koseki*, the schools and the language stay its own (`A4.3`; `L1626 ("Japan grants English no domestic official status")`). In 1871 Kido and Itō take Pacifica's money and refuse its constitution (`L749 ("Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi have read the Articles of 1856")`). In 1932 Takahashi refuses Julian the home army (`L1788`). Both refusals stand.

The grievances of `A5` are the price, and each has its holders.

**A2.2. Who in Japan argues for it, and against (PROPOSED).**
One voice for the partnership in each era, all already in canon, and one against, so that the case is an argument and not a brochure:
- **The 1850s: Shimazu Nariakira and Abe Masahiro.** Nariakira sees "a modest Pacific partner without missionary demands" as an alternative to stronger outside powers (`L300 ("a modest Pacific partner without missionary demands")`). Abe uses it as a counterweight: the shogunate can refuse one power by dealing with another. *Against:* the factions that distrust the treaty (`L306 ("A number of Japanese factions distrust the treaty")`) and, after 1858, the *shishi*.
- **1871: Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi.** They read the Articles of 1856 and conclude that Japan cannot afford a confederation of militias and vetoes (`L749`). Take the credit, learn the methods, build a unitary state: this is the partnership's first and most durable pattern. *Against, from 1877:* Satsuma. Pacifica backs the Meiji government against Saigō "and its oldest Japanese friends" (`L779 ("Pacifica backs the Meiji government against Saigō")`). From Saigō's cause come the Gen'yōsha of Fukuoka (1881) and, through its heirs, the Pan-Asian right whose charge in 1932 is not that Pacifica is foreign but that it chose Tokyo's state over Satsuma's honour (`A5`).
- **1907 and 1927: Saionji Kinmochi.** In 1907 he bargains with the Sacramento crisis as his lever (`L1220`) and refuses a Japanese count a crown equal to the Emperor's (`L1250 ("Saionji's government will not let a count of Japan take a crown")`). In 1927 he counsels acceptance of the Concord (`L1938 ("Saionji counsels acceptance in 1927")`). His argument is the constitutional one: Japan gains most by being indispensable to a partner it can bargain with, and loses most by trying to command it. *Against:* the army's continental school, which resents the shortened victory of 1894 (`L1084 ("Japan's army resents the shortened victory")`) and Korea's independence, and the navy that "wants Tsingtao and the German islands" (`L1336 ("Its navy wants Tsingtao and the German islands")`) and is denied the first (`L1403 ("Pacifica keeps the Tsingtao anchorage on lease")`). The most articulate statement of the dignity case comes from Saionji's own protégé. In December 1918, before he follows Saionji to the peace conference, the young Prince Konoe Fumimaro publishes an essay against a peace made to suit the great powers' convenience (*Hist.*: "Reject the Anglo-American-Centred Peace"; here its target is a peace centred on Pacifica and Britain). Konoe is a background voice and is not registered (D-26).
- **1927 and 1932: Takahashi Korekiyo.** His moratorium buys the time in 1927 (`L1605 ("Takahashi's three-week moratorium")`). In 1932 his cabinet states its offer as "a trade it means to win" (`L1788 ("a trade it means to win")`), and wins most of it. *Against:* the Restoration officers, for whom "Japan's villages pay for San Francisco's prosperity" (`L1584 ("Japan's villages pay for San Francisco's prosperity")`); Araki on Manchuria's acres; Mori Kaku on "the bakufu made permanent" (`L1792 ("the bakufu made permanent")`); Kashiwagi and the Kronstadt dead (`L1709 ("Japanese sailors died there for a Pacifican's hostages")`).
- **A public voice outside government: Nitobe Inazō (D-25; see `A2.5`).**

**A2.3. Breaking our timeline's humiliation ladder (PROPOSED).**
Late-Edo *jōi* ("expel the barbarian") was a defensive reaction against colonial subjugation and Christian subversion, coexisting with intense *rangaku* curiosity for practical craft (*Hist.*). Japanese ultranationalism in our timeline was forged over seventy years by cumulative racial and diplomatic humiliations. On the Pacific axis canon breaks each of them, or turns it:
1. **Unequal treaties (our timeline, 1858–1911):** Pacifica never has a consular court and ends its mixed panels on the fixed date of 1870 (`A1.2`, `A1.3`). Britain and the Atlantic powers revise only in 1894–99.
2. **The Triple Intervention of 1895:** none. Victoria's armistice of 1894 (`L1084 ("Victoria supports Japan's case for Korean independence")`) costs the army the mainland gains it hoped for, but by agreement, not by ultimatum.
3. **Portsmouth and the Hibiya riots of 1905:** avoided. Victoria advises Japan to refuse Roosevelt's mediation; Pacifican pressure on French lending brings Witte to terms; and Witte comes to Urajio to sign Russia's withdrawal from Manchuria, an indemnity and the recognition of Japan's southern gains (`L1213 ("Witte comes to Urajio")`).
4. **Racial exclusion (our timeline: San Francisco's schools in 1906, the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907–08, the Alien Land Laws of 1913 and 1920, Paris in 1919, the Exclusion Act of 1924):** not avoided at home, but answered. Inside the Realm the 1906 school crisis happens too: Silas's nativists and Harlan Tate's Sacramento board segregate Japanese pupils (`L1220`). Tokyo bargains hard in the renewal of 1907 and wins a joint war council, family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans. In the same year as our timeline's restriction, Japan forces a gain. Pacifica passes no exclusion or alien land law. Japanese gains Realm standing in 1927 and county standing in Hawaii in 1928 (`L1043 ("English is the Realm")`). At The Hague in 1919 Japan wins its racial-equality clause and loses Shandong, the reverse of Paris (`L1396`, `L1403`). Three doors stay barred: **British Columbia**, until Caledonia's Proclamation Day about 18 July 1932 (`L1160`, `L2037`); **White Australia**, which refuses the clause even in 1932 (`L1870`); and the **Union**, whose immigration restrictions make Roosevelt's courtship of Tokyo unreliable (`L1220 ("his immigration restrictions make the courtship unreliable")`). Two of the three are British.
5. **Naval ratios (our timeline: 5:5:3 in 1922, London in 1930):** the ratio grievance moves inside the Concord. The combined fleets count at parity with Britain (`L1403`), and Japan argues instead over the quota, the yards' share and the reserved squadron (`A5`).
6. **Abandonment in crisis (our timeline: London and New York in the panic of 1927, the gold standard of 1930–31):** in 1923 Julian brings relief and reconstruction credit to Yokohama; in 1927, when London's acceptance houses pull out first, he supplies the foreign credit while Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money (`L1607 ("Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money")`). The credit is about $600 million committed and about $497 million drawn (MODEL, `finance/CR-031-review.md`).

**A2.4. Where Japanese xenophobia and anxiety *do* survive (APPROVED direction for returnees; structure PROPOSED).**
Japanese suspicion does not vanish. It **splits along two axes**:
- **Outward toward the Atlantic and the British dominions:** racial-dignity resentment concentrates on Britain, Russia, the Union, British Columbia and White Australia. That makes the Pacific partnership feel like a shield of mutual dignity rather than submission to the West.
- **Inward against Pacifica, as anxiety about *kokutai* and soft absorption:** critics of Pacifica rarely call Pacificans barbarians; too many Japanese know Hawaiʻi, California, Chiyo and Julian firsthand. The critique is sharper and more intimate: **that Pacifica's wealth, examination ethos, English technical command and dynastic marriages are quietly turning Japan into a cultured province of a "Thalassia bakufu"** (`L1681 ("Japanese Restoration circles call it a new bakufu")`). Its oldest root is Satsuma's grievance of 1877, not foreignness (`A2.2`). The full catalogue is in `A5`.

**A2.5. Nitobe Inazō: the scholar the Realm asks for (APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 16:32, D-25; particulars PROPOSED).**
*Hist.* Nitobe Inazō (1862–1933) was in the second class of the Sapporo Agricultural College and studied agricultural economics at Johns Hopkins and Halle, where he took his doctorate. At nine he was sent to Tokyo as his uncle's heir and took the name Ōta Inazō, reverting to Nitobe when his elder brother died. He later served as Under-Secretary-General of the League of Nations, and died at Victoria, British Columbia, in 1933. His *Bushido: The Soul of Japan* (preface dated Malvern, Pennsylvania, 1899) began with a Belgian professor's question, "No religion! How do you impart moral education?". It answered with a chivalric, Christian-tinted portrait of samurai virtue for a Western audience, which Japanese and Western scholars have since called an invention (*Hist.*).

**In the Realm.** Nitobe is a Sapporo man of the northern school that Sapporo and Sitka built together (`L739`), and Satō Shōsuke's junior there. His graduate years are at Carquinez instead of Johns Hopkins, and his doctorate at Halle, as in our timeline. As a Sapporo professor in the 1890s he is among the drafters of the Hokkaido Ainu Act of 1899 (`A5.1`), which tests his colonial-policy learning against Sitka's tools. His audience differs from our timeline's. The Realm does not need Japan made romantic to respect it, and its ethos forbids the romance (`CR-076`: never glamour). Its examination state has already answered the Belgian's question in its own way: churches keep their schools, the state pays only for what it examines (`L45`), and conduct is measured, not preached. So the Belgian's question reaches Nitobe in reverse. In about 1900 the Polytechnic asks him, at a challenge evening with a respondent (`L405 ("challenge evening begins there as a laboratory habit")`; Peirce, who held a Japan fellowship at seventeen, is the natural one), to show by evidence what made Japan able to learn so fast.

**What a rigorous Nitobe tells them (PROPOSED; the substance is *Hist.* scholarship, the occasion is the Realm's):**
1. **There was no single code.** What foreigners call bushido is largely a Meiji reading of scattered Tokugawa texts. The samurai of 1700–1850 were a salaried administrative class: clerks, accountants, magistrates and teachers on stipends, most of whom never fought.
2. **Japan learned fast because of institutions, not virtues:**
   - Two centuries of domain schools and some fifteen thousand commoners' *terakoya* gave it one of the most literate populations of its age.
   - Village self-government in five-household groups made a habit of collective liability and record-keeping.
   - Merchant houses such as Mitsui ran as corporations that outlived their families. The Osaka rice exchange traded futures from the 1730s.
   - Ishida Baigan's *shingaku* gave commoners an ethic of honest dealing.
3. **Adoption is Japan's market in talent.** A house that lacks an able heir adopts one, often a son-in-law, and gives him its name. Nitobe was adopted himself, as Kiyoshi was in 1886 (`L989`). That is the nearest Japanese equivalent of "achievement outranks birth", and the reason a Japanese family can read Carquinez's examination. It is also why the examination itself is foreign to Japan, which never kept China's (*Hist.*: the ritsuryō state had one, and the bakufu's *gakumon ginmi* of 1792 was a test for retainers, not a ladder to office).
4. **The danger is loyalty without an account.** Japan's ethic is loyalty to a house and a lord, not truth to a measure. Its most celebrated story, the forty-seven rōnin, praises men who broke the law for their lord's honour and were sentenced for it. And its own recent past shows where that leads: the men who cut down Ii Naosuke at the Sakurada Gate in 1860, the rising at Saga in 1874 and Saigō's war of 1877 each claimed to serve the Emperor against his ministers. "A loyalty that answers to no ledger," he tells the Polytechnic, "has been claimed by men who act in the Emperor's name against the Emperor's government, and it will be again." It is the historian's reading of Chūshingura, of *gekokujō* and of the Restoration's own violence (*Hist.*).
5. **What Japan should take from the Realm, and what it should refuse.** Take the measure, the published account and the open licence. Refuse the examination as the road to power: Japan's houses and its adopted heirs already find talent, and an examination aristocracy would hand the country to the Carquinez returnees (`A5.2`). This is a Japanese scholar's case for the partnership on Japanese terms, made to the Realm's face.

**Why the Realm values him.** He tells the Polytechnic what it did not want to hear and proves it. Peirce's response, which he gives, is the evening's other half (`C`, set piece). The lectures are printed in English and Japanese. The Restoration circles never forgive the fourth point, and the Japanese purists never forgive the fifth.
- **After 1900 (PROPOSED, sketch):** women's education, as in our timeline; a seat on the Hague court's panel of arbitrators after 1919 in place of the League (canon has a permanent arbitration court, `L1403`); and the Japanese council of a Pacific relations institute at Honolulu from 1925 (*Hist.*: the Institute of Pacific Relations was founded at Honolulu in 1925). His death at Victoria in October 1933 falls after the book's end.
- **Registration:** `CT-NITOBE-INAZO` (1862–1933) joins the cast of `A7` as a person who can carry a scene.

**A2.6. The Marshalls: the one German group Japan keeps (APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 17:03, D-27; particulars PROPOSED).**
- **An old gap.** The Marshalls have stood inside Pacifica's ring of islands since 1885, by Pacifica's own bargain. At the papal arbitration Pacifica took the Carolines, and "Germany keeps the Marshalls" (`L804 ("Germany keeps the Marshalls")`). Pacifica wanted the Carolines' harbours and Wake for a cable, and left the copra atolls to the German trading houses at Jaluit. So in 1919 Japan does not open a hole in the ring. It inherits one Germany held for thirty-four years.
- **1914.** The Japanese navy, which "wants Tsingtao and the German islands" (`L1336 ("Its navy wants Tsingtao and the German islands")`), takes Jaluit and the Marshalls in the autumn, as in our timeline (*Hist.*). Meanwhile Pacifica and its partners take Samoa, Rabaul and Nauru (`L1336 ("Pacifica and Japan take Germany")`).
- **1919.** The Treaty of The Hague confirms the division. The Marshalls go to Japan; Samoa, German New Guinea with Rabaul, and Nauru go to Pacifica, which holds them, like Micronesia, in Ka Lōkahi's trust (`L1336`, `L1615 ("Ka Lōkahi takes the Marianas, Carolines and Palau into its trust")`). The division follows the equator, roughly the line of our timeline's own wartime division (*Hist.*). **Nauru** lies south of the line, but the Germans governed it from Jaluit with the Marshalls, so the treaty names it separately: it is detached from the Marshalls' administration for its phosphate and stays in Pacifica's trust. Japan buys its phosphate at the same published price as Australia and New Zealand, because Japan's fields need it and the Realm does not withhold the means of food.
- **Why Pacifica agrees (Julian's reasoning):**
  - *Japan is already there,* and the same conference has already refused it Tsingtao (`L1403`). A second refusal to an ally at one peace would break the partnership on its own ground. Shandong matters to Pacifica's China policy; the atolls cost it nothing. Japan's ambition goes onto coral instead of Chinese soil.
  - *Inside the ring is the safest place for Japanese expansion.* Pacifica and its trust hold most of the ring round the group: Wake to the north, the Carolines and Guam to the west, Nauru to the south-west, the equatorial islands to the east; the British Gilberts lie to the south (`CR-084`). The trunk wire runs north of it (`L1195 ("a second span from Midway by Wake to Guam")`). The Marshalls article itself forbids fortifying the group, and it depends on Pacifican coal, cables and copra buyers. The cipher room never reads Japanese traffic (`L1679 ("The cipher room never reads Japanese traffic")`); here geography does the watching.
  - *What Japan wants there is cheap to give:* the southern school's pride, and bonito grounds for Okinawan fishermen. The fish's way to the kitchen is `CR-089`'s.
- **The three conditions of 1919 (PROPOSED):**
  1. No fortification, a term of the Marshalls article itself and not of the naval limits of 1919.
  2. No alienation of Marshallese land. Japan writes Ka Lōkahi's rule into its own law, a second case of the mutual influence of `A5.1`.
  3. **Freedom of worship and no expulsion.** No church, school or teacher already in the islands may be closed or expelled for its faith, and the Marshallese worship as they choose. The clause names no mission and gives no foreign protector a right of complaint, so the Republic keeps its own rule: in 1858 California promised Japan no treaty-protected missions (`A1.2`), and in 1919 it asks for none. It answers the Honolulu churches, whose Micronesia mission sent Hawaiian teachers to the Marshalls from the late 1850s (*Hist.*, to verify), as a rule for the islands, like the Pope's condition for the Carolines in 1885 (`L804`), not as a protector's right. Tokyo's Home Ministry reads it as a mission clause by another name (G28).
- **The grievance runs the other way, for once.** Pacifica's admirals object in 1919 to a Japanese group inside the ring, and the Honolulu churches distrust Japanese rule over their congregations. The admirals' objection fades after 1927, when the common Navy guards the whole ring, and in 1932 its minister is Japanese. The churches' distrust does not fade entirely. Hawaii's sealed minute on its island claims (`CR-075`) does not reach the Marshalls; its claims are the uninhabited islands of its own chain and the equatorial line.

### A3. Civilizational fit: why two societies mesh across the Pacific

**A3.1. Hawaiʻi as the non-European monarchical bridge — Kalākaua's March 1881 Tokyo tour (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
**Hawaii's crown is Polynesian**: it is Victoria Kamāmalu's from 1872, she is Regent of the Realm from 1887, and the Realm's sovereign from 1907 is Julian, her hānai son of Noe's Maui line, who holds both crowns. The court sits on a volcanic Pacific island, carry a civic language of *mana* and send no state missionaries. Before 1887 the Japanese see two things side by side: Elias's republic, which has kept its word since 1858, and the Queen's court.
- **March 1881 (`C-1881-TOKYO-EMIGRATION`, PROPOSED):** Adapting his real tour (*Hist.*: he reached Yokohama on 4 March 1881 as King, and the Meiji Emperor gave him the Grand Cordon of the Order of the Chrysanthemum), **David Kalākaua** (`CT-DAVID-KALAKAUA`) visits Tokyo as **Victoria Kamāmalu's minister of the image and envoy** (`L848`), not as a sovereign. He receives the Grand Cordon of the Order of the Rising Sun; the band at Yokohama plays Hawaii's anthem, unnamed (PROPOSED).
- **The convention (APPROVED, D-20; terms PROPOSED).** Kalākaua concludes the **1881 Hawaii–Japan Family Emigration Convention**, the Kingdom of Hawaii's own: family passages to Hawaii, no penal contracts (Hawaii's abolition of 1856, `L51`), wives and children travelling with the men or after them. It is signed on the Confederation's behalf, as Hawaii signed for Pago Pago in 1878 (`L804 ("on the Confederation's behalf")`), because the common institutions hold external relations (`L364 ("customs, currency and external relations")`); the Council consents on the condition that its terms bind Hawaii alone. It binds neither California nor the Confederation's other members. On the mainland Japanese families keep coming under the earlier scheme and the assisted passages (`L783`), and the right to family entry and civic equality across the Realm is Tokyo's win in the renewal of 1907 (`L1220`). In our timeline Japan allowed contract emigration to Hawaii only from 1885, after Kalākaua's request (*Hist.*).
- **The proposal (APPROVED, D-21, with canon's terms; details PROPOSED).** In our timeline Kalākaua proposed in a private audience that his five-year-old niece Kaʻiulani marry a young imperial prince, and that Japan lead a union of Asian nations; both were declined in letters of 1882 (*Hist.*). Canon keeps the shape and changes the ocean: he proposes **an Oceanian union sealed by marrying Kaʻiulani to Prince Komatsu**, and the Emperor politely declines (`L848 ("proposes an Oceanian union")`). Here that proposal goes beyond his instructions. He is Victoria's envoy, not a sovereign; Hawaii is a member of the Confederation, whose institutions hold external relations; and a union of Pacific crowns is not his to offer. That is why he offers it: the vision is his own, and he thinks a match will carry what an envoy cannot sign. Tokyo declines courteously in 1882, the prince pleading a prior engagement. Victoria, told afterwards, says nothing in public and makes him answerable, as after the mutiny (`L818 ("makes him answerable for the Guard's loyalty")`). The first dynastic idea between the courts thus comes from Hawaii and is refused; the one that happens, in 1907, comes from the Kuroda side (`L1258 ("In November 1907 Julian marries Lady Chiyo")`). No sentence on the page links 1881 to Kaʻiulani's ministry of 1931.
- **What the visit leaves.** Kalākaua's reception stamps the image of **two sister Pacific monarchies** on the Japanese public.

**A3.2. Shared grammar of form, craft, and merit (PROPOSED synthesis of existing canon).**
Pacifica's examination state—*On the Public Arts*, anonymous scripts, achievement outranking birth, retirement at 68, the Five (*nā Kahuna*), and *the earned cup* (`L887`, linking Japanese *oshaku* with Hawaiian *ʻawa*)—is what the Japanese students at Carquinez write home about from 1860 (`L308`), and their families read it as *jitsugaku* (practical learning) given an institution's form. The domain men who sneer at it call it a merchants' school (PROPOSED). Shared hazards (earthquakes, tsunamis under the warnings compact, `C-1897-WARNINGS`, and typhoons) give the engineers of both countries the same first question about any building and any harbour.

**A3.3. Seismic engineering and San Francisco's *Pacific Modern* architecture (`C-1906-SEISMIC-CODE`; APPROVED direction; specifics PROPOSED).**
For Japanese pride to accept permanent union, cultural traffic must run **both ways**, and nowhere is that reversal more visible than in San Francisco—the world's pre-eminent city by the 1930s:
- **Seismic engineering (Japan teaches California and Thalassia):** Following the **April 1906 San Francisco earthquake** (*Hist.*), Japanese seismologist **Ōmori Fusakichi** (`CT-OMORI-FUSAKICHI`, already registered in `CR-085`) and structural engineer **Sano Toshikata** (`CT-SANO-TOSHIKATA`, *Hist.* 1880–1956) join the Polytechnic's reconstruction commission in San Francisco to map Bay-mud failure against bedrock (*Hist.* for Ōmori and Sano's 1906 California survey). Sano's pupil **Naitō Tachū** (`CT-NAITO-TACHU`, *Hist.* 1886–1970), a Tokyo Imperial University graduate who here spends a study year at Carquinez (PROPOSED), develops rigid-frame shear-wall design whose Tokyo buildings (Industrial Bank of Japan, Kabuki-za) survive the **1 September 1923 Great Kantō Earthquake** (*Hist.*). After 1923, Japan's 1924 seismic code and Naitō's reinforced-wall methods become the **shared Pacific standard** for Thalassia, San Francisco, and Manila.
- **San Francisco's equivalent to Art Deco — *Pacific Modern* (1910s–1930s):** Whereas New York's Art Deco chases thin Gothic verticality on Manhattan bedrock, San Francisco is rebuilt as a seismic, hydroelectric, aluminium-rich Pacific capital, fusing the Bay Area's timber tradition (Bernard Maybeck, Julia Morgan) and the Greenes' Pasadena houses with Japanese *kigumi* joinery and Naitō's shear-wall engineering:
  - *Terraced seismic massing:* Civic towers step back from battered stone bases echoing Japanese castle walls (*ishigaki*) and Pueblo adobe terraces.
  - *Structure as ornament:* Recessed reinforced-concrete frames clad in Sierra granite, volcanic tuff and matte anodized aluminium (from the aluminium champion, `CR-054`), under wide cantilevered copper-and-aluminium eaves (*tokyō* reimagined in metal) that shed Bay fog and conceal indirect electric uplighting.
  - *Thresholds and water courts:* Aluminium-and-redwood *shōji*-grid walls open lobbies and county halls onto interior water courts that borrow from the Californio *patio*, the Japanese *tsuboniwa* (moss, basalt, raked gravel) and Hawaiian running water (*loʻi*).
  - *Geometric frieze language:* Glazed terracotta and cast-aluminium friezes combine Japanese *seigaiha* (wave) and *asanoha* lattices with Hawaiian *kapa* geometry and Californio majolica. By 1931 the Polytechnic's earthquake code (`L1660 ("the Polytechnic's earthquake code")`), which the counties adopt, requires shear walls and stepped massing above six storeys, and it ties height to ground (see the skyline below) (PROPOSED).
- **The fight over the city (PROPOSED, D-30):** Pacific Modern wins, and others lose.
  - *Burnham's boulevards.* In 1905 Daniel Burnham's plan gives San Francisco Paris-style radial boulevards and a Beaux-Arts civic centre (*Hist.*). The city's Beaux-Arts architects and its improvement association want it built after the fire. The seismic commission's maps of Bay mud against bedrock (`A3.3`, first item) cut the boulevards where the ground will not hold them. In the civic-centre competition of 1912 a Pacific Modern entry beats the Beaux-Arts favourite (*Hist.*: in our timeline the Beaux-Arts design of Bakewell and Brown won). The losers go to Los Angeles and to the Union, and say for a generation that San Francisco chose engineers over architects.
  - *Ōmori against Imamura.* The seismologists have their own quarrel. In our timeline Imamura Akitsune warned in 1905 that a great earthquake would strike Tokyo, Ōmori dismissed the warning in public, and 1923 proved Imamura right; Ōmori died that November (*Hist.*). Here the quarrel is put to a challenge evening at Carquinez in 1906, in front of the Realm's engineers (`L405`). Ōmori is the respondent, and the evening is reported in both countries. The Polytechnic's code takes the larger figure. Tokyo does not, and after 1923 Japan's engineers are first in line to adopt Naitō's walls. Ōmori, who taught California so much, loses the one argument that mattered most to him. Imamura is a background figure, not registered.
  - *The carpenters.* Japanese joinery comes to San Francisco with Japanese carpenters, and the city's carpenters' union fights their licences until 1895, when the trades congress registers "tradesmen who have passed the Polytechnic's trade tests" (`L1080 ("the register of tradesmen who have passed the Polytechnic's trade tests")`) and the Japanese carpenters pass them (PROPOSED).
- **The ninety years of San Francisco: six layers, not one style (PROPOSED, D-32).** Each generation adds a layer, and by 1932 all of them stand together. Pacific Modern is one layer among them. It is the establishment by 1925, and it is not the newest.
  1. **The Gold Rush city (1846–1860s).** Canvas, imported prefabricated iron, and the fires of 1849–51 that turn it to brick; the Montgomery Block of 1853 (*Hist.*), which still stands in 1932. Around it are Californio adobe and, at Honolulu, coral-block churches. Nobody builds for earthquakes yet.
  2. **Ralston's city (1860s–1887).** The Bank of California and Ralston's Palace Hotel (*Hist.*, 1866 and 1875), built on bets that in this timeline pay (`CR-073`). The bay-windowed wooden rows of the hills. Hallidie's wire rope (`L330 ("Hallidie, who came as a boy in 1852")`) becomes the cable car. After the Hayward earthquake of 1868 (*Hist.*), the first rules on brick, and the first Japanese carpenters who know how a wooden frame should move.
  3. **The regency's revivals (1887–1906).** The Realm's heritage made into buildings:
     - the Mission revival in California's county halls (*Hist.* for the Mission Revival of the 1890s);
     - lava stone and coral at Honolulu under Victoria, beside Kalākaua's revival of the chants (`L848`);
     - the first tall steel frames on electric elevators;
     - Ernest Ransome's reinforced concrete, which in our timeline gave Golden Gate Park the Alvord Lake bridge of 1889 (*Hist.*);
     - the Ferry Building (*Hist.*, 1898), which survives everything after it.
  4. **The rebuilding and Pacific Modern (1906–1925)**, as above: the seismic commission's maps, Burnham's boulevards defeated, the civic centre of 1912, Maybeck's and Morgan's craft, Japanese joinery and Naitō's walls.
  5. **The Calculated (*Keisan-ha*, 1923–1932): the modern layer (name and origin APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 18:25, D-34; particulars PROPOSED).** It arrives when three things meet:
     - the Kantō earthquake, which proves Naitō's walls;
     - the Calculation Office's machines: Fuller's Carquinez machine from 1921, the stored-program machine from 1928 (`L107 ("the first stored-program machine runs in 1928")`). With them the Office can compute how a whole building moves in an earthquake, which no drafting room anywhere can do;
     - the Realm's aluminium and its cheap power.

     **The origin.**
     - *September 1923.* Tokyo burns, and Naitō's Industrial Bank stands, with the frame of his Kabuki-za, still being built. So does Frank Lloyd Wright's Imperial Hotel, and Wright says so in every paper.
     - *Spring 1924, a challenge evening at Carquinez* (`L405`). Naitō brings his photographs and his wall calculations, with a Pacific Modern master as respondent. The respondent's point is fair: Naitō sized his walls by hand and by judgement, so who can say why they held? Naitō answers that nobody can yet, and asks for machine time.
     - *The machine.* The Office already "sells tables and machine time to every navy and almanac in the world" (`L1655 ("sells tables and machine time to every navy and almanac in the world")`). Sophia Levy, its first order-writer (`CT-SOPHIA-LEVY`), turns Naitō's frames into orders. Within a year the Office can show by calculation why a building holds. That is the school's real foundation: architecture becomes something proved at a challenge evening.
     - *1925, the Gate competition.* The jury is Pacific Modern's establishment, and it chooses eaved stone towers on granite footings. The losing entry is Othmar Ammann's (`CT-OTHMAR-AMMANN`, 1879–1965). He is a Swiss engineer who came west in 1904 by attraction instead of to New York, where in our timeline he built the George Washington Bridge of 1931, the longest span in the world (*Hist.*). His entry has slender steel towers clad in aluminium and a deck stiffened by deflection theory, with Levy's calculations attached. The engineers publish their numbers. At a second challenge evening the winning towers fail under the Office's wind and earthquake loads, and the Development Fund, which is lending for the bridge, makes its loan conditional on the Office's figures; the bridge district awards Ammann the contract. It opens in 1929.
     - *The name.* A Pacific Modern master (Bernard Maybeck, in the background) says the Gate towers were "calculated, not designed". The young print the word on the cover of their journal, as Impressionists and Fauves once took their names from their critics (*Hist.*).
     - *The creed:* **a building states what it carries, and nothing else.** It is the Republic's rule against the false account (`L242`) applied to structure. It is also why the Calculated call Pacific Modern "costume with eaves".
     - *The circle (background, not registered):* Fuller and Levy at the Office (`CT-LEONARD-FULLER`, `CT-SOPHIA-LEVY`, notes only); Naitō (`CT-NAITO-TACHU`); Vladimir Shukhov, whose towers are licensed through the Realm's ties with federal Russia after 1921; Eduardo Torroja, the young Spaniard, on a Carquinez fellowship in 1925–27, whose first crews for the shells are the Spanish-speaking counties' engineers; Walther Bauersfeld's Zeiss dome; Leon Moisseiff's deflection theory, used without him, which the Union counts as a grievance; and Tokyo's Secession group of 1920 (Bunriha: Horiguchi Sutemi, Yamada Mamoru of the Central Telegraph Office of 1925), the school's Japanese wing, whom the Japanist critics of `A5.12` call traitors twice over (*Hist.* for all; to verify).

     Its buildings are unmistakably modern, and some of them amaze the world:
     - **The Gate span (1925–29, PROPOSED):** Ammann's suspension bridge across the Golden Gate, the longest span in the world when it opens in 1929. Its deck is stiffened by deflection theory worked on the Office's machines, and its towers are clad in anodized aluminium.
     - **The Twin Peaks tower (1930, PROPOSED):** a steel hyperboloid lattice that carries the press estate's television and the wireless chain above the fog (`L111 ("public television from 1930")`). It is built on the principle of Vladimir Shukhov's Russian towers of 1896 and 1922 (*Hist.*). From the city at night it looks like a lit net hung in the sky.
     - **The shells:** thin concrete vaults a few centimetres thick, computed and not guessed. They cover the electric trains' Transbay terminal and an air harbour's hangars. Freyssinet's and Zeiss-Dywidag's European shells of the 1920s are their teachers (*Hist.*).
     - **The planetarium in Golden Gate Park (late 1920s, PROPOSED):** under a geodesic steel dome of the kind Walther Bauersfeld built at Jena for the Zeiss projector in 1922–26 (*Hist.*), the first in the Pacific.
     - **The pendulum towers:** a handful of towers of thirty to forty storeys, and only on bedrock (Nob Hill, Rincon Hill, the foot of Telegraph Hill). Each has a steel core, Naitō's walls and a tuned pendulum hung at the top to damp the sway (*Hist.*: Frahm's patent of 1909 and Den Hartog's theory of 1928, to verify). Each design is proved at a challenge evening before the code admits it.
     - **Night:** buildings designed to be lit, on power that the Columbia sells cheaply after dark (`L1571 ("raise their steam in electric boilers on that secondary power")`). San Francisco is the first city whose skyline is composed for the night.
  6. **Thalassia (from 1928)** is not San Francisco. The Hawaiian capital follows the Thalassia Plan: tree belts, electric trams, no coal, low buildings of lava stone and timber in gardens (`L1697 ("With Victoria's backing he drafts the Thalassia Plan of 1928")`). The two capitals argue with each other in stone.
- **The skyline is a geological map (PROPOSED).** The code ties height to ground. The towers stand on the hills' rock; the old Bay fill and the made ground of the waterfront carry low, broad buildings on shear walls and shells. Anyone who knows the seismic commission's map of 1906 can read it in the skyline of 1932.
- **The last fight of the period (PROPOSED).** By 1925 Pacific Modern is the establishment. Its masters say machines make no architecture. The Calculated say Pacific Modern is costume with eaves. The young are Carquinez-trained, Pacifican and Japanese, with a few Austrians who came west by attraction (*Hist.*: Rudolph Schindler in Los Angeles from 1920 and Richard Neutra from 1925; background). Frank Lloyd Wright of the Union, whose Imperial Hotel in Tokyo survives 1923, quarrels in print with Naitō over who knew how to build for an earthquake (*Hist.*; Wright a background figure). In 1932 the civic towers are Pacific Modern, and the bridge, the tower and the shells belong to the Calculated. Neither school has won.

**A3.4. *Onsen* culture in the Rockies, Sierra, and American Southwest (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Pacifica spans North America's richest geothermal belt—Calistoga, Tassajara, Mammoth, and Lassen in California; Steamboat Springs in Washoe; **Ojo Caliente, Jemez Springs, and Pagosa Springs** in New Mexico and the Ute country (where Pueblo peoples already held centuries-old thermal bathing traditions); Glenwood Springs in the Ute homeland and Ouray in a mining district on Ute ground leased to the Realm (`L665`); Lava Hot Springs in Idaho; Lolo in Missoula County, and Chico in Three Forks County, west of the line of 1865 though east of the divide; and volcanic steam vents on Hawaiʻi Island.
- **Homeland compacts and *ryokan* lodges:** Rather than turning mineral springs into OTL's cramped Victorian sanitariums or seizing sacred springs, thermal valleys on Native lands (Pueblo, Ute, Shoshone, Paiute) are leased by compact with village royalties. Japanese bathmasters and stone-masons partner with Pacifica's **electrified mountain railways** (`L81`) to build timber-and-adobe *ryokan* lodges beside steaming granite and red-rock ***rotenburo* (open-air outdoor baths)** under falling Rocky Mountain snow, redwoods, or New Mexico stars.
- **Who loses (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The cure hotels.* The Victorian spa hotels that sold mineral water as a cure lose their trade once the Office of Public Measures prints the springs' analyses and the cure claims cannot stand beside them; the bathhouse sells only the bath (PROPOSED; how the Realm's label law treats health claims is `CR-089`'s, and nothing here rests on it).
  - *A refusal.* One Pueblo refuses to lease its sacred spring at any royalty, and the Realm's rule (no grant without a compact, `L739`) holds: the line runs from Santa Fe past the valley, and the valley stays closed (which Pueblo is OPEN).
- **Where the bath takes root, and where it does not (PROPOSED, D-33).** Five things decide it:
  - who owns the water: a nation under its compact, a county, or a private owner;
  - whether a bathing tradition is already there (the Shoshone's, Ute's, Tewa's or Tlingit's own uses of the springs; Spanish *baños*; the Finnish sauna; the Saints' Warm Springs; the Hawaiian sea);
  - faith and modesty;
  - whether Japanese families, railway crews or fishermen live nearby;
  - whether the electric railway reaches it.

  The memorable outcomes are those where the tradition already there wins its own terms:
  1. **Pagosa, the Ute bath.** The Ute keep the great spring in their homeland (`L665 ("The Utes keep Colorado west of the divide")`), and the Southern Ute council decides what is done with it (Ouray, the Uncompahgre leader who made the Ute treaty, grew up at Taos speaking Spanish, `L665`). The council hires Japanese bathmasters on contract and writes its own rules: women's hours, no photographs, and the sweat lodge apart and closed to outsiders. The baths' takings pay for the Ute schools. By the 1920s it is the Realm's most famous winter bath, and it belongs to nobody but the Ute.
  2. **Ojo Caliente, *baños* and the Tewa.** Spanish New Mexico already bathes. The Hispano bathhouse takes the Japanese rule of washing before entering, keeps Catholic separate days, and serves chile and coffee, not tea. The ruins of the old Tewa village above the springs stay closed under the compact. The result is called neither onsen nor *baño* by the people who use it.
  3. **The Saints' bath: Salt Lake's Warm Springs and the Wasatch.** The Saints bathed at the Warm Springs within a few years of 1847, where Shoshone had camped before them (*Hist.*). Deseret takes the bath readily, on its own terms: family tubs, separate days, no mixed bathing anywhere. Because the Word of Wisdom forbids hot drinks, no tea and no cup: the bath ends with cold barley water. The Swiss Saints' crater spring at Midway (*Hist.*, to verify) becomes Deseret's resort.
  4. **The Columbia bath, without a hot spring.** At Astoria and up the lower river, Finnish fishermen's saunas meet the Japanese cannery crews' tubs: steam, a hot soak, then a plunge into the cold river. The water is heated by the dams' cheap night power (`L1571`), so it is the bath that the Realm's electricity makes. It is its own thing within twenty years, and the Finns and the Japanese both claim it.
  5. **The family tub of the farm counties.** In the Hood River orchards and the Delta, Japanese farm families keep the family *furo* at home (*Hist.* for Hood River's family baths). It stays private and spreads only to neighbours who are invited. That is the most common bath in the Realm, and the least visible.
  6. **Tenakee, the northern school's bath.** The springs at Tenakee have a Tlingit name and long Tlingit use (*Hist.*). Under the Sitka compact (`L739`), the Tlingit, the cannery hands and the miners share one bathhouse by hours, kept by a committee. Sapporo's and Sitka's men soak there on their way between the two northern schools. It is the plainest bath in the Realm and the one Hokkaido visitors like best.
  7. **Fort Hall's springs, neutral ground.** In our timeline the Shoshone-Bannock lost Lava Hot Springs in a cession of 1902 (*Hist.*, to verify). Here the springs stay inside the Fort Hall homeland, and the nation opens them to settlers on its own terms. In the interior's quarrels (`CR-071`), the nation's bath is one of the few places settlers and Shoshone meet as nothing but bathers.
  8. **Where it does not take: Hawaii and Calistoga.**
     - *Hawaii* has no hot springs but the volcano's steam on Pele's land, and Hawaiians keep the vents of Kīlauea closed to bathing. The plantation camps' tubs and Honolulu's public bathhouses stay Japanese neighbourhood baths, and the Hawaiian bath is the sea. The onsen never becomes a resort culture in the islands.
     - *Calistoga* keeps its own Californian mud-and-cure tradition (*Hist.* for its mud baths). The Japanese bath is tried there and fails; the mud stays.
  9. **The reverse: Beppu.** In our timeline Aburaya Kumahachi (1863–1935) travelled for three years in North America, became a Christian, and in 1911 founded the Kamenoi inn at Beppu. He rebuilt it as a Western-style hotel in 1924 and in 1928 ran Japan's first sightseeing buses with women guides (*Hist.*). Here his three years are spent in the Realm's railway resorts. Beppu becomes the Japanese onsen town remade on a Pacifican model of rail, advertising and service, and Japan's purists say the bath has come home spoiled. Aburaya is a background figure, not registered.
- **Civic ritual and realistic friction:** Electric mountain trains run winter "snow-bath specials" out of San Francisco, Salt Lake, Santa Fe and Boise, and **Prince Chichibu** (`L1683`), whose outdoorsman's reputation is canon, is among the bathers; whether he or anyone is the trail's patron is left OPEN for the destination CR (see `## Hand-offs`). Senators, physicists, ranchers and naval officers use the same baths and the same cotton robes; the cup comes only in the evening and only after a deed, a climb or a day's survey, as `CR-076` requires. Where Deseret/Mormon and Methodist interior counties balk at communal nudity, Pacifica develops a characteristic split: strictly sex-separated baths everywhere, private family tubs (*kashikiri*) in conservative interior counties, and strict Japanese bathing etiquette (scrub thoroughly *before* entering the water) on the coast, in Hawaiʻi, and at railway resorts.

**A3.5. Music, cinema, and the "Pacific String" sound (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Building directly on `CR-079`'s Hawaiian steel guitar (`CT-JOSEPH-KEKUKU`), **George Beauchamp's** electric steel guitar (`CT-GEORGE-BEAUCHAMP`, `L111`), public radio (1912), electrical records, and Honolulu Japanese-language talkies (`L1653`):
- **The bent note (*Pacific String*; music corrected in revision 9):** the bridge between the traditions is the bent and sliding pitch, not a shared scale. Each instrument bends notes its own way:
- the *shakuhachi* player bends a note by shading the hole and tilting the head (*meri* and *kari*);
- the *koto* player raises a string's pitch by pressing it behind the bridge (*oshide*);
- the steel guitarist slides the bar along the string.

Hawaiian melody, Spanish guitar and the Western ballad do not share the Japanese scales. What they share is the voice that slides between notes. By the 1910s–20s Honolulu, Tokyo and Los Angeles studios pioneer an intimate balladry built on it, pairing amplified steel guitar with *koto*, Spanish guitar and *shakuhachi*. It is a transpacific counterpart to Japan's guitar-led *ryūkōka* (*Hist.* for Koga Masao's guitar songs of 1931, to verify).
- **Who resists (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The purists on both sides.* The *iemoto* houses of *koto* and *shakuhachi*, which license teachers and own the repertoire, forbid their licensed players to record with amplified instruments (*Hist.* for the *iemoto* system). The best young players leave the houses to record, and lose their licences.
  - *The conservatory.* San Francisco's conservatory refuses the steel guitar a chair until 1928.
- **The benshi's last stand (PROPOSED, D-30):** Honolulu's and San Francisco's Japanese film houses have *benshi* narrators, as Tokyo's do. When talking features come from Los Angeles in 1919–20 (`CR-079`), the narrators strike, as Tokyo's did in our timeline in 1932 (*Hist.*), and they lose. A few become radio's first voices; most do not.
- **Cinema and *ma*:** Some Honolulu and Los Angeles directors carry the *benshi*'s pauses and *ma* (deliberate interval) into the talking feature. The Los Angeles houses that sell to the Union call it slow, and the argument runs through the 1920s (PROPOSED).
- **Concert halls:** Some San Francisco and Tokyo composers write for *taiko* and *shakuhachi* beside Western strings; the conservatory's purists keep them off the main subscription series until the late 1920s (PROPOSED).

**A3.6. Games and physical culture: Go, Riichi Mahjong, *hanafuda*, baseball, regattas, and *jūdō* (APPROVED direction; specifics PROPOSED).**
1. **Go (*Igo*) at Carquinez and the Calculation Office:** Introduced by Japanese students at Carquinez from 1860, **Go** displaces chess as the prestige board game of Pacifica's engineers, naval staff, and the Calculation Office (`CT-CHARLES-PEIRCE`, `CT-JOHN-VON-NEUMANN`). Peirce takes it up because a won position in Go is a measured one, counted at the end, and he says so to the chess club, which never forgives him.
   - **The fight (PROPOSED, D-30):**
     - *Chess does not go quietly.* The Polytechnic's chess club keeps its room, its annual match against the Union's colleges and its argument that chess is the cleaner test of calculation, and it is right about the last. Go wins the naval staff and the Calculation Office; chess keeps the artillery school.
     - *The professional houses.* Japan's Go houses, the Hon'inbō first among them, grant ranks only to their own pupils (*Hist.* for the houses and for the Nihon Ki-in of 1924). For forty years Pacifica's best amateurs go to Tokyo to be beaten. The first Pacifican to take a professional rank is a Japanese Pacifican engineer, in the 1920s (PROPOSED; name OPEN), and the houses say for years that he was really Japanese.
2. **Riichi Mahjong (crystallized c. 1924–1930) and *Shōkin-jan* (Specie Mahjong):** Historically, Mahjong reached Japan in **1909** (*Hist.*), boomed in Tokyo in **1924** under Saburō Hirayama (*Hist.*), and was standardized under novelist Kikuchi Kan's **Japan Mahjong Federation in October 1929** (*Hist.*), whereas OTL America turned Joseph Babcock's simplified 1920 *"Mah-Jongg"* into a short-lived orientalist fad and missed the deep Japanese game. In Pacifica, because authentic Chinese Mahjong is already played in San Francisco, Honolulu, and Shanghai's Customs circles (`CR-081`), there is no dumbed-down Babcock craze. Instead, Chinese tile combinatorics, Japanese defensive reading (**neatly ordered *kawa* discard rows, *furiten* defense, strict *yaku* structure**), and Californian/Pacifican poker and actuarial stake-culture collide in the **1924–1930 Tokyo–Honolulu–San Francisco clubs**, Navy wardrooms, and Polytechnic common rooms—crystallizing the ***riichi* ready-hand wager** (turning a discard sideways, putting down a 1,000-point stick and locking one's hand in public) by about 1926 in the Tokyo, Honolulu and San Francisco clubs, roughly fifteen years ahead of OTL, so that *Shōkin-jan* can grow out of it after the panic of 1927 (*Hist.*: in OTL full *riichi*/*furiten*/*dora* coalesced post-WWII; here *dora* bonus indicators remain an emerging 1931–32 Osaka/Kansai parlor variant or post-1932). **John von Neumann** (`L107`), publishing his real 1928 game-theory paper on imperfect-information games, encounters a four-player imperfect-information game of defensive folding (*betaori*) and declared risk right inside Carquinez.
   - **The Pacifican high-stakes twist — *Shōkin-jan* (正金雀, "Specie Mahjong", c. 1927–1932; PROPOSED):** Born in San Francisco clearing-house clubs, Tokyo Nihonbashi back rooms, and Navy wardrooms right after the 1927 banking panic, *Shōkin-jan* is a playable, zero-prop variation on standard 136-tile Riichi built on Pacifica's gold-cover doctrine (`CR-057`) and on *On the Public Arts*'s rule that a false account is theft (`CR-049`, `L242`). Each player starts with **15,000 in Gold Sticks (*Shōkin*) and 15,000 in Paper Notes (*Tegata*)**, and Paper counts at settlement only as far as its holder's own Gold covers it; a player may print notes at the doctrine's own 50% line, and owes them back in Gold. A player may declare **Paper *Riichi*** even while *noten*, staking 1–3 notes (each a han to the declarer and, if the declarer deals in, to the winner), with two turns of grace to reach *tenpai* and one chance to **double the bill**. Opponents may buy a **Soft Assay (*Kantei*)** for 1,000 Gold: the declarer shows one tile of their choosing (the cover story), then the assayer names a suit or the honours and the declarer must show a tile of it or swear to hold none (the category knife). Or they may slam the felt and **Call Cover (*Shōkin Seikyū* / Bank Run)**, alone or with others queuing behind the first caller, each staking in full, to force a showdown, which the declarer may answer by **renewing** at double stakes and drawing one last tile off the wall. A bluff that survives to the end of the wall must still pass **the Clearing**, where any rival may present the note; a dead bluff behind someone else's win is caught by **the Audit**. A bank emptied by a run pays its queue in order and freezes the rest. A player out of Gold or below zero faces the **Discount Window**, where the rivals offer in turn to lend at 50% on a leash that takes the borrower's Gold, or all may **Refuse to Print (*Hakkō Kyohi*)**; while any rescue loan is unpaid, and through the South round, the table plays at **Emergency Rate**, and honba, heavy and paid in Gold, rise after every Call. Full playable rules are recorded in `## Appendix A — Rules of Shōkin-jan (Specie Mahjong)`.
3. ***Hanafuda* (Flower Cards):** Already beloved in Hawaiʻi as *Sakura*, *hanafuda* spreads across California, the Rockies, and Baja/Sonora, printed in regional Pacific editions adding redwood, cactus blossom, and hibiscus alongside pine, plum, and cherry. The Kyoto card-makers call the editions counterfeits and lose the Hawaiian market to Honolulu printers (PROPOSED).
4. **Transpacific university sport and martial arts:** In **April 1905**, mid-Russo-Japanese War (*Hist.*), Professor **Abe Isoo** (`CT-ABE-ISOO`, *Hist.* 1865–1949) leads **Waseda University's** baseball nine on a tour of California and Hawaiʻi—playing **Carquinez, Ralston College, and Honolulu** while Kiyoshi engages the Petropavlovsk squadron—launching an annual **Pacific Intercollegiate Series** (Waseda, Keiō, and Meiji rotating against Carquinez, Ralston and Honolulu; when Ralston College goes behind the wall in 1910 its public life is unchanged, team included, `L1288 ("Its public life is unchanged")`), alongside Carquinez–Tokyo Bay rowing regattas (where *the earned cup* travels both ways). **Kanō Jigorō's** (*Hist.* 1860–1938) **Kodokan *jūdō*** enters the physical curriculum of the **Pacific Companies** and county police (leverage and restraint in place of the club; the county police's boxing instructors fight it for a decade, PROPOSED), while **Hawaiian surfing** and Sierra skiing/mountaineering flow west to Shōnan/Kamakura and Hokkaido/the Japanese Alps.

**A3.7. Fashion and dress — reciprocal Pacific wardrobe (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Anchoring Japan's textile and silk role in the **Hayama annex** (`L1622`) in visible everyday style across the Realm:
- **Who loses (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The merchant tailors.* San Francisco's merchant tailors, who sew the frock coat, lose the young engineers to the *haori*. They fight it with a "suit of the Realm" in the 1900s and lose.
  - *Kyoto's weavers.* Nishijin's houses refuse to sell brocade by the bolt to San Francisco cutters who will cut it into evening wraps, and for a decade sell it only as finished kimono. The Japanist critics of `A5.12` quote them.
- **The Pacifican *haori* and indigo *noragi*:** Suited to San Francisco's foggy microclimate, a structured, straight-cut, collarless ***haori* outercoat** (in wool, raw silk, or heavy cotton) becomes the informal signature jacket of Polytechnic engineers, architects, faculty, and urban women. On Mare Island docks, redwood camps, and farms, Japanese ***aizome* indigo *noragi* and *sashiko*-quilted work-jackets** merge with California denim into a distinct Pacific indigo workwear tradition.
- **High-craft Honolulu silk shirts (1910s–1920s):** Adapting the real historical origin of the Aloha shirt (Japanese-Hawaiian tailors in Honolulu sewing collared shirts from Japanese *kimono* silk and *chirimen* crepe in the 1920s–30s, *Hist.*), high-craft **Honolulu *chirimen* silk shirts** in subtle *yūzen* wave and botanical prints emerge a decade early in Thalassia, Manila and Los Angeles; the Montgomery Street banks forbid them to their clerks until 1929 (PROPOSED).
- **Women's *hakama* mobility and San Francisco *shibui* couture:** Borrowing Meiji women students' pleated ***onna-bakama*** (*Hist.*), **tailored *hakama*-cut divided skirts and wide pleated trousers** become common campus, laboratory and fieldwork wear for Pacifican women. Running on Kyoto and Yokohama silk, San Francisco develops a couture of its own, which Paris ignores until the late 1920s, skipping the Victorian hourglass for kimono-draped sleeves, wrapped *obi*-inspired waistbands, **Nishijin-brocade and *yūzen*-silk evening *haori* wraps**, and restrained ***shibui* palettes** (indigo, slate, moss, persimmon, charcoal, pale gold).
- ***Yukata*, *samue*, and practical craft footwear:** Crisp cotton ***yukata*** (in *kasuri* indigo or Southwestern/Hawaiian geometric weaves) and loose ***samue*** replace the wool dressing-gown in many Pacifican homes and in the mountain *onsen* towns. Vulcanized rubber-soled split-toe ***jika-tabi*** work boots (historically invented in Japan c. 1922, *Hist.*) are adopted by San Francisco steel-frame riggers, redwood carpenters, and Columbia dam builders, alongside woven ***zōri*** sandals, oiled-paper ***wagasa*** umbrellas, and ***furoshiki*** wrapping cloths.

**A3.8. Everyday craft and domestic habits: Pan-Pacific *Mingei*, *genkan*, and *shin-hanga* (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
- **Pan-Pacific *Mingei* (1920s):** Japan's *mingei* folk-craft movement (coined by Yanagi Sōetsu in the 1920s, *Hist.*) links directly with **Pueblo pottery** (the San Ildefonso revival), California stoneware, and Hawaiian *koa* woodturning and *kapa*. Its case is functional, handmade everyday ware against gilded European luxury.
- **The quarrel over the hand (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The aluminium champion.* It sells the Realm's households pressed and anodized aluminium for every use, and the *mingei* men call its wares soulless. The champion answers that its saucepans are honest too, and cheaper. The households buy both.
  - *Who owns the craft.* Yanagi's admiration for Pueblo pottery meets the San Ildefonso potters' own decision to sign their pots and sell them to collectors at collectors' prices (*Hist.* for Maria Martinez's signed pots). The potters choose the market, and the *mingei* purists call it a fall. It is the potters' choice, and the Realm's rule on the nations' affairs keeps it theirs.
- **Domestic and civic habits:** Shoes-off ***genkan* entryways** become standard across Hawaiʻi, California redwood homes, and Rocky Mountain lodges (an immediate everyday marker separating Pacifican households from Eastern American ones). ***Shin-hanga*** woodblock aesthetics shape railway and national-park posters, while durable *washi* paper is used for official Realm charters and county maps. *(Railway lunch boxes and kitchen fusion are `CR-089`'s: the station box is its `A10` item 8.)*

### A4. How the marriage works in everyday life (diaspora, faith, firewalls, intimacy)

**A4.1. The "Letters Home" engine (PROPOSED).**
From the Wakamatsu colony (`L783 ("Wakamatsu colony")`), Hawaii's family convention of 1881 and the renewal of 1907 onward, Japanese immigration overtakes Chinese in the 1900s (`L41 ("Japanese immigration overtakes")`). Because migrants arrive into a Realm with no penal labour contracts, enforceable land titles, county-language grants, and open examinations, remittances, photographs, and Honolulu/San Francisco Japanese newspapers circulate back to Hiroshima, Yamaguchi, Kumamoto, Wakayama, and Kagoshima showing cousins as citizens, engineers, physicians and landowners. The letters do not tell everything: the emigration companies of Hiroshima and Yamaguchi still charge for the passage and the loan, the first years are debt, and the Sacramento board of 1906 is in the same newspapers (`A5.7`). Tokyo's Foreign Ministry limits passports to men it judges a credit to Japan (*Hist.* for the passport rules of our timeline), and the villages argue over who goes.

**A4.2. Religious reciprocity — Buddhist academies in the Pacifica League (APPROVED direction; institutional form PROPOSED).**
While Pacifica sends no missionaries to Japan (`A1.2`), Japanese **Jōdo Shinshū (Honganji)** temples, Zen centers, and Shinto shrines operate openly across Hawaiʻi and California under the Realm's religious floors. Under the **Pacifica League** rule (`L45`, where churches keep their schools and the state pays only for what it examines), **Bishop Yemyō Imamura** (`CT-YEMYO-IMAMURA`, *Hist.* 1867–1932, Bishop of Honolulu's Honpa Hongwanji Mission from 1900 to 1932) brings the **Hongwanji's bilingual academies in Hawaiʻi into the League's examination rolls**, and the Buddhist Mission in California, under its own head, follows on the exact same footing as Catholic and Methodist colleges—without surrendering Buddhist religious instruction. For Japanese families on both sides of the ocean, seeing a Buddhist academy's graduates enter Carquinez and the civil examinations proves that the Realm's floors hold for them. The Realm's own reason is the examination's: the League pays only for what it examines, and a bilingual academy that sends examined graduates enlarges the pool the Polytechnic draws from. Talent comes from every faith (`CR-034`), and a rule that kept Buddhist schools off the roll would cost the Realm engineers.

**A4.3. Sovereign firewalls protecting Japanese domestic life (canon synthesis).**
Ordinary Japanese accept permanent union in 1932 only because domestic boundaries remain unmistakable:
- The **Tennō never leaves Japan**; Hayama is Japan's court.
- **English has zero domestic official status in Japan** (`L1626`).
- Japan's **family registry (*koseki*), domestic courts, primary schools, Shinto rites, railways, posts, and home army** stay wholly Japanese.
- **No Pacifican sovereign enclaves or bases** exist on Japanese soil (Yokosuka and Hakodate are treaty rights, not ceded territory).

**A4.4. Mixed marriages (*kokusai kekkon*) — accepted in the islands, transgressive on the mainland (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Japan's 1873 *Daijōkan* Decree No. 103 (*Hist.*) legalized international marriage, while the 1898 Civil Code and *koseki* rules governed how a foreign spouse entered a Japanese house (a husband marrying in, *nyūfu*, most strictly). Ordinary mixed marriages flourish primarily in **Hawaiʻi** (where Hawaiian, Japanese, Chinese, Filipino, and Californio intermarriage has deep local roots) and California port cities, while remaining rare, socially fraught, and openly criticized in provincial mainland Japan. That unresolved friction is left deliberately intact: it gives real weight to Chiyo's delicate standing at both courts, and to why **Kahekili's betrothal to a collateral imperial princess** feels genuinely transgressive to Japanese conservatives (`L1780 ("Restoration officers strike")`).


**A4.5. Shinto: the firewall, and three crossings (PROPOSED, D-31).**
Shinto is the one part of Japanese life the partnership never touches, and that restraint is its chief role in the exchange. There are three crossings, each with its own friction.
- **The firewall.** Shinto rites stay wholly Japanese (`A4.3`). Pacifica proposes nothing about them and sends no priest, and the Treaty of Edo's no-missionaries article (`A1.2`) runs both ways in spirit. In our timeline the Meiji state classed shrine rites as a civic duty and not a religion, barred shrine priests from preaching in 1882 and set up a Shrine Bureau in 1900 (*Hist.*). How that "State Shinto" is built and taught belongs to the domestic-politics CR (`## Hand-offs`).
- **Crossing 1: two sacred genealogies.** The Emperor's line descends from Amaterasu. The Hawaiian aliʻi's lines descend from the gods in the genealogical chants that Kalākaua prints (`L848 ("prints the genealogical chants")`; *Hist.*: the *Kumulipo*, printed in 1889). In 1881 Kalākaua brings both claims into one room (`A3.1`), and Tokyo's court scholars read the chants as Hawaii's *Kojiki*. That recognition is what makes "two sister Pacific monarchies" more than a phrase: each court knows the other's form of sacred kingship. It is also why the Restoration purists find Kahekili's betrothal to an imperial princess intolerable (`A5.3`): it joins two divine lines. Japan's succession law excludes him (`L1258 ("Japan’s succession law excludes him")`), so the law keeps Amaterasu's line closed even where the families join.
- **Crossing 2: the shrines of the Japanese Pacificans, and the shrine question.** Shrines follow the emigrants to Hawaii and California (*Hist.*: Hilo and Honolulu had shrines by the 1900s; dates to verify). The Realm registers a shrine like any church under its floors (`L928 ("registered church and congregation")`). Tokyo's doctrine says shrine rites are not religion but civic duty. The Realm's courts rule on what is done, not on its name: worship before a kami is religion and voluntary, and a citizen of the Realm owes the Emperor no rite. Japan's consuls therefore cannot require the Emperor's portrait and the Rescript on Education in the Realm's schools, as they did in Japanese-language schools abroad in our timeline (*Hist.*, to verify). Many Japanese Pacificans keep the rites, and many do not. Tokyo's Home Ministry and the Restoration call the second group apostates (G29 in `A5`).
- **Crossing 3: places, cleansing and the dead.**
  - *Sacred places.* The Realm's compacts keep Native sacred places closed unless their people consent: the springs (`A3.4`), the four Diné mountains, Hawaii's protected grounds. Japanese visitors recognise in them the shrine forests of home.
  - *Cleansing.* The bath's rule of washing before entering (`A3.4`) sits close to *misogi*, the Shinto cleansing in water, and to Hawaiian cleansing in the sea. Nobody in the Realm makes a religion of it.
  - *The dead.* Japan enshrines Kiyoshi as a war god: "A son of Elias Thorne is now sacred in Japan" (`L1492 ("Japan enshrines Kiyoshi as a war god")`). Canon has Julian bring the sword home, and where it rests is OPEN (`L1492`). In Shinto a war god's sword belongs at his shrine, so the shrine's priests and Kashiwagi's faction want it, and its absence is a small grievance and a bond at once. This CR leaves the sword OPEN for the CR that decides it. In 1932 Kiyotada, a war god's son, takes the common Navy's oath at Thalassia (`A6.4`).
- **What the Realm takes from Shinto:** customs, not belief. Some non-Japanese families in Honolulu and San Francisco make the New Year shrine visit, and a torii stands in Golden Gate Park's Japanese garden (*Hist.* for the garden of 1894, to verify). The Realm's state has no religion and borrows none.

### A5. Japanese grievances: resolved, contained, unresolvable, latent (catalogue APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 16:01, D-24; dispositions PROPOSED)

**A5.1. Hokkaido and the Ainu: mutual institutional influence, 1870s–1932 (APPROVED direction; canon tension `T-AINU-1`; detail PROPOSED).**
- *Starting friction (`L739`, `APPROVED — CR-070`):* Early *Kaitakushi* settlement takes Ainu hunting and salmon grounds without asking, and Sitka's assayers quarrel with Sapporo over it in the 1870s.
- *The 1893–1899 turn:* When Pacifican-assisted cold-hardy paddy begins pushing across the Ishikari and Kamikawa plains from 1893 (`C-1893-HOKKAIDO-RICE`), Pacifica does not lecture Japan about an expansion it is helping to finance. Sitka's reason is practical: the Realm grants no land on a nation's country without its compact (`L739`), its northern fisheries science depends on salmon rivers that are managed and not stripped, and a Hokkaido that feeds itself is a Japan that does not need Manchuria (`A5.10`). Instead, Sitka's Northern Commission, Sapporo Agricultural College under **Satō Shōsuke** (`CT-SATO-SHOSUKE`), Hokkaido officials, and Ainu petitioners from Chikabumi (Asahikawa) and Piratori exchange concrete Alaskan and homeland legal instruments.
- *Why the Diet passes it (PROPOSED):* for Japanese reasons. The Home Ministry wants the fisheries revenue of managed rivers, which stripped rivers do not pay. Satō and Nitobe argue a colonial-policy case: villages with secure title are cheaper to govern and school than a dispossessed people. And the Realm's compact with Japan comes up for renewal in 1897; answering Sitka's reproach in law removes it from the table. The losers in Tokyo are the land companies' friends in the Diet and the Home Ministry's assimilationists, who wanted the Act our timeline got.
- *The 1899 Hokkaido Ainu Settlement & Fisheries Act (`C-1899-HOKKAIDO-AINU`, PROPOSED):* Rather than OTL's coercive *Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act* (which allotted rocky individual 5-*chō* plots forfeited if uncleared, *Hist.*, and came after the Kaitakushi's and the prefectures' bans on river salmon fishing and their measures against the Ainu language and *iomante*, *Hist.*), the Imperial Diet passes a reform crafted strictly within **unitary Japanese domestic law** (no separate foreign-treaty sovereignty, respecting *koseki*):
  1. **Inalienable collective *kotan* village trusts:** Registered village lands in Chikabumi, Piratori, and coastal districts cannot be alienated to speculators or confiscated for non-paddy use, averting OTL's 35-year Chikabumi dispossession scandal (*Hist.*).
  2. **Statutory salmon-river and coastal *kombu* shares:** Mirroring Alaska's Sitka rents and Kodiak conservation quotas (`CR-070`), Ainu villages hold protected river-salmon catch shares and coastal kelp rights. In 1932 their kombu trusts are among the "Hokkaido's kombu growers" who sign the marine-agronomy compact (`L1704`); the compact, its parties and its terms are unchanged (`CR-085`; `CR-089` `A2`).
  3. **Voluntary paddy and bilingual schools:** Households choosing Kamikawa paddy receive irrigated allotments and Sapporo training; village schools teach Japanese alongside Ainu oral literature rather than erasing it, enabling **Chiri Yukie** (*Hist.* 1903–1922, *Ainu Shinyōshū*) and **Iboshi Hokuto** (*Hist.* 1901–1929) to emerge as voices of a living bilingual Ainu literary revival with transpacific readership.
- *Residual limits (unresolved grit):* The 1870s–92 dispossession of the lower Ishikari is not undone. The paddy settlement of 1893–99, before the Act, takes Ainu ground outside the village lands, and after 1899 the hunting and gathering country outside the registered trusts stays open to settlers: the Act protects villages, not the country. Wajin settlers vastly outnumber Ainu across Hokkaido, and Tokyo *kokutai* hardliners resent "Sitka-style special village status" written into Japanese law.

**A5.2. Returnees, *kokusui hozon*, and Sōseki's *gaihatsuteki kaika* (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Even with a respectful partner, Pacifica's speed and scale produce genuine cultural vertigo:
- **1880s–90s *kokusui hozon* ("preservation of national essence"):** Thinkers like **Miyake Setsurei** (*Hist.* 1860–1945) and the *Seikyōsha* (*Nihonjin*, 1888, *Hist.*) resist uncritical assimilation. Because Pacifica never had consular courts and ended its mixed panels in 1870 without demanding interior landownership (`A1.3`), and although the Atlantic powers still make mixed residence their price in 1894–99, *kokusui hozon* stays primarily a vigorous cultural-preservation movement (craft, architecture, folklore) rather than pure anti-foreign hysteria—yet it constantly polices the border against over-eager imitation.
- **The returnee (*yōkō-gaeri*) and *moga* critique (1900s–1920s):** **Natsume Sōseki** (*Hist.* 1867–1916) gives enduring intellectual voice to the anxiety of *gaihatsuteki kaika* ("externally driven civilization", *Hist.*) — warning that chasing Carquinez's examination metrics and English engineering jargon risks hollowing out Japanese inner life. In the 1920s the Tokyo magazines' critics turn the same charge on the modern girl (*moga*) and Honolulu's film fashions (*Hist.* for the *moga* debate). Home-trained bureaucrats and provincial elders resent fluent **"Carquinez returnees"** who leap ahead in ministries, shipyards, and trading houses.
- *Why it cannot be fully resolved:* Living beside a richer, faster partner inherently pulls urban youth outward. The tension stays alive on the page as the permanent cultural cost of modernization.

**A5.3. Mixed marriages and dynastic anxiety (APPROVED direction).**
As framed in `A4.4`, ordinary intermarriage remains socially contested in mainland Japan, and **Kahekili's betrothal** moves that unresolved anxiety to the foot of the Chrysanthemum Throne—handing Restoration purists a grievance no legal text can erase (`L1780`).

**A5.4. Decentralized modernity and *nōhon-shugi*: why small villages are cushioned (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
In OTL, coal-and-steam centralization, colonial Korean rice dumping, and raw-silk collapse devastated rural Japan in the 1920s–30s, turning **Gondō Seikyō's** (`CT-GONDO-SEIKYO`, *Hist.* 1868–1937) agrarian fundamentalism (*nōhon-shugi*, *Hist.*) into fuel for mass rural despair and right-wing terrorism. In Pacifica's timeline, three converging mechanisms sustain a **decentralized rural-industrial path**:
1. **Early rural hydro-electrification (1890s–1910s):** Japan's mountainous rivers paired with **Pacifica's standardized AC hydro equipment** (Tesla/Ryan standard, `CR-052`, `CR-054`) bring regional hydro grids to Japanese prefectures a decade early. Utilities sell surplus **daytime power** cheaply to townships (*Hist.* for Japan's daytime rural-power model).
2. ***Sangyō Kumiai* cooperatives + fractional-horsepower electric workshops:** Following Japan's **1900 Industrial Cooperative Law** (*Hist.*), village *Sangyō Kumiai* pool credit to sign bulk electricity contracts, run electric centrifugal irrigation pumps and community rice mills, and put **small electric motors** into family workshops (weaving, silk-reeling, noodles, ceramics, woodworking, small machine parts). Light industry stays anchored in market towns rather than forcing every rural son and daughter into urban slums.
3. **Protected rice + domestic nitrogen + wireless weather (`CR-085`, `CR-078`):** With Korea independent (no colonial rice dumping breaking farm prices, `L1704`), affordable ammonium sulfate from hydro plants, and Okada's wireless typhoon/frost warnings, rural tenant debt remains far shallower than in OTL.
- *What remains unresolved:* The **1930–32 Atlantic Union depression** still collapses US raw-silk buying (`CR-085`), and Reserve Bank gold parity squeezes Nagano, Gunma, and Tōhoku silk villages hard enough to give Gondō's critique and the 15 May 1932 officers genuine moral purchase—**yet** rural stability explains why the countryside does not rise in support of the coup and why Takahashi's July 1932 yen reset eases the silk villages' debts while bringing no buyers back (`L1798`).

**A5.5. Sovereign dignity and the "Thalassia bakufu" charge (canon synthesis — unresolvable in principle).**
Huludao (1909–14), the Pacifican-led Common Foreign Ministry ending Japan's consular courts in China on 1 January 1930 (`L1752`), Hayama's industrial limits, and giving up the 1937 exit right (`CR-085`) constitute a grievance that **cannot be resolved in principle** for nationalists who equate dignity with unilateral freedom of action (Araki, Mori Kaku, Hiranuma). **A5.6. Satsuma, 1877, and the Pan-Asian right (G3; PROPOSED from canon).**

**A5.7. Sacramento, 1906: the grievance Japan settles itself (G9, G10; canon).**

**A5.8. The navy's prizes and its language (G5, G12, G13; canon and PROPOSED).**

Every Japanese officer meets that rule daily. Then, in 1915, Victoria's audiences with Yamagata and Ōkuma's cabinet produce "revised demands in April without the fifth group" (`L1338 ("revised demands in April without the fifth group")`). In 1919 the navy loses Tsingtao (`L1403`). What contains these grievances is what the navy gets instead:
- the Marshalls (`A2.6`);
- the combined fleets at parity with Britain (`L1403`, PROPOSED in canon);
- a Japanese admiral commanding the coalition in the Baltic (`L1478 ("under Kuroda Kiyoshi as the coalition's senior admiral")`);
- and, in 1932, the Navy seat by treaty law (`L1619`).

Kiyotada is the grievance's living answer and its proof: a bilingual officer who commands in English and is Japan's Navy minister (`A6`).

**A5.9. The Kronstadt dead, and the Altai that nobody in Japan knows (G14, G15; canon).**
Commander Kashiwagi, who served under Kiyoshi at Kronstadt, "came home believing that Japanese sailors died there for a Pacifican's hostages" (`L1709`). Kolchak, offered the Baltic under Kiyoshi, refuses "to serve under a Japanese admiral" (`L1450 ("refuses to serve under a Japanese admiral")`), a slight that is Russian and not Pacifican but is remembered beside it. Japan answers the Kronstadt grievance by enshrining Kiyoshi as a war god: it honours the act its sailors died in. The fleet faction does not accept the answer. Its men help plan the squadron's part in the coup of 15 May 1932, and Kashiwagi is court-martialled (`L1780`). **The boundary:** in the Altai in 1921 "Vance uses gas knowing the prisoners are present" (`L1507 ("Vance uses gas knowing the prisoners are present")`), and Japanese line-guard soldiers die with their captors. "Julian does not share the Altai secret with Hirohito" (`L1511 ("Julian does not share the Altai secret with Hirohito")`). It is the worst thing Pacifica does to Japanese soldiers, and the one grievance Japan never has. Nothing in this CR brings it to Japanese knowledge. The reader knows; the officers who strike in May do not.

**A5.10. The army and the land: 1894, Korea, Manchuria (G6, G8, G21, G22; canon).**
- **1894.** "Japan's army resents the shortened victory" (`L1084`). It gets its land war in 1898–99, and with it the Ussuri, Vladivostok and Sakhalin (`L1092`). The army learns that Pacifica stops Japan's wars early and pays it later, which is a reason for both gratitude and resentment.
- **Korea.** Korea stays independent and guaranteed, its guard drilled by a Pacific Company (`L1005`). The army's continental school never forgives it. The irony the army cannot see is that Korea's independence is why Japan "runs no drive to draw a colony's rice into its home market" (`L1704`): the denied prize saves Japan's farmers.
- **Manchuria.** "Japan's army officers count the district" of Huludao among their grievances (`L1711 ("Japan's army officers count the district")`). The land school's charge in 1931 is "that Pacifican contracts take Manchuria's harvest while its acres stay closed to Japan" (`L1766 ("Pacifican contracts take Manchuria's harvest while its acres stay closed to Japan")`). The answer is agricultural, not diplomatic. Japan's rice keeps pace with its people, so there is "no hunger argument for Manchuria" (`L1788`), and the army's argument is "closed by Japan's own ratified word" (`L1790`). Araki loses it.
- **China's courts.** In 1930 the army minister protests that a common ministry "run by Pacificans" gave away Japanese subjects' courts in China (`L1752`). The officers' grievance moves from Huludao's customs to Thalassia.

**A5.11. The atom and the keys (G23; canon).**
Three things, each answered differently:
- **The fuel's price.** The Restoration charges that the Tokyo station's fuel "is priced at will" (`L1662 ("the fuel is priced at will")`). It is answered by the published coal-parity tariff: **resolved**.
- **The secret.** "In Tokyo only the Prince Regent, Hirohito, is told" (`L1660 ("In Tokyo only the Prince Regent, Hirohito, is told")`), and Nagaoka "kept Pacifica's secret from his own government" (`L1636 ("kept Pacifica's secret from his own government")`), arguing to the end that silence "hides only Pacifica's head start" (`L1539 ("silence hides only Pacifica's head start")`). For most Japanese this is **latent**. For the few who learn of it after 1927 it is a grievance that a Japanese scientist's word to Pacifica outranked his duty to Japan.
- **The keys.** The devices are held under two Pacifican keys, and "the common Navy minister has no key" (`L1880 ("the common Navy minister has no key")`). Japan is inside the atomic order as a party to the Concord, and the Emperor countersigns the pledge: **contained**. In 1932 Kiyotada, as Navy minister, knows he holds no key and accepts it (`A6.4`). The purists call that the bakufu's sharpest form.

**A5.12. The borrowed culture (G26, second half; PROPOSED).**
The reciprocity of `A3` has a Japanese objector, not only Japanese teachers. From the 1900s the Japanist critics object that San Francisco makes Japanese things into fashion: the *haori* as an engineer's jacket, *Pacific Modern*'s eaves, the Honolulu silk shirt, Go in Carquinez's lounges. They say Japan's forms lose their meaning when worn by people who do not keep the life they came from. Our timeline's model is Okakura Kakuzō, who wrote *The Book of Tea* (1906) in English at Boston to explain Japan against the West's Japonisme (*Hist.*). Here the same argument is made against the Realm, in Japanese, by critics of the *kokusui hozon* line (`A5.2`). Okakura stays a background figure, not registered. The grievance is unresolvable and cultural. It gives the Realm's borrowing a price: some Japanese read *Pacific Modern* as tribute, and others read it as theft.

**Boundaries for the prose.** The Altai never reaches Japanese knowledge (`A5.9`), and neither does the British gold on Kronstadt (`CR-046`: never told to Hirohito, never learned by Kiyotada). The atomic secret is spoken of only as canon has it (`A5.11`). No grievance is settled on the page by the narrator's verdict.

### A6. Identity profile: Kuroda Kiyotada (`CT-KURODA-SON`), Common Navy Minister

*Resolves `CR-085`'s open item (`L2047 ("the given name and the descent of Kiyoshi's son")`, `L1794`): the given name, descent, career, and cultural meaning of Kiyoshi's son in the treaty-law Navy seat from 1 August 1932.*

**A6.1. Identity, birth, and genealogy (name APPROVED; biographical dates PROPOSED).**
- **Name:** **Kuroda Kiyotada (黒田清忠)** (*"clear loyalty"*; continuing the house's *Kiyo-* character: Kiyotaka → Kiyoshi → Kiyotada; Kiyotaka's own son Kiyonaka, heir of the main house, is of Kiyoshi's generation, `L989`). Registry ID: existing `CT-KURODA-SON`.
- **Birth & descent:** Born **c. 1888** (two years younger than his half-uncle Julian, born 1886) to **Count Kuroda Kiyoshi** (`CT-KIYOSHI`, Elias Thorne × Uehara Hisa) and **Hyaku**, Kiyotaka's adopted daughter, whom Kiyoshi married on entering the house in 1886 (`L989 ("Kiyoshi's bride is Kiyotaka's adopted daughter Hyaku")`). Three-quarters Japanese, one-quarter Elias Thorne; **2nd Count Kuroda** (branch house) upon his father's death in May 1920. Nephew by marriage to **Chiyo** (`CT-CHIYO`), the wife of his half-uncle Julian, and half-first cousin to Silas's younger daughter (married to Tanabe Makoto in 1909, `CR-022`).

**A6.2. Formation and the 1907 lesson (PROPOSED).**
Raised bilingual between Tokyo/Kagoshima and Pearl Harbor, Kiyotada graduates from the **Imperial Japanese Naval Academy at Etajima** and completes naval engineering and gunnery courses at **Carquinez**. At **nineteen (1907)** he watches his father petition for Pacifica's crown (`L1248`) and get turned back by *both* capitals (Saionji refusing a Japanese count's elevation; Hawaiʻi and Thalassia standing for Julian). He learns what canon says he learned: **a fleet cannot make a sovereign** (`L1780 ("a fleet cannot make a sovereign")`). His own gloss, which he repeats to Carquinez officers, is that dual loyalty survives only when the boundary between the two crowns is guarded.

**A6.3. Kronstadt (1920) and the Tokyo intelligence crucible (canon synthesis + PROPOSED detail).**
At **thirty-two (May 1920)** Kiyotada inherits his father's branch countship and the crushing weight of his father's enshrinement as a Japanese *gunshin* (war god) after Kronstadt. Posted to the **Navy General Staff intelligence division in Tokyo** (`CR-045`), he becomes **Maria Grishina-Almazova's reader** from 1920 (`L65`). When Maria marries **Commander Kashiwagi**—a destroyer officer who served *under Kiyoshi at Kronstadt* and came home convinced Japanese sailors died there for a Pacifican's hostages—Kiyotada quietly reads reports on his own father's embittered subordinates and the British naval attaché, forwarding them to Thalassia. From about 1928 he holds a sea command in the western squadron (PROPOSED); Maria's reports go on reaching him by name, through the Navy General Staff's courier, and so does her warning of 1932. Per `CR-046`, **he never learns that British gold paid the Chekists on Kronstadt in his father's last weeks.**

**A6.4. May to August 1932, and what his descent means in the Navy seat (APPROVED seat & loyalty; cultural meaning PROPOSED).**
At **forty-four**, commanding Japanese units of the western squadron on **15 May 1932**, Kiyotada receives Maria's warning of which ships and which day (`CR-045`) and holds the Japanese fleet units loyal in his father's name (`L1780`). Named **Common Navy Minister** by Julian in July with Saitō's countersignature, to take the seat on **1 August 1932** (`L1792 ("names the common Navy minister's successor")`), and sworn in at Thalassia with the common Navy's oath, his first official act is **carrying out the order that dissolves his own father's reserved western squadron** into one consolidated Concord command, an order his predecessor laid before the Council at Hayama in June (`L1786 ("lays the order before the Council himself")`). What it costs him: the reports he forwarded in 1931–32 named officers who had served under his father, and as minister he signs the postings that end their careers (PROPOSED).
- **The key he does not hold:** as Navy minister he commands the fleet that would carry a device only on an order under both keys, and he holds neither (`A5.11`). He knows it, and he accepts it, for the reason of 1907: a fleet cannot make a sovereign (PROPOSED).
- **Cultural meaning:** To **Pacifican skeptics** (Tate's bloc), Elias Thorne's grandson is the officer who never pressed a claim, held the fleet in May, and dismantled the reserved squadron himself; the few who know the Office's British file add that he forwarded the second folder (`L1709 ("the second folder of Pacifica's British file")`). To **Japanese purists and Restorationists**, he is an infuriating paradox—untouchable as a Kuroda peer and the son of the Kronstadt *gunshin*, yet sitting in Japan's treaty-guaranteed Navy chair as living proof of the quiet fusion of the two houses they fear most.

## Appendix A — Rules of *Shōkin-jan* (正金雀 — Specie Mahjong)

*The complete rules as agreed with the author on 25–27 November 2026 (the working notes of rule revisions 12–14 in the Pacifica project, `claude/CR-088-shokin-jan-rev14.md`, the last review's edits of 26 November, 00:14, the Category Knife, the Maturity Draw and the contested Window of 27 November, 06:30, the first fresh-context review's round of 07:29 (D-37), the teller queue, the moratorium and the second review's round of 09:19 (D-38), and the Appendix review's round of 10:57 (D-39)), on the base of standard Japanese riichi mahjong as the Japanese Mahjong Wiki (riichi.wiki) sets it out. Every Shōkin-jan rule is stated as a change to that base. The table's settings are the author's choices in A.9; settings marked Proposed there, and every number, are to be checked at the table. Heavy Honba and Emergency Rate are story assumptions for the clubs of 1927–32, not historical fact (A.10).*

### A.1 The base game and the stakes (standard riichi, changed only where this appendix says so)
- **Table and tiles:** four players, 136 tiles. A *hanchan* of East and South rounds; the dealer keeps the seat on a win, and on tenpai at an exhaustive draw.
- **Winning:** four groups and a pair (or the standard exceptions), with at least one *yaku*. Hands are scored in *han* and *fu* on the standard table; mangan is 8,000 (12,000 for the dealer).
- **Furiten:** a player whose discards include a tile that would complete the hand cannot win by ron, and can still win by tsumo. A player who passes a winning tile cannot win by ron until their own next discard; a riichi player who passes one is furiten for the rest of the hand (for Paper Riichi's grace turns, see A.5).
- **Riichi:** the hand must be closed and tenpai, the player must hold at least 1,000 points (in Shōkin-jan, 1,000 Gold), and at least four tiles must remain in the live wall. The declarer turns a discard sideways and puts down a 1,000-point stick, which the next winner of a hand collects. The hand is then locked; no kan may change the wait. Riichi is 1 han and a yaku, and ippatsu applies. In Shōkin-jan this is Gold Riichi (A.4).
- **Exhaustive draw:** tenpai players collect a total of 3,000 from noten players:
  - with one tenpai, each noten player pays 1,000 to the tenpai player;
  - with two tenpai, each noten player pays 1,500;
  - with three tenpai, the noten player pays 1,000 to each.

  Riichi sticks and other deposits stay on the table, and the honba count rises by one. Paper Riichi declarers are settled first at the Clearing (A.5).
- **Heavy Honba (S24):** each honba adds **900** to a winning hand, in place of standard riichi's 300:
  - on a ron, the discarder pays 900 per honba;
  - on a tsumo, each player pays 300 per honba;
  - honba are always paid **Gold first**, even on a Paper Riichi win;
  - honba apply **only to winning hands**, never to a Call of Cover, a Renewal, a Confession, the Audit, a presentment or the Assay;
  - the honba count rises as standard, and after every Call (A.7).
- **Emergency Rate (*Hijō Ritsu*, S25):** a hand is played at Emergency Rate if it **starts in the South round**, or **starts while any rescue loan (A.8) is unpaid**. At Emergency Rate:
  - every winning hand gains **+1 han**. It is not a yaku: it counts only for a hand that already has one, like dora;
  - a tenpai hand valued at a Call (A.7) gains the same +1 han;
  - the noten penalty rises by **2,000, or 3,000 for the dealer** (A.5);
  - flat payments do not change: Confession, the Fraud Penalty, the presenter's payment, the Assay fee and its bad-note payment, and honba.
- **Chombo** (a false win, a false riichi, or a false Open Specie, A.4): a mangan-sized penalty paid like a mangan tsumo. A non-dealer pays 2,000 to each non-dealer and 4,000 to the dealer; a dealer pays 4,000 to each. **A chombo that aborts the hand** (a false win) is followed by no rotation, no other exchanges and no honba: every Gold Riichi stick, Paper Riichi note and pending Assay fee laid in that hand returns to its owner, deposits from earlier hands stay on the table, and an Open Specie is still checked. **A chombo discovered at a reveal** (a false riichi at the draw, a failed Open Specie, a broken "none") does not abort the hand's settlement: the offender counts as noten for the draw, the Audit or the Call, and pays the chombo on top; where another player won, the win stands (A.4, A.6). A noten Paper Riichi is never chombo: it is settled by A.5 and A.7.
- **End:** the game ends when a player goes below zero (*tobi*) and no rival rescues them (A.8). A player at exactly zero continues. Tobi counts the Gold and Paper on the table, less any recorded shortfall and, until a rescue, any frozen claims (A.8); loans and printing debts do not count.
- **No abortive draws (S31):** the hand is never abandoned on four winds, nine terminals, four kans or four riichi; a triple ron is played as the house plays a double ron.
- **Period settings:** no *dora*, and so no *ura dora* or *kan dora*; no red fives (S9).

### A.2 The two currencies
- **Stacks:** each player starts with **15,000 in Gold Sticks (*Shōkin*, brass) and 15,000 in Paper Notes (*Tegata*, thin slips)** (S11). The house may use any two colours of sticks. Everyone starts exactly at full cover: a point of Gold lost uncovers a point of Paper, and a point of Paper won beyond one's Gold is uncovered.
- **Cover:** a player's notes are covered up to the Gold that player holds. Covered Paper counts at par at settlement; uncovered Paper counts for nothing (S1). This is full cover, stricter than the Reserve Bank's 50% (`CR-057`).
- **Hot paper:** uncovered Paper may still be paid out wherever Paper may be paid (A.3). Whether it is worth anything depends on the receiver's Gold.
- **No borrowing from the bank (S2),** with the one exception of Unbacked Issue. Paper otherwise enters play only from the starting stacks and from payments.
- **Unbacked Issue (optional, S12):**
  - when declaring a Paper Riichi or Doubling the Bill (A.5), a player may take 1–3 fresh notes from the reserve instead of from their own stack;
  - **printed notes need only the 50% test:** after printing, the player's Gold must be at least **half of everything that player has printed** this hanchan (the doctrine's 50% line). The full-cover test in A.5 applies only to notes from the player's own stack. A player with no Gold can never print, and cannot declare any riichi (A.8);
  - the scorekeeper writes the amount against the player's name on the score sheet;
  - **printing debt is settled only at the end:** it does not count towards a player's total for tobi during play (A.8);
  - at settlement the printer repays the recorded sum to the reserve in Gold, before anything else (A.8). Any shortfall comes off the printer's score;
  - a borrower on the Leash may print (S27; A.8);
  - in every other respect, printed notes are ordinary Paper.

### A.3 What is paid in what (S5)
- **A win is paid in the currency of its declaration:**
  - wins with no riichi, Gold Riichi wins and Open Specie wins are paid **Gold first**;
  - Paper Riichi wins are paid **Paper first**: credit is paid in credit. On a tsumo, each of the three payers pays their own share Paper first;
  - **a Paper declarer's deal-in is paid wholly Gold first, whatever the winner declared** (A.5): leverage overrides Paper first;
  - **honba are paid Gold first on every win** (A.1).
- **"First"** means the payer must use that currency until it is exhausted and may then pay in the other. A payer whose Gold runs out on a Gold-first payment pays the rest at two notes for one Gold, and a payer who runs out of both is covered by the reserve (A.8).
- **Noten payments** at an exhaustive draw: the payer chooses.
- **Gold first, always:** chombo; the noten penalty (A.5); the Fraud Penalty (A.5); Confession (A.5); the presenter's payment (A.5); the Assay fee and its bad-note payment (A.6); every payment at a Call, including a drop at a Renewal (A.7); the whole payment for a Paper declarer's deal-in (A.5); a recorded shortfall's repayment (A.8); honba (A.1); rescue loans and their repayment (A.8).
- **Order of payment:** when one event makes one player owe several payments, they are made in this order: the hand's payment or the showdown's (at a Call, to the callers in queue order, A.7); then penalties, in the order of the list above; and among payees of the same kind, in turn order from the payer. The order decides who is paid in Gold and who in Paper when the payer runs short.
- **Deposits** are collected by the next winner in the currency in which they were laid.

### A.4 Gold Riichi
- Standard riichi (A.1), with a **1,000 Gold** stick. 1 han, ippatsu as standard.
- **Open Specie (*Kōkai*), optional at declaration (S13):**
  - **with the declaration,** before the riichi discard can be claimed by ron, the declarer turns **two tiles of the hand, of the declarer's own choice**, face up, and they stay face up;
  - **at least one must be part of the wait:** a tile that, together with the winning tile, forms the completed group or the pair, under any valid reading of the tenpai hand. On a single-tile wait (*tanki*), the tile waiting for its pair counts: on a wait for East to pair a lone East, the East in hand is part of the wait;
  - **+1 han** (2 in all); **no ippatsu**;
  - **no kan may use an exposed tile** (S23). Other closed kans follow the standard rule: none that changes the wait.
- **Checking:** at the end of every hand, however it ends, the declarer lays the hand face up. If neither exposed tile is part of the wait, it is chombo:
  - if the declarer won, the win is void;
  - if another player won, the win stands and the declarer also pays the chombo.

### A.5 Paper Riichi (*Tegata Riichi*)
- **Declaration:**
  - the hand must be closed;
  - it **need not be tenpai**;
  - **cover:** the declarer's Gold must be at least the notes staked from their own stack; notes printed under Unbacked Issue must meet the 50% test in A.2 instead;
  - at least four tiles must remain in the live wall.

  The declarer turns a discard sideways and lays **1, 2 or 3 Paper Notes of 1,000 each**.
- **Value:** **1 han per note** (1–3 han), in place of riichi's 1 han. **Paper Riichi counts as the hand's yaku, like riichi.** Never ippatsu (S4).
- **Grace (S3):** for the declarer's **next two own turns** the hand is not locked. The declarer may exchange a drawn tile for a tile in the hand (*tedashi*) to reach tenpai or improve the wait. After the second turn the hand locks as an ordinary riichi. Furiten during grace:
  - a tile in the declarer's own discards, before or after the declaration, that would complete the hand as it now stands makes it furiten, as standard;
  - **during grace, passing a winning tile makes the declarer furiten only until their own next discard,** as for a hand without riichi;
  - **riichi furiten begins when the hand locks:** from then on, a winning tile passed makes the declarer furiten for the rest of the hand.
- **Leverage both ways:** if a Paper Riichi declarer deals into another player's ron, **the winner adds the declarer's notes as han** to the winning hand, and the declarer pays the whole payment Gold first, even when the winner holds a Paper Riichi. **Leverage han are not a yaku:** they count only for a hand that already has one. A note is a bill, and a bill presented against its issuer is presented at its face. If two players win on the same discard (where the house allows a double ron), each adds the notes.
- **No calls (S32):** from the declaration to the end of the hand, in grace and after the lock, a Paper declarer may not call pon, chi or any kan, closed kans included. The standard riichi rule's closed kan after the lock does not apply to Paper Riichi.
- **The declaring discard:** if it is claimed by ron, the declaration fails as a riichi does: the notes are not laid (nor printed), no leverage applies, and the win is scored as on an ordinary discard.
- **Doubling the Bill:**
  - once per hand, on the first or second grace turn, when discarding, the declarer may lay **one more note**: 1 → 2 or 2 → 3, never 1 → 3;
  - the cover test applies again after the raise;
  - the raise does not extend the grace turns;
  - the extra note counts in everything: han, leverage and penalties.
- **The noten penalty in force (S19)** rises with the notes and with Emergency Rate (A.1). It applies wherever a Paper declarer is found noten by a Call (A.7) or by a presentment at the Clearing, and it sets the tenpai minimum and the drop at a Call (A.7).

  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 1 | 8,000 | 12,000 | 10,000 | 15,000 |
  | 2 | 10,000 | 15,000 | 12,000 | 18,000 |
  | 3 | 12,000 | 18,000 | 14,000 | 21,000 |

- **The Audit, when another player wins** (and for the other declarers at a Call, A.7):
  - **every** Paper declarer lays the hand face up, and each is judged separately;
  - **each** declarer **whose hand has locked** and who is noten (karaten counts as noten) pays a **4,000 Gold Fraud Penalty to the winner** (S15). With a double ron, it goes to the first winner in turn order from the discarder;
  - the notes go to the winner as deposits, as usual;
  - a declarer still in grace loses only the notes: being noten while grace is open is not a fraud, so there is no Fraud Penalty and no bad-note payment (A.6);
  - furiten tenpai counts as tenpai here.
- **The Clearing, at an exhaustive draw:**
  1. Each Paper declarer, in turn order from the dealer, says "tenpai" or "noten", **hand face down**.
  2. **Confession (S21):** a declarer who says "noten" pays **half a mangan**, whatever the number of notes: 4,000 paid like a tsumo (1,000 to each non-dealer, 2,000 to the dealer), or 6,000 if the declarer is dealer (2,000 to each). The notes are forfeit to the table as deposits. **Confession does not abort the draw:** the declarer counts as noten for the standard noten payments and for the dealer's seat.
  3. **Presentment (S14):** against a declarer who says "tenpai", each opponent who holds Gold of at least 2,000 per note staked, in turn order, starting after the declarer, may **present the note**, once. Only the first presenter does. A Paper declarer and a player who has just confessed may not present. With several Paper declarers, they are taken one at a time in turn order from the dealer. A confessor's hand is not shown.
     - **If noten** (karaten counts as noten): the declarer pays the noten penalty in force **in full to the presenter**, who takes the notes.
     - **If tenpai** (furiten counts as tenpai): the presenter pays the declarer **2,000 Gold per note**.

     **Presentment does not abort the draw:** a declarer shown tenpai counts as tenpai for the standard noten payments and the dealer's seat; a declarer found noten counts as noten for the draw.
  4. **Unpresented:** the declarer counts as tenpai for noten payments and for the dealer's seat, and the hand goes back into the wall face down, unseen.
  5. **The notes at the Clearing (S36):** whatever is found, the notes of a Paper declarer who confesses, is shown tenpai or goes unpresented stay on the table as deposits, like a riichi stick. Only a presenter who catches a noten declarer takes the notes.

### A.6 The Soft Assay (*Kantei*): the Category Knife (S28)
- **Who and when:** once in all per Paper Riichi (the first opponent to ask), any opponent who holds at least 1,000 Gold may, on that opponent's own turn before drawing, lay a **1,000 Gold** fee beside the declarer's notes and call "*Kantei*".
- **The cover story:** the declarer turns **one tile of their own choice** face up.
- **The category knife:** the assayer then names **one of the four tile groups**: Characters, Circles, Bamboos, or Honours (winds and dragons).
  - **If the declarer holds a hidden tile of that group,** the declarer turns one hidden tile of their choice from that group face up.
  - The cover-story tile may not belong to the named group: if it does, the assayer names another.
  - **If not, the declarer says "none"** and turns no second tile. From then on the declarer may hold no hidden tile of that group: any tile of it drawn during the grace turns, or on a Maturity Draw (A.7), must be discarded. A declarer found at any reveal, a win included, holding a hidden tile of a group they denied commits chombo (A.1): if the declarer won, the win is void; if another player won, the win stands and the declarer also pays the chombo.
  - Every tile shown stays face up and is true at the moment it is shown; during the grace turns the declarer may later discard it.
- **The discard tell:** if, during the grace turns or on a Maturity Draw (A.7), the declarer discards either face-up tile, the fee goes back to the assayer at once. The declarer cannot claim it, whatever happens after.
- **The fee, at the end of the hand** (unless the discard tell has already returned it):
  - **to the declarer** if the declarer wins the hand; is found tenpai at a Call against them (in the sense of Hollow Specie, A.7), or completes the hand on a Maturity Draw; is conceded at a Renewal by every caller (A.7); or is shown tenpai or goes unpresented at the Clearing;
  - **back to the assayer, and nothing more,** when the check is aborted: another player wins, or a Call is made against another Paper declarer, and at the Audit the declarer is tenpai or still in grace;
  - **back to the assayer, plus 1,000 Gold per note from the declarer (a bad note),** when the declarer is found noten: by a Call against them, in grace or after the lock (Hollow Specie included); by a confession or a presentment at the Clearing; or at an Audit after the hand has locked. The same applies, besides the chombo, when the declarer is found holding a tile of a group they denied.
- **Limit:** on any one turn a player may Assay or Call Cover, not both.

### A.7 Calling Cover (*Shōkin Seikyū*, the Bank Run)
- **Timing:** once per Paper Riichi, any opponent **who holds Gold at least equal to the noten penalty in force for the notes staked, and does not have an active Paper Riichi of their own,** may call, on that opponent's own turn before drawing, but **only after the declarer has completed at least one full turn (a draw and a discard) after declaring**:
  - during the grace turns, this leaves one window, between the first and second grace turns, in which each eligible opponent gets exactly one chance, in turn order;
  - after the hand locks, the window stays open for the rest of the hand.
- **The call:** slam the felt and call "**Shōkin**", **naming one Paper declarer** ("Shōkin on East!"). The hand ends at once; the declarer does not yet show the hand.
- **The run: the teller's queue (optional, S16):** in turn order from the caller, each other eligible opponent (the same Gold test) says "join" or "pass", once. The callers stand in a **queue** in that order, the first caller at its head. **Each caller stakes the full amount independently**, as at a teller's window: one caller puts at risk one stake, three callers three. A borrower may not join against their own lender (A.8).
- **Renewal and the Maturity Draw (optional, S17):** once the queue is formed (one caller or several), the declarer may **renew** once per hand instead of showing the hand. **Renewal doubles only the showdown between the named declarer and the callers:** the noten penalty and the tenpai payment, never a Fraud Penalty or any other payment. If any caller takes, it also buys **one Maturity Draw** (below). Each caller, in queue order, then either:
  - **drops:** pays the declarer the **full noten penalty in force** for the notes staked (A.5), undoubled, in Gold, and leaves the queue. The payment stands whatever the showdown shows; or
  - **takes:** stays in the queue, now staking the full amount doubled. A caller may take only if their paying capacity (Gold plus half their Paper, the two-for-one rate) is at least the doubled noten penalty in force; otherwise they must drop.

  If every caller drops, **the hand ends unshown** and the declarer takes back the notes.
  - **The Maturity Draw:** when at least one caller takes, the declarer may, before showing, draw **one last tile from the live wall**. A declarer already tenpai may decline and show at once. A Call is always made before a draw, so at least one tile remains.
    - **If the tile completes a winning hand,** the declarer lays all fourteen tiles face up and wins the Call as tenpai, even if furiten, since furiten never bars a self-drawn tile. The hand is **valued as a ron on the completed hand**, with no tsumo han and no tsumo fu (notes and Emergency Rate included; minimum the noten penalty in force), doubled, in Gold.
    - **Otherwise,** a hand that has **locked must discard the drawn tile**; a hand **still in grace may keep it and discard another**, and so may reach tenpai. No one may claim the discard, and discarding a face-up Assay tile triggers the discard tell (A.6). The declarer then lays the thirteen tiles face up, to be judged under Hollow Specie.
- **Showing:** the declarer lays the hand face up.
- **Hollow Specie:** for a Call only (except a hand completed on a Maturity Draw), the declarer counts as **noten** if the hand is noten, **karaten** (every copy of every winning tile is visible to the declarer: in their own hand, in any discard pond, in any called group, or face up in another player's hand under Open Specie or an Assay) or **furiten** of any kind. The hand may be solvent, but if it cannot win by ron now it is illiquid.
- **If tenpai (S6):** **each caller still in the queue pays the declarer the full amount**: the hand's value as a ron on its **highest-valued wait, notes included** (and Emergency Rate's +1 han, if in force), or on the completed hand after a Maturity Draw, **with a minimum equal to the noten penalty in force for the notes staked**, doubled if renewed, in Gold. No honba are added. A caller who runs dry is covered by the reserve, and the shortfall is written against the caller (A.8). The declarer takes back the notes.
- **If noten:** the declarer owes **each caller still in the queue the full noten penalty in force** (A.5), doubled if renewed, and pays them **in queue order from its own vault**: Gold first, then Paper at two for one. **The reserve does not cover a noten bank's payments to its queue, nor the bad-note payment to its assayer**, which is paid from the vault after the queue (the 1,000 Gold Assay fee on the felt returns as normal). When the vault is empty, every payee not yet paid in full holds a **frozen claim** for the rest, written on the score sheet in queue order (the moratorium, A.8). A caller who runs dry paying a tenpai bank is still covered by the reserve. If the bank is on a Leash, Gold it receives during the run, from drops or any other payment of the Call, enters the vault first to pay the run; the Leash takes only what remains afterwards.
- **The spoils:** the named declarer's notes, if the declarer is found noten, go to the first caller still in the queue. Any Fraud Penalties that fall to the callers (below) are paid one each to callers in queue order, starting again at the head if there are more penalties than callers.
- **Other Paper declarers (S22):** when a Call ends the hand, every other Paper declarer lays the hand face up and is judged as at an Audit (A.5), with one difference: **their notes stay on the table as deposits** for the next winning hand, whatever is found, like any Gold Riichi stick. A locked noten declarer pays the **4,000 Gold Fraud Penalty** to whoever won the Call: the named declarer, if found tenpai or conceded by every caller; otherwise the callers still in the queue, as spoils. A declarer still in grace pays nothing.
- **Afterwards:** however a Call ends, including when every caller drops after a Renewal, the remaining deposits stay on the table, there is no rotation, and the honba count rises by one, once.

### A.8 The Discount Window, the Leash, the Poison Pill, and settlement
- **Out of Gold (S7):**
  - once a player's Gold is exhausted, any Gold-first obligation or remainder of one is paid in Paper at **two notes for one Gold**, in steps of 200 Paper for each 100 Gold. If a single odd 100 Paper remains and the debt is not yet cleared, surrendering it clears 100 Gold, so every shortfall stays a multiple of 100;
  - this applies whether the player starts the payment with no Gold or runs out partway through it. A player with 1,000 Gold who owes 8,000 pays the 1,000, then the remaining 7,000 at two for one;
  - **the payee keeps all Paper paid at this rate;**
  - a player with no Gold cannot declare any riichi.
- **Shortfall (S30):** when a payer's Gold and Paper are both exhausted, **the reserve pays the payee the rest in full** (except a noten bank's payments on a run, A.7), in the currency the payment was being made in: Paper for the Paper-first part of a Paper Riichi win, Gold for everything else. The scorekeeper writes the shortfall at face value against the payer. No winner loses what was earned because a rival went broke, and a tsumo or a confession needs no sharing out. A player at 0 Gold and 2,000 Paper who deals into a 12,000 Paper Riichi pays the 2,000 Paper; the reserve credits the winner the other 10,000 in Paper, and the player carries a 10,000 shortfall.
- **Totals:** a player's total for tobi and for the Window is their Gold plus their Paper at par, **less any recorded shortfall** and, until a rescue loan is accepted, **any frozen claims** against them; a player who carries either is below zero. Loans, whether owed or owed to them, and printing debts count only at settlement.
- **The moratorium (S33):** a frozen claim is what a bank emptied by a run (A.7) still owes a caller or an assayer, written in queue order, at no interest.
  - Claims created by a run count against the bank's total for the Window that run opens, even if an earlier rescue loan is still unpaid: an earlier rescue cannot shield the bank. Once a rescue loan is accepted at that Window, they count only at settlement, like a loan. A frozen claim held by a player counts for that player only at settlement, like a loan owed to them.
  - A rescue loan does not pay it. Rescue money comes first: while a rescue loan is unpaid, the Leash takes the bank's Gold income. Once the loan is cleared, **half of each Gold receipt, rounded down to 100, goes to the bank's frozen claims in queue order, and the bank keeps the rest** (S33).
  - At settlement, frozen claims come straight after loans (Step 2), in queue order, in Gold only; what is still owed is the claimants' loss.
  - If no one rescues the bank, the game ends and the claims against it lapse.
- **The Discount Window (S29):** before the next hand starts, the Window opens for each player who is out of Gold or below zero in total. If more than one player is eligible, they are taken in turn order from the dealer of the next hand.
  - **Offers in turn order:** starting with the player after the borrower, each rival speaks once: they pass, or state aloud a sum in Gold at or above the floor, at the fixed rate. No rival may offer more Gold than they hold.
  - **The choice:** when every rival has spoken, the borrower accepts one offer or none. A borrower out of Gold but not below zero who takes no loan plays on out of Gold; a borrower below zero who takes no loan, because no one offered or because they declined every offer, ends the game (Refusal to Print, below).
  - While any rescue loan is unpaid, each new hand is played at Emergency Rate (A.1).
- **The floor (S20):** the loan first repays the reserve the borrower's recorded shortfall, in Gold (never a frozen claim), then must leave the borrower with **at least 5,000 Gold**. A borrower at 0 Gold and 4,000 Paper with no shortfall needs at least 5,000. A borrower with nothing on the table and a 2,000 shortfall needs at least 7,000. A bank emptied by a run, with 38,500 in frozen claims and no shortfall, needs 5,000.
- **Interest (S18):** fixed at **50%**, charged once, on the principal.
- **The Leash:**
  - until principal and interest are repaid, **all Gold the borrower receives from other players, from the reserve or from other players' deposits** goes to the lender first, oldest loan first, and only then to any frozen claims. The borrower's own returned stick or Assay fee is not income, and Paper income is not taken. A claimant on the Leash has repayments of their claim taken by their lender like any other Gold;
  - the borrower may not Call Cover, join a run, or present at the Clearing against the lender;
  - the borrower may use Unbacked Issue (S27). Printing debt is repaid before loans at settlement, so a debtor who prints can burn the lender's collateral.
- **Repeat rescues:** a borrower may be rescued again, from any rival including the same lender, on the same floor. The loans stack.
- **No other deals:** the only binding agreement at the table is the loan, stated aloud at the Window. No other deals or promises are recognised. Every player plays for themselves.
- **Refusal to Print (*Hakkō Kyohi*, S8):** if a player goes below zero in total and takes no loan before the next hand, whether every rival passed or the player declined every offer, the rivals have refused to print. The game ends at once (*tobi*), the shortfall is settled in Step 1, and the frozen claims against the refused player lapse.
- **Settlement, in this order:**
  1. **Printing debts** (Unbacked Issue) **and recorded shortfalls** are repaid to the reserve in Gold. Whatever cannot be repaid comes off the player's score.
  2. **Loans** are repaid **in Gold only**, oldest loan first, and then **frozen claims**, in queue order (unless they lapsed). No Paper is seized (S35). Debtors are settled one at a time, in seat order from the hanchan's first dealer; any Gold a debtor who has defaulted receives from a later debtor passes at once to their own unpaid creditors, before Step 3. Anything still owed is the creditor's loss (**the Poison Pill**).
  3. **Cover and ranking:** each player's Paper counts only up to that player's Gold. Each player's score, less anything unpaid from Step 1, gives the ranking. Ties are broken by seat order from the hanchan's first dealer.
  4. **Deposits (S26):** the remaining deposits go to the player ranked first, who adds them to their stack and re-checks Cover, without changing the ranking.
  5. **Uma (S10):** +15,000 / +5,000 / −5,000 / −15,000.

### A.10 Balance (a reasoned estimate, to be checked at the table)
- **Leverage keeps Gold Riichi alive.** Without leverage on a deal-in, a genuinely tenpai player would always declare Paper Riichi with three notes, since 3 han beats Gold Riichi's 1–2, and Gold Riichi would die out. With leverage, each note is a han for the declarer's opponents too. A locked declarer deals in often, and every deal-in costs the hand's value plus the notes in Gold. Three notes are therefore a bet on winning *soon*; one note, or Gold Riichi with Open Specie, is the prudent choice in a long hand or against a dealer's attack. Leverage han are not a yaku, so only a hand that could already win can collect them.
- **The currency keeps Gold valuable.** Paper Riichi wins pay mostly in Paper, and Paper beyond a player's Gold is worth nothing at settlement. A player who wins only on paper ends with notes the player cannot cover. The leader late in South is usually the player with the most *Gold*.
- **Two acts.** With no dora and the standard 1 han, East plays tight and positional: Assays, Gold cushions, cautious Paper. South flips to panic. Emergency Rate stops first place coasting to South 4 by folding, and gives trailing players real haneman and baiman threats. Heavy Honba adds flat Gold after every stalled bank run, so cheap, fast hands become worth fighting for. A rescue loan in East brings the second act forward and keeps it burning until the loan is repaid. That gives the debtor leverage, and gives first place a reason to want the debt cleared.
- **Three notes in South.** At Emergency Rate, a three-note Paper Riichi starts at 4 han, close to mangan on almost any hand. Dealing into a plain 1-han tanyao costs 5 han in Gold through leverage. This is intended: it makes three notes frightening in South and pushes players back to one or two notes, or to Gold Riichi with Open Specie. The raised noten penalty keeps Calls as balanced as in East, so bluffs grow costlier along with everything else and do not become safer.
- **Hot paper.** After a big Paper Riichi win, the baron holds uncovered Paper. A Call on the baron's own Paper Riichi is dangerous for the caller: if the hand is tenpai, the caller pays in Gold, the one thing the baron lacks, so the table calls the baron only on a strong read. But the baron can deal into Paper Riichi hands, and pay noten payments, in Paper that is worth nothing to them. Bad paper passes from player to player, and the receiver's cover decides whether it is worth anything. Honba, paid Gold first, give the baron a reason to push into a hand with honba stacked on it.
- **Distressed paper.** Taking a broke rival's Paper at two for one rewards a Gold-rich winner who has spare cover, and floods a Paper-rich winner with hot paper.
- **Tsumo against ron.** A Paper Riichi tsumo is spread over three payers and is nearly always paid wholly in Paper. A large ron off a player short of Paper spills into Gold. A Paper-rich baron would therefore like to hunt a ron off one rival. But a locked hand that passes up a win must discard the winning tile and becomes furiten for the whole wait. So the hunt is possible only before riichi or during the grace turns, and it is expensive.
- **Where a bluff can pay.** There are four cases: (1) unpresented at the Clearing; (2) every caller drops after a Renewal, and the bluffer collects the conceded stake without showing; (3) the semi-bluff, reaching tenpai during the grace turns; (4) a renewed Call taken, and the Maturity Draw completes the hand or, for a hand still in grace, brings it to tenpai. Everywhere else a bluff only loses.
- **Confess or bluff.** At the Clearing, a noten declarer can confess for half a mangan spread over the table (4,000, or 6,000 for the dealer), plus the ordinary noten payments, or claim tenpai and risk the full noten penalty paid to one presenter. The choice is live on every number of notes. For a non-dealer, confession costs the same 4,000 as the Audit's Fraud Penalty, before the noten payments; a dealer pays more to confess.
- **Renewal.** The tenpai minimum equals the noten penalty in force on every number of notes, so the stake is the same either way. A drop always concedes the full undoubled stake, and a rational caller should take whenever they put the declarer's chance of being noten above roughly a quarter. Tenpai declarers renew to double their payout, and bluffers renew to buy concessions, so the Renewal itself proves nothing. A dropped bluff wins without showing, as a fold does in poker.
- **The Maturity Draw.** Without it, a bluffer caught by a Call has no reason to renew: a taker doubles the loss. With it, renewing pays when the last draw's chance exceeds about a quarter. In the grace window, when Calls on a bluff usually come, 85 to 95 tiles are unseen from the declarer's seat, so a quarter needs about 22 live tiles; late in the wall, about 14 among fifty-odd. A locked noten hand has no chance at all, since it must complete on that tile, so renewing a bluff is a grace-window play: a disciplined declarer one tile from tenpai, with a wide wait, may renew even when caught; a declarer two tiles away still burns. Callers who take must still sweat one tile. Because a completed hand is valued as a ron, the draw gives no tsumo bonus the rest of the Call lacks.
- **Solvency and liquidity.** A furiten tenpai hand survives the Clearing and the Audit but loses a Call. A declarer who slides into riichi furiten after the lock must keep a straight face for the rest of the wall; during grace, a passed tile costs only one go-around. A renewed Call taken gives a furiten hand one last chance: the Maturity Draw is self-drawn, which furiten never bars. Refinancing a bill that has fallen due buys time at twice the cost of default.
- **Reading the raise.** A raise on the first grace turn comes before the Call window opens. A raise on the second comes after the declarer has survived it, and reads like "I just reached tenpai".
- **The discard tell is also a priced fake tell.** A tenpai declarer can discard a knifed tile they don't need, at a cost of at most 1,000, to look like a hand being rebuilt and invite a Call.
- **The Category Knife.** The assayer's skill is in reading the discards: a declarer who has thrown honours invites an "Honours" knife, and bluffers must clean their hands of obvious dead tiles before declaring. Its real teeth is "none": it freezes a whole group for the rest of the hand, and it is checked whenever the hand is shown; an unpresented hand at the Clearing goes back unseen, so a "none" is a risk only to a declarer who will be shown. Its weakness is deliberate: when the declarer holds the named group, they still choose which tile to show, so against a hand spread over all four groups the knife is a second cover story. The Open Specie carries one guaranteed truth chosen by the declarer; the Category Knife carries one truth about a group chosen by the assayer.
- **Loans.** Debts are settled in Gold only, so Paper is no collateral: a Paper-rich borrower is no safer a loan than a Paper-poor one, and every unpaid loan is the Poison Pill. A Paper shortfall repaid in Gold at par is the price of having been carried by the reserve. The Leash takes only Gold, so it pushes the debtor towards Paper Riichi, and a debtor who prints can burn the lender's collateral (S27). Fourth place loses the same uma whether or not they go bust, so their spite costs them nothing, and the lender knows it.
- **Contested rescues.** At a fixed rate, offers still differ in principal: a later speaker can offer 9,000 against an earlier 5,000, a cushion of Gold against the next Gold-first payment that keeps the borrower off the two-for-one rate. The borrower chooses whose Leash to wear: a rival they never meant to Call, leaving them free to hunt the reckless one, or a rival they mean to load with the Poison Pill. Because a borrower below zero may decline every offer and end the game, the player who most needs the game to go on, usually third place, may have to offer above the floor.
- **The teller's queue.** Each caller stakes in full, so the crowd changes the bank's danger: a noten bank renewed and taken by three callers on three notes owes 72,000, or 108,000 as dealer, against 15,000 Gold at the start. But the queue is paid from the vault in order, so being first matters and joining late is a bet on how much is left. A rich bank invites a crowd; a poor bank, or a baron holding hot paper with little Gold, is worth running only for the first caller. A run on a tenpai bank is a massacre: three takers on a renewed run owe at least 60,000 on two notes and 72,000 on three, more than the 45,000 Gold they can hold between them. That is why taking needs paying capacity (S34): at starting stacks, 15,000 Gold and 15,000 Paper are 22,500, enough to take a renewed one- or two-note run and lose it without the reserve, but not a three-note run.
- **The moratorium.** Frozen claims cannot be turned into a loan: rescue money is senior and the claims earn nothing, so no caller can lend to a bank to be repaid their own claim at 50%. An unpaid caller's reason to rescue is plain: if nobody lends 5,000, their claim dies with the game. It is the panic of 1927 at the table, and Japan's three-week moratorium of that spring (*Hist.*). Once the rescue is repaid, the bank keeps half of what it wins, so it still plays to win and does not become a kingmaker with nothing to lose.
- **Pacing.** The declaration needs four tiles in the wall; no Call comes before the declarer's first full turn, and no player may Assay and Call on one turn. Together these give the declarer at least one draw and the table at least one round of reading.
- **What only play can settle:**
  - the presenter's payment and the interest and floor of the loans;
  - whether half a mangan makes confession too cheap;
  - whether 900 honba stack too fast after repeated Calls (the house may drop to 600);
  - whether players Call three-note declarations in South at all, or fold unless a queue forms behind them;
  - whether a dealer caught noten, who keeps the seat with a honba added, is punished enough;
  - whether a renewed three-note noten penalty at Emergency Rate (28,000, or 42,000 for the dealer) is too lethal.
  - whether the Maturity Draw makes Calls too dangerous against a wide wait one tile from tenpai, so that callers wait for the lock rather than ambush in the grace window;
  - whether offers on principal alone give the Window enough friction, or the rate should be bid;
  - whether a deep shortfall makes the floor larger than any rival's Gold, so that a Refusal to Print becomes automatic;
  - how often a renewed run on a tenpai bank leaves several players below zero at once;
  - whether a one-note Paper Riichi still crowds out Gold Riichi for a tenpai hand now that its note stays on the table at the Clearing (S36): it keeps the grace turns and the Clearing's 2,000 per note, and Gold Riichi keeps ippatsu, payment in Gold and freedom from leverage and from Calls.

  The house is invited to adjust them, as the clubs of 1928 surely did.
- **History.** The parlour stakes raisers sometimes claimed for the 1920s (*wareme*, *sen-go* honba, Japanese sets keeping only the four Seasons) could not be confirmed.
