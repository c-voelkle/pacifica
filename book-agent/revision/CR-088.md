## A. The change

### A1. The founding trust: Perry, Shimoda, Edo and equal law (1853–1871)

*Closes Unresolved #14 (`L2002 ("Japanese alternate treaties")`) and anchors why Pacifica is never filed under the unequal-treaty powers in Japanese memory.*

**A1.1. Perry's reshaped 1853–54 mission (APPROVED direction; operational detail PROPOSED).**
Canon leaves Perry's altered mission OPEN and rejects a coaling embargo as its explanation (`L300 ("Matthew Perry")`). In this timeline Commodore Matthew Perry **does** reach Uraga and Edo Bay in 1853 and 1854, sailing via the Atlantic, Cape of Good Hope, Macao, and Naha (*Hist.* for the Atlantic/Indian Ocean route). His route and his lack of a Pacific base are what they were in our timeline; the real Perry had no Californian harbour behind him either. What changes is what waits for him:
- Without California (no San Francisco, no Mare Island, and a sharper North–South sectional struggle in Washington after 1850), Washington has no Pacific coast of its own to argue from, and nothing behind the East India Squadron but the long Atlantic road.
- Crucially, **Edo is not caught blind, and this is the difference that matters.** California's deniable Satsuma/Ryukyu channel—opened through Nakahama Manjirō (1851), Hamada Hikozō, and Shimazu Nariakira—has already delivered charts, intelligence on Western navies, and California's modest, non-missionary technical offer before Perry's black ships anchor.
- **Abe Masahiro** (`CT-ABE`) uses California deliberately as a diplomatic counterweight. Rather than facing a monolithic Western gunboat demand, the Bakufu grants Perry limited shipwreck and provisioning terms while quietly authorizing Satsuma-brokered technical exchanges with California in 1854, maturing into the **1855 Shimoda agreement** (`C-1855-SHIMODA`). In Japanese political memory, the opening of the country is tied to an immediate distinction: **the Atlantic powers arrived with threats; California arrived first with charts, castaways, and no priests.**

**A1.2. The 1858 Treaty of Edo (`C-1858-EDO`; clauses PROPOSED).**
Concluded in 1858 (`L306 ("Treaty of Edo")`) during the Ansei crisis after Abe's and Nariakira's deaths, the treaty is built against the model Townsend Harris imposes for the United States the same summer (*Hist.* for Harris's 1858 treaty). Elias's reason is capability, not fairness (D-29, PROPOSED). He does not hold that nations are owed equal terms. He holds that Japan can be made strong, that a strong Japan is worth more to the Republic than a dependent one, and that every clause should make Japan stronger in ways that bind it to California. Each article below therefore has two halves: what Japan gains, and what the Republic gains by giving it.
- **What California gets** is in the treaty too: coal, silk, copper and tools on stated terms (`L302 ("Coal, provisions, silk, copper, tools")`); Japanese shipwrights and naval students in California's yards; and a partner across the ocean that is not Britain's.
1. **The bullion clause:** Elias warns shogunal and Satsuma officials of the gap between Japan's domestic gold–silver ratio, about 1:5, and the world's, about 1:15 (*Hist.* for Japan's pre-1859 disparity; canon, `L304 ("he warns his partners and offers financial expertise")`). California's coin is exchanged by assayed weight and fineness.
   - *What Japan gains:* its gold stock, which would otherwise drain abroad.
   - *What the Republic gains:* the gap pays whoever holds silver, and in 1858 that means the Mexican-dollar houses of Hong Kong and Shanghai, which are British and American. California holds gold, so the warning costs it little and denies its rivals a windfall. The assay itself is a product: a Japan that has learned to trust Californian weights brings its old coinage to Ralston's bank to be refined for the new yen in 1872 (`L755 ("his bank contracts to refine Japan's old coinage for the new yen")`).
   - Japan still loses gold to the European traders, and so learns the difference by measurement, not by assurance. The rule behind the clause is the Republic's own: a falsified account "robs everyone who must build on it" (`L242 ("falsified account robs everyone who must build on it")`), and a false weight in a treaty is the same.
2. **The no-missionaries article:** California renounces any state-backed or treaty-protected mission on Japanese soil. It is the Republic's general rule, kept with China too (`L762 ("policy of no missionaries")`).
   - *What Japan gains:* relief from its deepest ideological fear since the Shimabara rising of 1637–38.
   - *What the Republic gains:* it gives up a lever it never wanted, and the duty that comes with it. A protected mission is a pretext. Honolulu learned that in 1839, when a French frigate took a toleration edict and a $20,000 bond in the missions' name (`A1.2` item 3). A murdered missionary is a casus belli that his protector must answer or lose face. The Republic will owe Japan no such answer, and Japan learns that California will never come with priests and then with ships. The clause costs the Republic nothing and is worth more to Edo than any other.
3. **Mixed panels and a fixed sunset (APPROVED, D-18, D-19; details PROPOSED):** Instead of consular courts and a permanent 5 per cent tariff cap, the treaty sets a **fixed date, twelve years on, in 1870**. Until then a case involving a Californian at Nagasaki or Kanagawa is heard by a **panel of one Californian and one Japanese judge under a Japanese president**, with reasons written in both languages; outside those ports Japanese law applies. On the date the panels end and the tariff is Japan's own. Japan undertakes to publish commercial and maritime codes, but the end does not wait for them (in our timeline the Criminal Code came in 1880, the Commercial Code in 1890 and the Civil Code in 1896–98, *Hist.*). Pacifica never has a consular court in Japan, and this is meant to be noticed.
   - **Why California can do what the Atlantic powers will not (PROPOSED).** Four reasons, none of them kindness:
     - *Hawaii's memory.* The Confederation's Hawaiian half has lived under the system from inside. In 1839 the French frigate *L'Artémise* extracted the Edict of Toleration and a $20,000 bond from Kamehameha III; in 1843 a British captain seized the islands (`L360 ("the British seizure of 1843")`); and the treaty with France of 1846 still has every Frenchman tried by a jury of natives or of foreigners proposed by the French consul (*Hist.*). Hawaii's negotiators will not have the Confederation do to Japan what Honolulu still endures.
     - *No gunboats to spare.* A consular court rests on a squadron that can enforce it. In 1858 the Republic's ships are few and needed at home, and a court it could not enforce would be a court the shogunate would learn to ignore.
     - *The panel is a school.* Elias wants Japanese judges who can try a Californian merchant by 1870, and the only way to get them is to have them try Californians from 1858. The Japanese judges of the panels are the first commercial judges of Meiji (PROPOSED).
     - *The comparison is the point.* Every Japanese official who deals with Harris's consular court and with the Nagasaki panel in the same year learns which partner treats Japan as a state. Elias builds that comparison on purpose.
   - **Who objects.** California's Nagasaki merchants petition for Harris's terms in 1859, and the Council refuses them (PROPOSED).
4. **Technical and student reciprocity:** two-way shipyard and foundry training, leading directly to the March 1860 *Kanrin Maru* engineers' request for Japanese admission to Carquinez (`L308 ("Kanrin Maru")`).
   - *What Japan gains:* the methods.
   - *What the Republic gains:* the people. Japanese engineers become the Polytechnic's competitors and colleagues, so each side improves the other's work (`CR-031-R1`; `L760 ("Japanese and Pacifican engineers learn from one another and compete")`). Carquinez recruits its talent from every background (`CR-034`), and Japan is the deepest pool across the ocean.

**A1.3. Honoring the sunset in 1870–1871 (`C-1870-JP-ALLIANCE`; PROPOSED connective detail).**
Following Pacifica's restraint during the 1868 Edo transition (`L745 ("negotiated transition at Edo")`), Elias keeps the 1858 date in the **1870–1871 Japanese alliance** (`L747 ("1870–1871 Japanese alliance")`). The mixed panels end, the tariff is Japan's, and there is **no demand for unrestricted foreign landownership in the Japanese interior** (*naichi zakkyo* stays under Japanese domestic law; see `A5.2`).
- **The trade, not the gift (PROPOSED reading of canon):** the same alliance gives the Republic dockyard rights at Yokosuka, a coaling lease at Hakodate and Takashima coal (`L747`). Equality is what Japan gets; a western Pacific base system without a single sovereign foothold is what the Republic gets. A sovereign base would be a grievance Japan would one day fight to end. A lease from an equal is a right Japan will keep granting as long as it profits from it. The partnership is renewed by compact from 1887, every ten years (`L991`).
- **Why no interior land:** the Republic's merchants trade at the ports and do not need the interior. Demanding it would make Pacifica's treaty the template the Atlantic powers cite, and would aim Japan's grievance at Thalassia instead of London. Canon's "no consular courts" (`L747`) stands as written: there were never any. Mixed residence stays a live question all the same, because Britain and the Atlantic powers make it their price in the revisions of 1894–99 (*Hist.*), so the Japanese debate is softened, not removed. Pacifica achieves legal equality with Japan **twenty-four to forty years ahead** of Britain (1894/1899) and the Atlantic powers (*Hist.* for OTL's 1894 Anglo-Japanese treaty taking effect in 1899 and full tariff autonomy in 1911).

### A2. Why Japan accepts Pacifica: its own interest first, the broken ladder second (1853–1932)

Japan accepts the partnership because it wins by it, and the Japanese who say so are reasoning people with names. Pacifica's good conduct is the second reason, not the first. Japanese in our timeline grew xenophobic in a world where they were humiliated and robbed of the fruits of their wars. Here they are neither, and they can count the difference. *(Research behind this section: `outputs/sessions/20261125-cr088-japan/research-gains.md`.)*

**A2.1. The bargain on Japanese grounds (synthesis of canon; APPROVED direction, 25 November 2026, 14:36; wording PROPOSED).**
A Japanese statesman can defend the partnership in the Diet without one word of gratitude. Five sentences carry it, each resting on canon:
1. **Japan wins its wars with Pacifica, and is paid for the ones Pacifica stops.** In 1894 Victoria cuts the war short with Korea independent, an indemnity and Japan's economic predominance (`L1084`). In 1898–99 the army gets the land war it was denied and keeps the Ussuri region, Vladivostok, the Razdolnaya and all Sakhalin (`L1092 ("Japan gains the Ussuri region")`). In 1905 Witte comes to Urajio to sign (`L1213`). In our timeline Japan won its wars and lost their fruit, to Europe in 1895 and to its own exhausted treasury in 1905 (*Hist.*).
2. **Japan's territory grows without colonies of rice-growers.** Enkaishū, Urajio and Karafuto, the Marshall atolls from 1914 (`A2.6`), then Kamchatka and the Koryak coast under Japanese protection from 1921 (`L1515 ("a republic under Japanese protection")`), are cold lands, settled and worked. Korea stays independent, "so Japan runs no drive to draw a colony's rice into its home market, and the Japanese farmer's price is not broken by one" (`L1704 ("Korea is independent, so Japan runs no drive")`), and its rice keeps pace with its people from the 1890s (`L1188 ("Japan's home rice crop keeps pace with its people from the 1890s")`). The denial the army most resents, Korea (`A5`), is the reason Japan's villages do not starve.
3. **Japan's standing rises in every treaty.** Equal in 1870–71 (`L747`); its subjects equal in the Realm from 1907, because "a more confident Japan bargains hard" (`L1220 ("A more confident Japan bargains hard")`); its racial-equality clause in the peace of 1919 (`L1396 ("Japan's racial-equality clause")`); the combined fleets at parity with Britain (`L1403 ("Naval limits count the combined Pacifica–Japan fleets at parity with Britain")`); a co-equal party in 1927 (`L1615 ("Japan's Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council's conditional advice")`); the Navy seat by treaty law in 1932 (`L1619 ("the Navy seat is Japanese by treaty law")`).
4. **Japan's money is rescued and never conquered.** It receives credit in 1870–71, in 1873, for the gold transition of 1897 (`L1088 ("Pacifican gold loan support Japan's gold transition")`, PROPOSED in canon), after the earthquake of 1923 and in 1927. Each time the money is lent, never collected by a customs administration or a debt commission. In 1927 Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money and Pacifica supplies the foreign credit (`L1607`).
5. **Japan keeps what it cares most about, and refuses what it does not want.** The Emperor in Japan, the home army, the *koseki*, the schools and the language stay its own (`A4.3`; `L1626 ("Japan grants English no domestic official status")`). In 1871 Kido and Itō take Pacifica's money and refuse its constitution (`L749 ("Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi have read the Articles of 1856")`). In 1932 Takahashi refuses Julian the home army (`L1788`). Both refusals stand.

The grievances of `A5` are the price, and each has its holders.

**A2.2. Who in Japan argues for it, and against (PROPOSED).**
One voice for the partnership in each era, all already in canon, and one against, so that the case is an argument and not a brochure:
- **The 1850s: Shimazu Nariakira and Abe Masahiro.** Nariakira sees "a modest Pacific partner without missionary demands" as an alternative to stronger outside powers (`L300 ("a modest Pacific partner without missionary demands")`). Abe uses it as a counterweight: the shogunate can refuse one power by dealing with another. *Against:* the factions that distrust the treaty (`L306 ("A number of Japanese factions distrust the treaty")`) and, after 1858, the *shishi*.
- **1871: Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi.** They read the Articles of 1856 and conclude that Japan cannot afford a confederation of militias and vetoes (`L749`). Take the credit, learn the methods, build a unitary state: this is the partnership's first and most durable pattern. *Against, from 1877:* Satsuma. Pacifica backs the Meiji government against Saigō "and its oldest Japanese friends" (`L779 ("Pacifica backs the Meiji government against Saigō")`). From Kagoshima's grievance come the Gen'yōsha (1881) and, through its heirs, the Pan-Asian right whose charge in 1932 is not that Pacifica is foreign but that it chose Tokyo's state over Satsuma's honour (`A5`).
- **1907 and 1927: Saionji Kinmochi.** In 1907 he bargains with the Sacramento crisis as his lever (`L1220`) and refuses a Japanese count a crown equal to the Emperor's (`L1250 ("Saionji's government will not let a count of Japan take a crown")`). In 1927 he counsels acceptance of the Concord (`L1938 ("Saionji counsels acceptance in 1927")`). His argument is the constitutional one: Japan gains most by being indispensable to a partner it can bargain with, and loses most by trying to command it. *Against:* the army's continental school, which resents the shortened victory of 1894 (`L1084 ("Japan's army resents the shortened victory")`) and Korea's independence, and the navy that "wants Tsingtao and the German islands" (`L1336 ("Its navy wants Tsingtao and the German islands")`) and is denied the first (`L1403 ("Pacifica keeps the Tsingtao anchorage on lease")`). The most articulate statement of the dignity case comes from Saionji's own protégé. In December 1918, before he follows Saionji to the peace conference, the young Prince Konoe Fumimaro publishes an essay against a peace made to suit the great powers' convenience (*Hist.*: "Reject the Anglo-American-Centred Peace"; here its target is a peace centred on Pacifica and Britain). Konoe is a background voice and is not registered (D-26).
- **1927 and 1932: Takahashi Korekiyo.** His moratorium buys the time in 1927 (`L1605 ("Takahashi's three-week moratorium")`). In 1932 his cabinet states its offer as "a trade it means to win" (`L1788 ("a trade it means to win")`), and wins most of it. *Against:* the Restoration officers, for whom "Japan's villages pay for San Francisco's prosperity" (`L1584 ("Japan's villages pay for San Francisco's prosperity")`); Araki on Manchuria's acres; Mori Kaku on "the bakufu made permanent" (`L1792 ("the bakufu made permanent")`); Kashiwagi and the Kronstadt dead (`L1709 ("Japanese sailors died there for a Pacifican's hostages")`).
- **A public voice outside government: Nitobe Inazō (D-25; see `A2.5`).**

**A2.3. Breaking our timeline's humiliation ladder (PROPOSED).**
Late-Edo *jōi* ("expel the barbarian") was a defensive reaction against colonial subjugation and Christian subversion, coexisting with intense *rangaku* curiosity for practical craft (*Hist.*). Japanese ultranationalism in our timeline was forged over seventy years by cumulative racial and diplomatic humiliations. On the Pacific axis canon breaks each of them, or turns it:
1. **Unequal treaties (our timeline, 1858–1911):** Pacifica never has a consular court and ends its mixed panels on the fixed date of 1870 (`A1.2`, `A1.3`). Britain and the Atlantic powers revise only in 1894–99.
2. **The Triple Intervention of 1895:** none. Victoria's armistice of 1894 (`L1084 ("Victoria supports Japan's case for Korean independence")`) costs the army Liaodong, but by agreement, not by ultimatum.
3. **Portsmouth and the Hibiya riots of 1905:** avoided. Victoria advises Japan to refuse Roosevelt's mediation; Pacifican pressure on French lending brings Witte to terms; and Witte comes to Urajio to sign Russia's withdrawal from Manchuria, an indemnity and the recognition of Japan's southern gains (`L1213 ("Witte comes to Urajio")`).
4. **Racial exclusion (our timeline: San Francisco's schools in 1906, the Gentlemen's Agreement of 1907–08, the Alien Land Laws of 1913 and 1920, Paris in 1919, the Exclusion Act of 1924):** not avoided at home, but answered. Inside the Realm the 1906 school crisis happens too: Silas's nativists and Harlan Tate's Sacramento board segregate Japanese pupils (`L1220`). Tokyo bargains hard in the renewal of 1907 and wins a joint war council, family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans. In the same year as our timeline's restriction, Japan forces a gain. Pacifica passes no exclusion or alien land law. Japanese gains Realm standing in 1927 and county standing in Hawaii in 1928 (`L1043 ("English is the Realm")`). At The Hague in 1919 Japan wins its racial-equality clause and loses Shandong, the reverse of Paris (`L1396`, `L1403`). Only **British Columbia**, until Caledonia's Proclamation Day on 18 July 1932 (`L1160`, `L2037`), and **White Australia**, which refuses the clause even in 1932 (`L1870`), still bar the door, and both are British.
5. **Naval ratios (our timeline: 5:5:3 in 1922, London in 1930):** the ratio grievance moves inside the Concord. The combined fleets count at parity with Britain (`L1403`), and Japan argues instead over the quota, the yards' share and the reserved squadron (`A5`).
6. **Abandonment in crisis (our timeline: London and New York in the panic of 1927, the gold standard of 1930–31):** in 1923 Julian brings relief and reconstruction credit to Yokohama; in 1927, when London's acceptance houses pull out first, he supplies the foreign credit while Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money (`L1607 ("Japan rebuilds its banks with its own money")`). The credit is about $600 million committed and about $497 million drawn (MODEL, `finance/CR-031-review.md`).

**A2.4. Where Japanese xenophobia and anxiety *do* survive (APPROVED direction for returnees; structure PROPOSED).**
Japanese suspicion does not vanish. It **splits along two axes**:
- **Outward toward the Atlantic and the British dominions:** racial-dignity resentment concentrates on Britain, Russia, the Union, British Columbia and White Australia. That makes the Pacific partnership feel like a shield of mutual dignity rather than submission to the West.
- **Inward against Pacifica, as anxiety about *kokutai* and soft absorption:** critics of Pacifica rarely call Pacificans barbarians; too many Japanese know Hawaiʻi, California, Chiyo and Julian firsthand. The critique is sharper and more intimate: **that Pacifica's wealth, examination ethos, English technical command and dynastic marriages are quietly turning Japan into a cultured province of a "Thalassia bakufu"** (`L1681 ("Japanese Restoration circles call it a new bakufu")`). Its oldest root is Satsuma's grievance of 1877, not foreignness (`A2.2`). The full catalogue is in `A5`.

**A2.5. Nitobe Inazō: the scholar the Realm asks for (APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 16:32, D-25; particulars PROPOSED).**
*Hist.* Nitobe Inazō (1862–1933) was in the second class of the Sapporo Agricultural College and studied agricultural economics at Johns Hopkins and Halle, where he took his doctorate. At nine he was sent to Tokyo as his uncle's heir and took the name Ōta Inazō, reverting to Nitobe when his elder brother died. He later served as Under-Secretary-General of the League of Nations, and died at Victoria, British Columbia, in 1933. His *Bushido: The Soul of Japan* (preface dated Malvern, Pennsylvania, 1899) began with a Belgian professor's question, "No religion! How do you impart moral education?". It answered with a chivalric, Christian-tinted portrait of samurai virtue for a Western audience, which Japanese and Western scholars have since called an invention (*Hist.*).

**In the Realm.** Nitobe is a Sapporo man of the northern school that Sapporo and Sitka built together (`L739`), and Satō Shōsuke's junior there. His graduate years are at Carquinez instead of Johns Hopkins, and his doctorate at Halle, as in our timeline. As a Sapporo professor in the 1890s he is among the drafters of the Hokkaido Ainu Act of 1899 (`A5.1`), which tests his colonial-policy learning against Sitka's tools. His audience differs from our timeline's. The Realm does not need Japan made romantic to respect it, and its ethos forbids the romance (`CR-076`: never glamour). Its examination state has already answered the Belgian's question in its own way: churches keep their schools, the state pays only for what it examines (`L45`), and conduct is measured, not preached. So the Belgian's question reaches Nitobe in reverse. In about 1900 the Polytechnic asks him, at a challenge evening with a respondent (`L405 ("challenge evening begins there as a laboratory habit")`; Peirce, who held a Japan fellowship at seventeen, is the natural one), to show by evidence what made Japan able to learn so fast.

**What a rigorous Nitobe tells them (PROPOSED; the substance is *Hist.* scholarship, the occasion is the Realm's):**
1. **There was no single code.** What foreigners call bushido is largely a Meiji reading of scattered Tokugawa texts. The samurai of 1700–1850 were a salaried administrative class: clerks, accountants, magistrates and teachers on stipends, most of whom never fought.
2. **Japan learned fast because of institutions, not virtues:**
   - Two centuries of domain schools and some fifteen thousand commoners' *terakoya* gave it one of the most literate populations of its age.
   - Village self-government in five-household groups made a habit of collective liability and record-keeping.
   - Merchant houses such as Mitsui ran as corporations that outlived their families. The Osaka rice exchange traded futures from the 1730s.
   - Ishida Baigan's *shingaku* gave commoners an ethic of honest dealing.
3. **Adoption is Japan's market in talent.** A house that lacks an able heir adopts one, often a son-in-law, and gives him its name. Nitobe was adopted himself, as Kiyoshi was in 1886 (`L989`). That is the nearest Japanese equivalent of "achievement outranks birth", and the reason a Japanese family can read Carquinez's examination. It is also why the examination itself is foreign to Japan, which never adopted China's (*Hist.*).
4. **The danger is loyalty without an account.** Japan's ethic is loyalty to a house and a lord, not truth to a measure. Its most celebrated story, the forty-seven rōnin, praises men who broke the law for their lord's honour and were sentenced for it. "A loyalty that answers to no ledger," he tells the Polytechnic, "will one day be claimed by men who act in the Emperor's name against the Emperor's government." This is not a prophecy. It is the historian's reading of Chūshingura and of Japan's own *gekokujō* tradition, and the officers of 15 May 1932, "men claiming to act for him" (`L1938`), prove it right.
5. **What Japan should take from the Realm, and what it should refuse.** Take the measure, the published account and the open licence. Refuse the examination as the road to power: Japan's houses and its adopted heirs already find talent, and an examination aristocracy would hand the country to the Carquinez returnees (`A5.2`). This is a Japanese scholar's case for the partnership on Japanese terms, made to the Realm's face.

**Why the Realm values him.** He tells the Polytechnic what it did not want to hear and proves it. Peirce's response, which he gives, is the evening's other half (`C`, set piece). The lectures are printed in English and Japanese. The Restoration circles never forgive the fourth point, and the Japanese purists never forgive the fifth.
- **After 1900 (PROPOSED, sketch):** women's education, as in our timeline; a seat on the Hague court's panel of arbitrators after 1919 in place of the League (canon has a permanent arbitration court, `L1403`); and the Japanese council of a Pacific relations institute at Honolulu from 1925 (*Hist.*: the Institute of Pacific Relations was founded at Honolulu in 1925). His death at Victoria in October 1933 falls after the book's end.
- **Registration:** `CT-NITOBE-INAZO` (1862–1933) joins the cast of `A7` as a person who can carry a scene.

**A2.6. The Marshalls: the one German group Japan keeps (APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 17:03, D-27; particulars PROPOSED).**
- **An old gap.** The Marshalls have stood inside Pacifica's ring of islands since 1885, by Pacifica's own bargain. At the papal arbitration Pacifica took the Carolines, and "Germany keeps the Marshalls" (`L804 ("Germany keeps the Marshalls")`). Pacifica wanted the Carolines' harbours and Wake for a cable, and left the copra atolls to the German trading houses at Jaluit. So in 1919 Japan does not open a hole in the ring. It inherits one Germany held for thirty-four years.
- **1914.** The Japanese navy, which "wants Tsingtao and the German islands" (`L1336 ("Its navy wants Tsingtao and the German islands")`), takes Jaluit and the Marshalls in the autumn, as in our timeline (*Hist.*). Meanwhile Pacifica and its partners take Samoa, Rabaul and Nauru (`L1336 ("Pacifica and Japan take Germany")`).
- **1919.** The Treaty of The Hague confirms the division. The Marshalls go to Japan; Samoa, German New Guinea with Rabaul, and Nauru go to Pacifica, which holds them, like Micronesia, in Ka Lōkahi's trust (`L1336`, `L1615 ("Ka Lōkahi takes the Marianas, Carolines and Palau into its trust")`). The division follows the equator, roughly the line of our timeline's own wartime division (*Hist.*). **Nauru**, which the Germans governed from Jaluit, is carved out for its phosphate and stays in Pacifica's trust. Japan buys its phosphate at the same published price as Australia and New Zealand, because Japan's fields need it and the Realm does not withhold the means of food.
- **Why Pacifica agrees (Julian's reasoning):**
  - *Japan is already there,* and the same conference has already refused it Tsingtao (`L1403`). A second refusal to an ally at one peace would break the partnership on its own ground. Shandong matters to Pacifica's China policy; the atolls cost it nothing. Japan's ambition goes onto coral instead of Chinese soil.
  - *Inside the ring is the safest place for Japanese expansion.* Pacifica surrounds the group: Wake to the north, the Carolines and Guam to the west, Nauru to the south-west, the equatorial islands to the east. The trunk wire runs north of it (`L1195 ("a second span from Midway by Wake to Guam")`). The group cannot be fortified under the naval limits of 1919, and it depends on Pacifican coal, cables and copra buyers. The cipher room never reads Japanese traffic (`L1679 ("The cipher room never reads Japanese traffic")`); here geography does the watching.
  - *What Japan wants there is cheap to give:* the southern school's pride, and bonito grounds for Okinawan fishermen. The fish's way to the kitchen is `CR-089`'s.
- **The three conditions of 1919 (PROPOSED):**
  1. No fortification, under the naval limits.
  2. No alienation of Marshallese land. Japan writes Ka Lōkahi's rule into its own law, a second case of the mutual influence of `A5.1`.
  3. **Freedom for the Hawaiian missions.** The Marshalls' Protestants are Hawaii's converts: the Micronesia mission sent Hawaiian teachers there from Honolulu from the late 1850s (*Hist.*, to verify). The condition mirrors the 1885 condition for the Catholic missions in the Carolines (`L804`). It also reverses the Treaty of Edo: in 1858 California promised Japan no missionaries (`A1.2`), and in 1919 Japan promises to protect Hawaii's.
- **The grievance runs the other way, for once.** Pacifica's admirals object in 1919 to a Japanese group inside the ring, and the Honolulu churches distrust Japanese rule over their congregations. The admirals' objection fades after 1927, when the common Navy guards the whole ring, and in 1932 its minister is Japanese. The churches' distrust does not fade entirely. Hawaii's sealed minute on its island claims (`CR-075`) does not reach the Marshalls; its claims are the uninhabited islands of its own chain and the equatorial line.

### A3. Civilizational fit: why two societies mesh across the Pacific

**A3.1. Hawaiʻi as the non-European monarchical bridge — Kalākaua's March 1881 Tokyo tour (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Japan could never culturally mirror a white-settler egalitarian republic with an evangelical missionary impulse. Pacifica's crowns are **Polynesian**: Hawaii's is Victoria Kamāmalu's from 1872, she is Regent of the Realm from 1887, and Julian, of Noe's Maui line, holds both crowns from 1907. They are seated on a volcanic Pacific island, carry a civic language of *mana* and send no state missionaries. Before 1887 the Japanese see two things side by side: Elias's republic, which has kept its word since 1858, and the Queen's court.
- **March 1881 (`C-1881-TOKYO-EMIGRATION`, PROPOSED):** Adapting his real tour (*Hist.*: he reached Yokohama on 4 March 1881 as King, and the Meiji Emperor gave him the Grand Cordon of the Order of the Chrysanthemum), **David Kalākaua** (`CT-DAVID-KALAKAUA`) visits Tokyo as **Victoria Kamāmalu's minister of the image and envoy** (`L848`), not as a sovereign. He receives the Grand Cordon of the Order of the Rising Sun; the band at Yokohama plays Hawaii's anthem, unnamed (PROPOSED).
- **The convention (APPROVED, D-20; terms PROPOSED).** Kalākaua concludes the **1881 Hawaii–Japan Family Emigration Convention**, the Kingdom of Hawaii's own: family passages to Hawaii, no penal contracts (Hawaii's abolition of 1856, `L51`), wives and children travelling with the men or after them. It binds neither California nor the Confederation. On the mainland Japanese families keep coming under the earlier scheme and the assisted passages (`L783`), and the right to family entry and civic equality across the Realm is Tokyo's win in the renewal of 1907 (`L1220`). In our timeline Japan allowed contract emigration to Hawaii only from 1885, after Kalākaua's request (*Hist.*).
- **The proposal (APPROVED, D-21; details PROPOSED).** In our timeline Kalākaua proposed in a private audience that his five-year-old niece Kaʻiulani marry a young imperial prince, and that Japan lead a union of Asian nations; both were declined in letters of 1882 (*Hist.*). Here he makes the marriage proposal alone, beyond his instructions, and no federation, since Hawaii is in the Confederation. Tokyo declines courteously in 1882, the prince pleading a prior engagement. Victoria, told afterwards, says nothing in public and makes him answerable, as after the mutiny (`L818 ("makes him answerable for the Guard's loyalty")`). The first dynastic idea between the courts thus comes from Hawaii and is refused; the one that happens, in 1907, comes from the Kuroda side (`L1258 ("In November 1907 Julian marries Lady Chiyo")`). No sentence on the page links 1881 to Kaʻiulani's ministry of 1931.
- **What the visit leaves.** Kalākaua's reception stamps the image of **two sister Pacific monarchies** on the Japanese public.

**A3.2. Shared grammar of form, craft, and merit (PROPOSED synthesis of existing canon).**
Pacifica's examination state—*On the Public Arts*, anonymous scripts, achievement outranking birth, retirement at 68, the Five (*nā Kahuna*), and *the earned cup* (`L887`, linking Japanese *oshaku* with Hawaiian *ʻawa*)—is culturally legible to Meiji and Taishō Japan in a way Jacksonian Atlantic politics never was. It speaks a recognizable vocabulary of *jitsugaku* (practical learning), craft discipline, and institutional form. Shared **Pacific hazard solidarity**—earthquakes, tsunamis (`C-1897-WARNINGS`), and typhoons—deepens a common sobriety toward nature and engineering.

**A3.3. Seismic engineering and San Francisco's *Pacific Modern* architecture (`C-1906-SEISMIC-CODE`; APPROVED direction; specifics PROPOSED).**
For Japanese pride to accept permanent union, cultural traffic must run **both ways**, and nowhere is that reversal more visible than in San Francisco—the world's pre-eminent city by the 1930s:
- **Seismic engineering (Japan teaches California and Thalassia):** Following the **April 1906 San Francisco earthquake** (*Hist.*), Japanese seismologist **Ōmori Fusakichi** (`CT-OMORI-FUSAKICHI`, already registered in `CR-085`) and structural engineer **Sano Toshikata** (`CT-SANO-TOSHIKATA`, *Hist.* 1880–1956) join the Polytechnic's reconstruction commission in San Francisco to map Bay-mud failure against bedrock (*Hist.* for Ōmori and Sano's 1906 California survey). Sano's pupil **Naitō Tachū** (`CT-NAITO-TACHU`, *Hist.* 1886–1970)—who studies structural mechanics at Carquinez rather than MIT—develops rigid-frame shear-wall design whose Tokyo buildings (Industrial Bank of Japan, Kabuki-za) survive the **1 September 1923 Great Kantō Earthquake** (*Hist.*). After 1923, Japan's 1924 seismic code and Naitō's reinforced-wall methods become the **shared Pacific standard** for Thalassia, San Francisco, and Manila.
- **San Francisco's equivalent to Art Deco — *Pacific Modern* (1910s–1930s):** Whereas New York's Art Deco chases thin Gothic verticality on Manhattan bedrock, San Francisco is rebuilt as a seismic, hydroelectric, aluminium-rich Pacific capital, fusing Bay Area timber pioneers (the tradition of Bernard Maybeck, Julia Morgan, and Greene & Greene) with Japanese *kigumi* joinery and Naitō's shear-wall engineering:
  - *Terraced seismic massing:* Civic towers step back from battered stone bases echoing **Japanese castle walls (*ishigaki*) and Southwestern Pueblo/adobe terraces**, rather than precarious Atlantic needle spires.
  - *Structure as ornament:* Deeply recessed reinforced-concrete frames clad in Sierra granite, volcanic tuff, and **matte anodized aluminium** (from Pacifica's own aluminium champion, `CR-054`), crowned by **wide cantilevered copper-and-aluminium eaves** (*tokyō* reimagined in metal) that shed Bay fog and conceal indirect electric uplighting.
  - *Thresholds and water courts:* Wide aluminium-and-redwood ***shōji*-grid curtain walls** open major lobbies and county halls onto quiet interior water courts blending the **Californio *patio***, **Japanese *tsuboniwa*** (moss, basalt, raked gravel), and **Hawaiian running water (*loʻi*)**.
  - *Geometric frieze language:* Glazed terracotta and cast-aluminium friezes weave **Japanese *seigaiha* (wave) and *asanoha* lattices** with **Hawaiian *kapa* geometry** and **Spanish-Californio majolica**. By 1931 the Realm's code requires shear walls and stepped massing above six storeys, and it ties height to ground (see the skyline below) (PROPOSED).
- **The fight over the city (PROPOSED, D-30):** Pacific Modern wins, and others lose.
  - *Burnham's boulevards.* In 1905 Daniel Burnham's plan gives San Francisco Paris-style radial boulevards and a Beaux-Arts civic centre (*Hist.*). The city's Beaux-Arts architects and its improvement association want it built after the fire. The seismic commission's maps of Bay mud against bedrock (`A3.3`, first item) cut the boulevards where the ground will not hold them. In the civic-centre competition of 1912 a Pacific Modern entry beats the Beaux-Arts favourite (*Hist.*: in our timeline the Beaux-Arts design of Bakewell and Brown won). The losers go to Los Angeles and to the Union, and say for a generation that San Francisco chose engineers over architects.
  - *Ōmori against Imamura.* The seismologists have their own quarrel. In our timeline Imamura Akitsune warned in 1905 that a great earthquake would strike Tokyo, Ōmori dismissed the warning in public, and 1923 proved Imamura right; Ōmori died that November (*Hist.*). Here the quarrel is put to a challenge evening at Carquinez in 1906, in front of the Realm's engineers (`L405`). Ōmori is the respondent, and the evening is reported in both countries. The Realm's code takes the larger figure. Tokyo does not, and after 1923 Japan's engineers are first in line to adopt Naitō's walls. Ōmori, who taught California so much, loses the one argument that mattered most to him. Imamura is a background figure, not registered.
  - *The carpenters.* Japanese joinery comes to San Francisco with Japanese carpenters, and the city's carpenters' union fights their licences until 1895, when the trades congress registers "tradesmen who have passed the Polytechnic's trade tests" (`L1080 ("the register of tradesmen who have passed the Polytechnic's trade tests")`) and the Japanese carpenters pass them (PROPOSED).
- **The ninety years of San Francisco: six layers, not one style (PROPOSED, D-32).** Each generation adds a layer, and by 1932 all of them stand together. Pacific Modern is one layer among them. It is the establishment by 1925, and it is not the newest.
  1. **The Gold Rush city (1846–1860s).** Canvas, imported prefabricated iron, and the fires of 1849–51 that turn it to brick; the Montgomery Block of 1853 (*Hist.*), which still stands in 1932. Around it are Californio adobe and, at Honolulu, coral-block churches. Nobody builds for earthquakes yet.
  2. **Ralston's city (1860s–1887).** The Bank of California and Ralston's Palace Hotel (*Hist.*, 1866 and 1875), built on bets that in this timeline pay (`CR-073`). The bay-windowed wooden rows of the hills. Hallidie's wire rope (`L330 ("Hallidie, who came as a boy in 1852")`) becomes the cable car. After the Hayward earthquake of 1868 (*Hist.*), the first rules on brick, and the first Japanese carpenters who know how a wooden frame should move.
  3. **The regency's revivals (1887–1906).** The Realm's heritage made into buildings:
     - the Mission revival in California's county halls (*Hist.* for the Mission Revival of the 1890s);
     - lava stone and coral at Honolulu under Victoria, beside Kalākaua's revival of the chants (`L848`);
     - the first tall steel frames on electric elevators;
     - Ernest Ransome's reinforced concrete, which in our timeline gave Golden Gate Park the Alvord Lake bridge of 1889 (*Hist.*);
     - the Ferry Building (*Hist.*, 1898), which survives everything after it.
  4. **The rebuilding and Pacific Modern (1906–1925)**, as above: the seismic commission's maps, Burnham's boulevards defeated, the civic centre of 1912, Maybeck's and Morgan's craft, Japanese joinery and Naitō's walls.
  5. **The Calculated (*Keisan-ha*, 1923–1932): the modern layer (name and origin APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 18:25, D-34; particulars PROPOSED).** It arrives when three things meet:
     - the Kantō earthquake, which proves Naitō's walls;
     - the Calculation Office's machines: Fuller's Carquinez machine from 1921, the stored-program machine from 1928 (`L107 ("the first stored-program machine runs in 1928")`). With them the Office can compute how a whole building moves in an earthquake, which no drafting room anywhere can do;
     - the Realm's aluminium and its cheap power.

     **The origin.**
     - *September 1923.* Tokyo burns, and Naitō's Industrial Bank and Kabuki-za stand. So does Frank Lloyd Wright's Imperial Hotel, and Wright says so in every paper.
     - *Spring 1924, a challenge evening at Carquinez* (`L405`). Naitō brings his photographs and his wall calculations, with a Pacific Modern master as respondent. The respondent's point is fair: Naitō sized his walls by hand and by judgement, so who can say why they held? Naitō answers that nobody can yet, and asks for machine time.
     - *The machine.* The Office already "sells tables and machine time to every navy and almanac in the world" (`L1655 ("sells tables and machine time to every navy and almanac in the world")`). Sophia Levy, its first order-writer (`CT-SOPHIA-LEVY`), turns Naitō's frames into orders. Within a year the Office can show by calculation why a building holds. That is the school's real foundation: architecture becomes something proved at a challenge evening.
     - *1925, the Gate competition.* The jury is Pacific Modern's establishment, and it chooses eaved stone towers on granite footings. The losing entry is Othmar Ammann's (`CT-OTHMAR-AMMANN`, 1879–1965). He is a Swiss engineer who came west in 1904 by attraction instead of to New York, where in our timeline he built the George Washington Bridge of 1931, the longest span in the world (*Hist.*). His entry has slender steel towers clad in aluminium and a deck stiffened by deflection theory, with Levy's calculations attached. The engineers publish their numbers. At a second challenge evening the winning towers fail under the Office's wind and earthquake loads, and the Patrimony, which is paying, gives Ammann the bridge. It opens in 1929.
     - *The name.* A Pacific Modern master (Bernard Maybeck, in the background) says the Gate towers were "calculated, not designed". The young print the word on the cover of their journal, as Impressionists and Fauves once took their names from their critics (*Hist.*).
     - *The creed:* **a building states what it carries, and nothing else.** It is the Republic's rule against the false account (`L242`) applied to structure. It is also why the Calculated call Pacific Modern "costume with eaves".
     - *The circle (background, not registered):* Fuller and Levy at the Office (`CT-LEONARD-FULLER`, `CT-SOPHIA-LEVY`, notes only); Naitō (`CT-NAITO-TACHU`); Vladimir Shukhov, whose towers are licensed through the Realm's ties with federal Russia after 1921; Eduardo Torroja, the young Spaniard, on a Carquinez fellowship in 1925–27, whose first crews for the shells are the Spanish-speaking counties' engineers; Walther Bauersfeld's Zeiss dome; Leon Moisseiff's deflection theory, used without him, which the Union counts as a grievance; and Tokyo's Secession group of 1920 (Bunriha: Horiguchi Sutemi, Yamada Mamoru of the Central Telegraph Office of 1925), the school's Japanese wing, whom the Japanist critics of `A5.12` call traitors twice over (*Hist.* for all; to verify).

     Its buildings are unmistakably modern, and some of them amaze the world:
     - **The Gate span (1925–29, PROPOSED):** Ammann's suspension bridge across the Golden Gate, the longest span in the world when it opens in 1929. Its deck is stiffened by deflection theory worked on the Office's machines, and its towers are clad in anodized aluminium.
     - **The Twin Peaks tower (1930, PROPOSED):** a steel hyperboloid lattice that carries the press estate's television and the wireless chain above the fog (`L111 ("public television from 1930")`). It is built on the principle of Vladimir Shukhov's Russian towers of 1896 and 1922 (*Hist.*). From the city at night it looks like a lit net hung in the sky.
     - **The shells:** thin concrete vaults a few centimetres thick, computed and not guessed. They cover the electric trains' Transbay terminal and an air harbour's hangars. Freyssinet's and Zeiss-Dywidag's European shells of the 1920s are their teachers (*Hist.*).
     - **The planetarium in Golden Gate Park (late 1920s, PROPOSED):** under a geodesic steel dome of the kind Walther Bauersfeld built at Jena for the Zeiss projector in 1922–26 (*Hist.*), the first in the Pacific.
     - **The pendulum towers:** a handful of towers of thirty to forty storeys, and only on bedrock (Nob Hill, Rincon Hill, the foot of Telegraph Hill). Each has a steel core, Naitō's walls and a tuned pendulum hung at the top to damp the sway (*Hist.*: Frahm's patent of 1909 and Den Hartog's theory of 1928, to verify). Each design is proved at a challenge evening before the code admits it.
     - **Night:** buildings designed to be lit, on power that the Columbia sells cheaply after dark (`L1571 ("raise their steam in electric boilers on that secondary power")`). San Francisco is the first city whose skyline is composed for the night.
  6. **Thalassia (from 1928)** is not San Francisco. The Hawaiian capital follows the Thalassia Plan: tree belts, electric trams, no coal, low buildings of lava stone and timber in gardens (`L1697 ("With Victoria's backing he drafts the Thalassia Plan of 1928")`). The two capitals argue with each other in stone.
- **The skyline is a geological map (PROPOSED).** The code ties height to ground. The towers stand on the hills' rock; the old Bay fill and the made ground of the waterfront carry low, broad buildings on shear walls and shells. Anyone who knows the seismic commission's map of 1906 can read it in the skyline of 1932.
- **The last fight of the period (PROPOSED).** By 1925 Pacific Modern is the establishment. Its masters say machines make no architecture. The Calculated say Pacific Modern is costume with eaves. The young are Carquinez-trained, Pacifican and Japanese, with a few Austrians who came west by attraction (*Hist.*: Rudolph Schindler and Richard Neutra in Los Angeles from 1914 and 1923; background). Frank Lloyd Wright of the Union, whose Imperial Hotel in Tokyo survives 1923, quarrels in print with Naitō over who knew how to build for an earthquake (*Hist.*; Wright a background figure). In 1932 the civic towers are Pacific Modern, and the bridge, the tower and the shells belong to the Calculated. Neither school has won.

**A3.4. *Onsen* culture in the Rockies, Sierra, and American Southwest (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Pacifica spans North America's richest geothermal belt—Calistoga, Tassajara, Mammoth, and Lassen in California; Steamboat Springs in Washoe; **Ojo Caliente, Jemez Springs, and Pagosa Springs** in New Mexico and the Ute country (where Pueblo peoples already held centuries-old thermal bathing traditions); Glenwood Springs and Ouray on the Western Slope; Lava Hot Springs in Idaho; Lolo and Chico in Montana; and volcanic steam vents on Hawaiʻi Island.
- **Homeland compacts and *ryokan* lodges:** Rather than turning mineral springs into OTL's cramped Victorian sanitariums or seizing sacred springs, thermal valleys on Native lands (Pueblo, Ute, Shoshone, Paiute) are leased by compact with village royalties. Japanese bathmasters and stone-masons partner with Pacifica's **electrified mountain railways** (`L81`) to build timber-and-adobe *ryokan* lodges beside steaming granite and red-rock ***rotenburo* (open-air outdoor baths)** under falling Rocky Mountain snow, redwoods, or New Mexico stars.
- **Who loses (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The cure hotels.* The Victorian spa hotels that sold mineral water as a cure lose their trade once the Office of Public Measures prints the springs' analyses and the cure claims cannot stand beside them; the bathhouse sells only the bath (PROPOSED; how the Realm's label law treats health claims is `CR-089`'s, and nothing here rests on it).
  - *A refusal.* One Pueblo refuses to lease its sacred spring at any royalty, and the Realm's rule (no grant without a compact, `L739`) holds: the line runs from Santa Fe past the valley, and the valley stays closed (which Pueblo is OPEN).
- **Where the bath takes root, and where it does not (PROPOSED, D-33).** Five things decide it:
  - who owns the water: a nation under its compact, a county, or a private owner;
  - whether a bathing tradition is already there (the Shoshone's, Ute's, Tewa's or Tlingit's own uses of the springs; Spanish *baños*; the Finnish sauna; the Saints' Warm Springs; the Hawaiian sea);
  - faith and modesty;
  - whether Japanese families, railway crews or fishermen live nearby;
  - whether the electric railway reaches it.

  The memorable outcomes are those where the tradition already there wins its own terms:
  1. **Pagosa, the Ute bath.** The Ute keep the great spring in their homeland (`L665 ("The Utes keep Colorado west of the divide")`), and Ouray's people decide what is done with it; Ouray himself grew up at Taos speaking Spanish (`L665`). The Southern Ute council hires Japanese bathmasters on contract and writes its own rules: women's hours, no photographs, and the sweat lodge apart and closed to outsiders. The baths' takings pay for the Ute schools. By the 1920s it is the Realm's most famous winter bath, and it belongs to nobody but the Ute.
  2. **Ojo Caliente, *baños* and the Tewa.** Spanish New Mexico already bathes. The Hispano bathhouse takes the Japanese rule of washing before entering, keeps Catholic separate days, and serves chile and coffee, not tea. The ruins of the old Tewa village above the springs stay closed under the compact. The result is called neither onsen nor *baño* by the people who use it.
  3. **The Saints' bath: Salt Lake's Warm Springs and the Wasatch.** The Saints bathed at the Warm Springs within a few years of 1847, where Shoshone had camped before them (*Hist.*). Deseret takes the bath readily, on its own terms: family tubs, separate days, no mixed bathing anywhere. Because the Word of Wisdom forbids hot drinks, no tea and no cup: the bath ends with cold barley water. The Swiss Saints' crater spring at Midway (*Hist.*, to verify) becomes Deseret's resort.
  4. **The Columbia bath, without a hot spring.** At Astoria and up the lower river, Finnish fishermen's saunas meet the Japanese cannery crews' tubs: steam, a hot soak, then a plunge into the cold river. The water is heated by the dams' cheap night power (`L1571`), so it is the bath that the Realm's electricity makes. It is its own thing within twenty years, and the Finns and the Japanese both claim it.
  5. **The family tub of the farm counties.** In the Hood River orchards and the Delta, Japanese farm families keep the family *furo* at home (*Hist.* for Hood River's family baths). It stays private and spreads only to neighbours who are invited. That is the most common bath in the Realm, and the least visible.
  6. **Tenakee, the northern school's bath.** The springs at Tenakee have a Tlingit name and long Tlingit use (*Hist.*). Under the Sitka compact (`L739`), the Tlingit, the cannery hands and the miners share one bathhouse by hours, kept by a committee. Sapporo's and Sitka's men soak there on their way between the two northern schools. It is the plainest bath in the Realm and the one Hokkaido visitors like best.
  7. **Fort Hall's springs, neutral ground.** In our timeline the Shoshone-Bannock lost Lava Hot Springs in a cession of 1902 (*Hist.*, to verify). Here the springs stay inside the Fort Hall homeland, and the nation opens them to settlers on its own terms. In the interior's quarrels (`CR-071`), the nation's bath is one of the few places settlers and Shoshone meet as nothing but bathers.
  8. **Where it does not take: Hawaii and Calistoga.**
     - *Hawaii* has no hot springs but the volcano's steam on Pele's land, and Hawaiians keep the vents of Kīlauea closed to bathing. The plantation camps' tubs and Honolulu's public bathhouses stay Japanese neighbourhood baths, and the Hawaiian bath is the sea. The onsen never becomes a resort culture in the islands.
     - *Calistoga* keeps its own Californian mud-and-cure tradition (*Hist.* for its mud baths). The Japanese bath is tried there and fails; the mud stays.
  9. **The reverse: Beppu.** In our timeline Aburaya Kumahachi (1863–1935) travelled for three years in North America, became a Christian, and in 1911 founded the Kamenoi inn at Beppu. He rebuilt it as a Western-style hotel in 1924 and in 1928 ran Japan's first sightseeing buses with women guides (*Hist.*). Here his three years are spent in the Realm's railway resorts. Beppu becomes the Japanese onsen town remade on a Pacifican model of rail, advertising and service, and Japan's purists say the bath has come home spoiled. Aburaya is a background figure, not registered.
- **Civic ritual and realistic friction:** Electric mountain trains run winter "snow-bath specials" out of San Francisco, Salt Lake, Santa Fe and Boise, and **Prince Chichibu** (`L1683`), whose outdoorsman's reputation is canon, is among the bathers; whether he or anyone is the trail's patron is left OPEN for the destination CR (see `## Hand-offs`). Stripped of titles in the cedar bathhouse, senators, physicists, ranchers, and naval officers soak together before stepping out in cotton robes for tea; the cup comes only in the evening and only after a deed, a climb or a day's survey, as `CR-076` requires. Where Deseret/Mormon and Methodist interior counties balk at communal nudity, Pacifica develops a characteristic split: strictly sex-separated baths everywhere, private family tubs (*kashikiri*) in conservative interior counties, and strict Japanese bathing etiquette (scrub thoroughly *before* entering the water) on the coast, in Hawaiʻi, and at railway resorts.

**A3.5. Music, cinema, and the "Pacific String" sound (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Building directly on `CR-079`'s Hawaiian steel guitar (`CT-JOSEPH-KEKUKU`), **George Beauchamp's** electric steel guitar (`CT-GEORGE-BEAUCHAMP`, `L111`), public radio (1912), electrical records, and Honolulu Japanese-language talkies (`L1653`):
- **The bent note (*Pacific String*; music corrected in revision 9):** the bridge between the traditions is the bent and sliding pitch, not a shared scale. Each instrument bends notes its own way:
- the *shakuhachi* player bends a note by shading the hole and tilting the head (*meri* and *kari*);
- the *koto* player raises a string's pitch by pressing it behind the bridge (*oshide*);
- the steel guitarist slides the bar along the string.

Hawaiian melody, Spanish guitar and the Western ballad do not share the Japanese scales. What they share is the voice that slides between notes. By the 1910s–20s Honolulu, Tokyo and Los Angeles studios pioneer an intimate balladry built on it, pairing amplified steel guitar with *koto*, Spanish guitar and *shakuhachi*. It is a transpacific counterpart to Japan's guitar-led *ryūkōka* (*Hist.* for Koga Masao's guitar songs of 1931, to verify).
- **Who resists (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The purists on both sides.* The *iemoto* houses of *koto* and *shakuhachi*, which license teachers and own the repertoire, forbid their licensed players to record with amplified instruments (*Hist.* for the *iemoto* system). The best young players leave the houses to record, and lose their licences.
  - *The conservatory.* San Francisco's conservatory refuses the steel guitar a chair until 1928.
- **The benshi's last stand (PROPOSED, D-30):** Honolulu's and San Francisco's Japanese film houses have *benshi* narrators, as Tokyo's do. When talking features come from Los Angeles in 1919–20 (`CR-079`), the narrators strike, as Tokyo's did in our timeline in 1932 (*Hist.*), and they lose. A few become radio's first voices; most do not.
- **Cinema and *ma*:** Influenced by Japan's *benshi* storytelling tradition and *ma* (deliberate interval and stillness), Honolulu and Los Angeles talking features develop a calmer, more visually composed dramatic rhythm than Broadway-derived Atlantic sound films.
- **Concert halls:** San Francisco and Tokyo conservatories routinely score *taiko* percussion and *shakuhachi* alongside Western strings, giving Pacifica's symphonic culture a recognizably oceanic voice.

**A3.6. Games and physical culture: Go, Riichi Mahjong, *hanafuda*, baseball, regattas, and *jūdō* (APPROVED direction; specifics PROPOSED).**
1. **Go (*Igo*) at Carquinez and the Calculation Office:** Introduced by Japanese students at Carquinez from 1860, **Go** displaces chess as the prestige board game of Pacifica's engineers, naval staff, and the Calculation Office (`CT-CHARLES-PEIRCE`, `CT-JOHN-VON-NEUMANN`). Where chess is European annihilation of a king, Go embodies Pacifica's own strategic grammar—influence, balance, efficiency, and living groups—with *goban* boards standard in county halls and Rocky Mountain *onsen* lounges.
   - **The fight (PROPOSED, D-30):**
     - *Chess does not go quietly.* The Polytechnic's chess club keeps its room, its annual match against the Union's colleges and its argument that chess is the cleaner test of calculation, and it is right about the last. Go wins the naval staff and the Calculation Office; chess keeps the artillery school.
     - *The professional houses.* Japan's Go houses, the Hon'inbō first among them, grant ranks only to their own pupils (*Hist.* for the houses and for the Nihon Ki-in of 1924). For forty years Pacifica's best amateurs go to Tokyo to be beaten. The first Pacifican to take a professional rank is a Japanese Pacifican engineer, in the 1920s (PROPOSED; name OPEN), and the houses say for years that he was really Japanese.
2. **Riichi Mahjong (crystallized c. 1924–1930) and *Shōkin-jan* (Specie Mahjong):** Historically, Mahjong reached Japan in **1909** (*Hist.*), boomed in Tokyo in **1924** under Saburō Hirayama (*Hist.*), and was standardized under novelist Kikuchi Kan's **Japan Mahjong Federation in October 1929** (*Hist.*), whereas OTL America turned Joseph Babcock's simplified 1920 *"Mah-Jongg"* into a short-lived orientalist fad and missed the deep Japanese game. In Pacifica, because authentic Chinese Mahjong is already played in San Francisco, Honolulu, and Shanghai's Customs circles (`CR-081`), there is no dumbed-down Babcock craze. Instead, Chinese tile combinatorics, Japanese defensive reading (**neatly ordered *kawa* discard rows, *furiten* defense, strict *yaku* structure**), and Californian/Pacifican poker and actuarial stake-culture collide in the **1924–1930 Tokyo–Honolulu–San Francisco clubs**, Navy wardrooms, and Polytechnic common rooms—crystallizing the ***riichi* ready-hand wager** (laying a 1,000-point stick sideways and locking one's hand in public) roughly fifteen years ahead of OTL (*Hist.*: in OTL full *riichi*/*furiten*/*dora* coalesced post-WWII; here *dora* bonus indicators remain an emerging 1931–32 Osaka/Kansai parlor variant or post-1932). **John von Neumann** (`L107`), publishing his real 1928 game-theory paper on imperfect-information games, encounters a four-player imperfect-information game of defensive folding (*betaori*) and declared risk right inside Carquinez. Paired with Go, Riichi Mahjong becomes Pacifica's signature game of diplomacy and crisis reading.
   - **The Pacifican high-stakes twist — *Shōkin-jan* (正金雀, "Specie Mahjong", c. 1927–1932; PROPOSED):** Born in San Francisco clearing-house clubs, Tokyo Nihonbashi back rooms, and Navy wardrooms right after the 1927 banking panic, *Shōkin-jan* is a playable, zero-prop variation on standard 136-tile Riichi that fuses Pacifica's 50% gold-cover doctrine (`CR-057`) with *On the Public Arts*'s weaponized truth (`CR-050`/`CR-051`). Stacks split into **Gold Sticks (*Shōkin*)** and **Paper Notes (*Tegata*)**; players may declare **Paper *Riichi*** even while *noten* (stacking 1–3 Paper Notes with up to two *tedashi* tile swaps to scramble into *tenpai*—or baiting a trap with a genuine monster hand); opponents may buy a **Soft Assay (*Kantei*)** for 1,000 Gold to force the declarer to expose two *true* tiles of the declarer's own choosing, or slam the felt and **Call Cover (*Shōkin Seikyū* / Bank Run)** to force an immediate showdown; and an insolvent player with zero Gold faces the **Discount Window**, where rivals may advance a conditional rescue loan or **Refuse to Print (*Hakkō Kyohi*)**. Full playable rules are recorded in `## Appendix A — Rules of Shōkin-jan (Specie Mahjong)`.
3. ***Hanafuda* (Flower Cards):** Already beloved in Hawaiʻi as *Sakura*, *hanafuda* spreads across California, the Rockies, and Baja/Sonora, printed in regional Pacific editions adding redwood, cactus blossom, and hibiscus alongside pine, plum, and cherry.
4. **Transpacific university sport and martial arts:** In **April 1905**, mid-Russo-Japanese War (*Hist.*), Professor **Abe Isoo** (`CT-ABE-ISOO`, *Hist.* 1865–1949) leads **Waseda University's** baseball nine on a tour of California and Hawaiʻi—playing **Carquinez, Ralston College, and Honolulu** while Kiyoshi engages the Petropavlovsk squadron—launching an annual **Pacific Intercollegiate Series** (Waseda, Keiō, and Meiji rotating against Carquinez, Ralston, and Honolulu), alongside Carquinez–Tokyo Bay rowing regattas (where *the earned cup* travels both ways). **Kanō Jigorō's** (*Hist.* 1860–1938) **Kodokan *jūdō*** enters the physical curriculum of the **Pacific Companies** and county police (favouring leverage and restraint over Atlantic billy-clubs), while **Hawaiian surfing** and Sierra skiing/mountaineering flow west to Shōnan/Kamakura and Hokkaido/the Japanese Alps.

**A3.7. Fashion and dress — reciprocal Pacific wardrobe (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Anchoring Japan's textile and silk role in the **Hayama annex** (`L1622`) in visible everyday style across the Realm:
- **Who loses (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The merchant tailors.* San Francisco's merchant tailors, who sew the frock coat, lose the young engineers to the *haori*. They fight it with a "suit of the Realm" in the 1900s and lose.
  - *Kyoto's weavers.* Nishijin's houses refuse to sell brocade by the bolt to San Francisco cutters who will cut it into evening wraps, and for a decade sell it only as finished kimono. The Japanist critics of `A5.12` quote them.
- **The Pacifican *haori* and indigo *noragi*:** Suited to San Francisco's foggy microclimate, a structured, straight-cut, collarless ***haori* outercoat** (in wool, raw silk, or heavy cotton) becomes the informal signature jacket of Polytechnic engineers, architects, faculty, and urban women. On Mare Island docks, redwood camps, and farms, Japanese ***aizome* indigo *noragi* and *sashiko*-quilted work-jackets** merge with California denim into a distinct Pacific indigo workwear tradition.
- **High-craft Honolulu silk shirts (1910s–1920s):** Adapting the real historical origin of the Aloha shirt (Japanese-Hawaiian tailors in Honolulu sewing collared shirts from Japanese *kimono* silk and *chirimen* crepe in the 1920s–30s, *Hist.*), high-craft **Honolulu *chirimen* silk shirts** in subtle *yūzen* wave and botanical prints emerge a decade early as dignified warm-weather attire across Thalassia, Manila, Los Angeles, and summer San Francisco.
- **Women's *hakama* mobility and San Francisco *shibui* couture:** Borrowing Meiji women students' pleated ***onna-bakama*** (*Hist.*), **tailored *hakama*-cut divided skirts and wide pleated trousers** become standard campus, laboratory, and fieldwork attire for Pacifican women decades before New York accepts trousers on women. Running directly on Kyoto and Yokohama silk, San Francisco develops its own rival to Paris couture—skipping the Victorian hourglass for kimono-draped sleeves, wrapped *obi*-inspired waistbands, **Nishijin-brocade and *yūzen*-silk evening *haori* wraps**, and restrained ***shibui* palettes** (indigo, slate, moss, persimmon, charcoal, pale gold).
- ***Yukata*, *samue*, and practical craft footwear:** Crisp cotton ***yukata*** (in *kasuri* indigo or Southwestern/Hawaiian geometric weaves) and loose ***samue*** replace heavy Victorian wool dressing-gowns across Pacifican homes and Rocky Mountain *onsen* towns. Vulcanized rubber-soled split-toe ***jika-tabi*** work boots (historically invented in Japan c. 1922, *Hist.*) are rapidly adopted by San Francisco steel-frame riggers, redwood carpenters, and Columbia dam builders, alongside woven ***zōri*** sandals, oiled-paper ***wagasa*** umbrellas, and ***furoshiki*** wrapping cloths.

**A3.8. Everyday craft and domestic habits: Pan-Pacific *Mingei*, *genkan*, and *shin-hanga* (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
- **Pan-Pacific *Mingei* (1920s):** Japan's *mingei* folk-craft movement (coined by Yanagi Sōetsu in the 1920s, *Hist.*) links directly with **Pueblo pottery** (the San Ildefonso revival), California stoneware, and Hawaiian *koa* woodturning and *kapa*. Its case is functional, handmade everyday ware against gilded European luxury.
- **The quarrel over the hand (PROPOSED, D-30):**
  - *The aluminium champion.* It sells the Realm's households pressed and anodized aluminium for every use, and the *mingei* men call its wares soulless. The champion answers that its saucepans are honest too, and cheaper. The households buy both.
  - *Who owns the craft.* Yanagi's admiration for Pueblo pottery meets the San Ildefonso potters' own decision to sign their pots and sell them to collectors at collectors' prices (*Hist.* for Maria Martinez's signed pots). The potters choose the market, and the *mingei* purists call it a fall. It is the potters' choice, and the Realm's rule on the nations' affairs keeps it theirs.
- **Domestic and civic habits:** Shoes-off ***genkan* entryways** become standard across Hawaiʻi, California redwood homes, and Rocky Mountain lodges (an immediate everyday marker separating Pacifican households from Eastern American ones). ***Shin-hanga*** woodblock aesthetics shape railway and national-park posters, while durable *washi* paper is used for official Realm charters and county maps. *(Railway lunch boxes and kitchen fusion are `CR-089`'s: the station box is its `A10` item 8.)*

### A4. How the marriage works in everyday life (diaspora, faith, firewalls, intimacy)

**A4.1. The "Letters Home" engine (PROPOSED).**
From the Wakamatsu colony (`L783 ("Wakamatsu colony")`), Hawaii's family convention of 1881 and the renewal of 1907 onward, Japanese immigration overtakes Chinese in the 1900s (`L41 ("Japanese immigration overtakes")`). Because migrants arrive into a Realm with no penal labour contracts, enforceable land titles, county-language grants, and open examinations, remittances, photographs, and Honolulu/San Francisco Japanese newspapers circulate back to Hiroshima, Yamaguchi, Kumamoto, Wakayama, and Kagoshima showing cousins living as citizens, engineers, physicians, and landowners rather than humiliated contract labourers.

**A4.2. Religious reciprocity — Buddhist academies in the Pacifica League (APPROVED direction; institutional form PROPOSED).**
While Pacifica sends no missionaries to Japan (`A1.2`), Japanese **Jōdo Shinshū (Honganji)** temples, Zen centers, and Shinto shrines operate openly across Hawaiʻi and California under the Realm's religious floors. Under the **Pacifica League** rule (`L45`, where churches keep their schools and the state pays only for what it examines), **Bishop Yemyō Imamura** (`CT-YEMYO-IMAMURA`, *Hist.* 1867–1932, Bishop of Honolulu's Honpa Hongwanji Mission from 1900 to 1932) brings **Hongwanji bilingual academies in Hawaiʻi and California into the League's examination rolls** on the exact same footing as Catholic and Methodist colleges—without surrendering Buddhist religious instruction. For Japanese families on both sides of the ocean, seeing a Buddhist academy's graduates enter Carquinez and the civil examinations proves that the Realm's floors hold for them. The Realm's own reason is the examination's: the League pays only for what it examines, and a bilingual academy that sends examined graduates enlarges the pool the Polytechnic draws from. Talent comes from every faith (`CR-034`), and a rule that kept Buddhist schools off the roll would cost the Realm engineers.

**A4.3. Sovereign firewalls protecting Japanese domestic life (canon synthesis).**
Ordinary Japanese accept permanent union in 1932 only because domestic boundaries remain unmistakable:
- The **Tennō never leaves Japan**; Hayama is Japan's court.
- **English has zero domestic official status in Japan** (`L1626`).
- Japan's **family registry (*koseki*), domestic courts, primary schools, Shinto rites, railways, posts, and home army** stay wholly Japanese.
- **No Pacifican sovereign enclaves or bases** exist on Japanese soil (Yokosuka and Hakodate are treaty rights, not ceded territory).

**A4.4. Mixed marriages (*kokusai kekkon*) — accepted in the islands, transgressive on the mainland (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Japan's 1873 *Daijōkan* Decree No. 103 (*Hist.*) legalized international marriage, while the 1898 Civil Code and *koseki* rules strictly regulated foreign spouses (*nyūfu*). Ordinary mixed marriages flourish primarily in **Hawaiʻi** (where Hawaiian, Japanese, Chinese, Filipino, and Californio intermarriage has deep local roots) and California port cities, while remaining rare, socially fraught, and openly criticized in provincial mainland Japan. That unresolved friction is left deliberately intact: it gives real weight to Kiyoshi's 1907 rejection, to Chiyo's delicate standing at both courts, and to why **Kahekili's betrothal to a collateral imperial princess** feels genuinely transgressive to Japanese conservatives (`L1780 ("Restoration officers strike")`).


**A4.5. Shinto: the firewall, and three crossings (PROPOSED, D-31).**
Shinto is the one part of Japanese life the partnership never touches, and that restraint is its chief role in the exchange. There are three crossings, each with its own friction.
- **The firewall.** Shinto rites stay wholly Japanese (`A4.3`). Pacifica proposes nothing about them and sends no priest, and the Treaty of Edo's no-missionaries article (`A1.2`) runs both ways in spirit. In our timeline the Meiji state classed shrine rites as a civic duty and not a religion, barred shrine priests from preaching in 1882 and set up a Shrine Bureau in 1900 (*Hist.*). How that "State Shinto" is built and taught belongs to the domestic-politics CR (`## Hand-offs`).
- **Crossing 1: two sacred genealogies.** The Emperor's line descends from Amaterasu. The Hawaiian aliʻi's lines descend from the gods in the genealogical chants that Kalākaua prints (`L848 ("prints the genealogical chants")`; *Hist.*: the *Kumulipo*, printed in 1889). In 1881 Kalākaua brings both claims into one room (`A3.1`), and Tokyo's court scholars read the chants as Hawaii's *Kojiki*. That recognition is what makes "two sister Pacific monarchies" more than a phrase: each court knows the other's form of sacred kingship. It is also why the Restoration purists find Kahekili's betrothal to an imperial princess intolerable (`A5.3`): it joins two divine lines. Japan's succession law excludes him (`L1258 ("Japan’s succession law excludes him")`), so the law keeps Amaterasu's line closed even where the families join.
- **Crossing 2: the shrines of the Japanese Pacificans, and the shrine question.** Shrines follow the emigrants to Hawaii and California (*Hist.*: Hilo and Honolulu had shrines by the 1900s; dates to verify). The Realm registers a shrine like any church under its floors (`L928 ("registered church and congregation")`). Tokyo's doctrine says shrine rites are not religion but civic duty. The Realm's courts rule on what is done, not on its name: worship before a kami is religion and voluntary, and a citizen of the Realm owes the Emperor no rite. Japan's consuls therefore cannot require the Emperor's portrait and the Rescript on Education in the Realm's schools, as they did in Japanese-language schools abroad in our timeline (*Hist.*, to verify). Many Japanese Pacificans keep the rites, and many do not. Tokyo's Home Ministry and the Restoration call the second group apostates (G29 in `A5`).
- **Crossing 3: places, cleansing and the dead.**
  - *Sacred places.* The Realm's compacts keep Native sacred places closed unless their people consent: the springs (`A3.4`), the four Diné mountains, Hawaii's protected grounds. Japanese visitors recognise in them the shrine forests of home.
  - *Cleansing.* The bath's rule of washing before entering (`A3.4`) sits close to *misogi*, the Shinto cleansing in water, and to Hawaiian cleansing in the sea. Nobody in the Realm makes a religion of it.
  - *The dead.* Japan enshrines Kiyoshi as a war god: "A son of Elias Thorne is now sacred in Japan" (`L1492 ("Japan enshrines Kiyoshi as a war god")`). Canon has Julian bring the sword home, and where it rests is OPEN (`L1492`). In Shinto a war god's sword belongs at his shrine, so the shrine's priests and Kashiwagi's faction want it, and its absence is a small grievance and a bond at once. This CR leaves the sword OPEN for the CR that decides it. In 1932 Kiyotada, a war god's son, takes the common Navy's oath at Thalassia (`A6.4`).
- **What the Realm takes from Shinto:** customs, not belief. Some non-Japanese families in Honolulu and San Francisco make the New Year shrine visit, and a torii stands in Golden Gate Park's Japanese garden (*Hist.* for the garden of 1894, to verify). The Realm's state has no religion and borrows none.

### A5. Japanese grievances: resolved, contained, unresolvable, latent (catalogue APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 16:01, D-24; dispositions PROPOSED)

**A5.1. Hokkaido and the Ainu: mutual institutional influence, 1870s–1932 (APPROVED direction; canon tension `T-AINU-1`; detail PROPOSED).**
- *Starting friction (`L739`, `APPROVED — CR-070`):* Early *Kaitakushi* settlement takes Ainu hunting and salmon grounds without asking, and Sitka's assayers quarrel with Sapporo over it in the 1870s.
- *The 1893–1899 turn:* When Pacifican-assisted cold-hardy paddy begins pushing across the Ishikari and Kamikawa plains from 1893 (`C-1893-HOKKAIDO-RICE`), Pacifica does not lecture Japan about an expansion it is helping to finance. Sitka's reason is practical: the Realm grants no land on a nation's country without its compact (`L739`), its northern fisheries science depends on salmon rivers that are managed and not stripped, and a Hokkaido that feeds itself is a Japan that does not need Manchuria (`A5.10`). Instead, Sitka's Northern Commission, Sapporo Agricultural College under **Satō Shōsuke** (`CT-SATO-SHOSUKE`), Hokkaido officials, and Ainu petitioners from Chikabumi (Asahikawa) and Piratori exchange concrete Alaskan and homeland legal instruments.
- *The 1899 Hokkaido Ainu Settlement & Fisheries Act (`C-1899-HOKKAIDO-AINU`, PROPOSED):* Rather than OTL's coercive *Hokkaido Former Aborigines Protection Act* (which allotted rocky individual 5-*chō* plots forfeited if uncleared, outlawed river salmon fishing, and banned the Ainu language and *iomante*, *Hist.*), the Imperial Diet passes a reform crafted strictly within **unitary Japanese domestic law** (no separate foreign-treaty sovereignty, respecting *koseki*):
  1. **Inalienable collective *kotan* village trusts:** Registered village lands in Chikabumi, Piratori, and coastal districts cannot be alienated to speculators or confiscated for non-paddy use, averting OTL's 35-year Chikabumi dispossession scandal (*Hist.*).
  2. **Statutory salmon-river and coastal *kombu* shares:** Mirroring Alaska's Sitka rents and Kodiak conservation quotas (`CR-070`), Ainu villages hold protected river-salmon catch shares and coastal kelp rights. In 1932 their kombu trusts are among the "Hokkaido's kombu growers" who sign the marine-agronomy compact (`L1704`); the compact, its parties and its terms are unchanged (`CR-085`; `CR-089` `A2`).
  3. **Voluntary paddy and bilingual schools:** Households choosing Kamikawa paddy receive irrigated allotments and Sapporo training; village schools teach Japanese alongside Ainu oral literature rather than erasing it, enabling **Chiri Yukie** (*Hist.* 1903–1922, *Ainu Shinyōshū*) and **Iboshi Hokuto** (*Hist.* 1901–1929) to emerge as voices of a living bilingual Ainu literary revival with transpacific readership.
- *Residual limits (unresolved grit):* The 1870s–92 dispossession of the lower Ishikari is not undone, Wajin settlers vastly outnumber Ainu across Hokkaido, and Tokyo *kokutai* hardliners resent "Sitka-style special village status" written into Japanese law.

**A5.2. Returnees, *kokusui hozon*, and Sōseki's *gaihatsuteki kaika* (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
Even with a respectful partner, Pacifica's speed and scale produce genuine cultural vertigo:
- **1880s–90s *kokusui hozon* ("preservation of national essence"):** Thinkers like **Miyake Setsurei** (*Hist.* 1860–1945) and the *Seikyōsha* (*Nihonjin*, 1888, *Hist.*) resist uncritical assimilation. Because Pacifica never had consular courts and ended its mixed panels in 1870 without demanding interior landownership (`A1.3`), and although the Atlantic powers still make mixed residence their price in 1894–99, *kokusui hozon* stays primarily a vigorous cultural-preservation movement (craft, architecture, folklore) rather than pure anti-foreign hysteria—yet it constantly polices the border against over-eager imitation.
- **The returnee (*yōkō-gaeri*) and *moga* critique (1900s–1920s):** **Natsume Sōseki** (*Hist.* 1867–1916) gives enduring intellectual voice to the anxiety of *gaihatsuteki kaika* ("externally driven civilization", *Hist.*) — warning that chasing Carquinez's examination metrics, Honolulu cinema fashions (*moga*), and English engineering jargon risks hollowing out Japanese inner life. Home-trained bureaucrats and provincial elders resent fluent **"Carquinez returnees"** who leap ahead in ministries, shipyards, and trading houses.
- *Why it cannot be fully resolved:* Living beside a richer, faster partner inherently pulls urban youth outward. The tension stays alive on the page as the permanent cultural cost of modernization.

**A5.3. Mixed marriages and dynastic anxiety (APPROVED direction).**
As framed in `A4.4`, ordinary intermarriage remains socially contested in mainland Japan, and **Kahekili's betrothal** moves that unresolved anxiety to the foot of the Chrysanthemum Throne—handing Restoration purists a grievance no legal text can erase (`L1780`).

**A5.4. Decentralized modernity and *nōhon-shugi*: why small villages are cushioned (APPROVED direction; detail PROPOSED).**
In OTL, coal-and-steam centralization, colonial Korean rice dumping, and raw-silk collapse devastated rural Japan in the 1920s–30s, turning **Gondō Seikyō's** (`CT-GONDO-SEIKYO`, *Hist.* 1868–1937) agrarian fundamentalism (*nōhon-shugi*, *Hist.*) into fuel for mass rural despair and right-wing terrorism. In Pacifica's timeline, three converging mechanisms sustain a **decentralized rural-industrial path**:
1. **Early rural hydro-electrification (1890s–1910s):** Japan's mountainous rivers paired with **Pacifica's standardized AC hydro equipment** (Tesla/Ryan standard, `CR-052`, `CR-054`) bring regional hydro grids to Japanese prefectures a decade early. Utilities sell surplus **daytime power** cheaply to townships (*Hist.* for Japan's daytime rural-power model).
2. ***Sangyō Kumiai* cooperatives + fractional-horsepower electric workshops:** Following Japan's **1900 Industrial Cooperative Law** (*Hist.*), village *Sangyō Kumiai* pool credit to sign bulk electricity contracts, run electric centrifugal irrigation pumps and community rice mills, and put **small electric motors** into family workshops (weaving, silk-reeling, noodles, ceramics, woodworking, small machine parts). Light industry stays anchored in market towns rather than forcing every rural son and daughter into urban slums.
3. **Protected rice + domestic nitrogen + wireless weather (`CR-085`, `CR-078`):** With Korea independent (no colonial rice dumping breaking farm prices, `L1704`), affordable ammonium sulfate from hydro plants, and Okada's wireless typhoon/frost warnings, rural tenant debt remains far shallower than in OTL.
- *What remains unresolved:* The **1930–32 Atlantic Union depression** still collapses US raw-silk buying (`CR-085`), and Reserve Bank gold parity squeezes Nagano, Gunma, and Tōhoku silk villages hard enough to give Gondō's critique and the 15 May 1932 officers genuine moral purchase—**yet** rural stability explains why the countryside does not rise in support of the coup and why Takahashi's July 1932 yen reset calms the silk belt.

**A5.5. Sovereign dignity and the "Thalassia bakufu" charge (canon synthesis — unresolvable in principle).**

### A6. Identity profile: Kuroda Kiyotada (`CT-KURODA-SON`), Common Navy Minister

*Resolves `CR-085`'s open item (`L2047 ("the given name and the descent of Kiyoshi's son")`, `L1794`): the given name, descent, career, and cultural meaning of Kiyoshi's son in the treaty-law Navy seat from 1 August 1932.*

**A6.1. Identity, birth, and genealogy (name APPROVED; biographical dates PROPOSED).**
- **Name:** **Kuroda Kiyotada (黒田清忠)** (*"clear loyalty"*; continuing the house's *Kiyo-* character: Kiyotaka → Kiyoshi → Kiyotada; Kiyotaka's own son Kiyonaka, heir of the main house, is of Kiyoshi's generation, `L989`). Registry ID: existing `CT-KURODA-SON`.
- **Birth & descent:** Born **c. 1888** (two years younger than his half-uncle Julian, born 1886) to **Count Kuroda Kiyoshi** (`CT-KIYOSHI`, Elias Thorne × Uehara Hisa) and **Hyaku**, Kiyotaka's adopted daughter, whom Kiyoshi married on entering the house in 1886 (`L989 ("Kiyoshi's bride is Kiyotaka's adopted daughter Hyaku")`). Three-quarters Japanese, one-quarter Elias Thorne; **2nd Count Kuroda** (branch house) upon his father's death in May 1920. Kinsman-by-marriage to **Chiyo** (`CT-CHIYO`) and first cousin to Silas's younger daughter (married to Tanabe Makoto in 1909, `CR-022`).

**A6.2. Formation and the 1907 lesson (PROPOSED).**
Raised bilingual between Tokyo/Kagoshima and Pearl Harbor, Kiyotada graduates from the **Imperial Japanese Naval Academy at Etajima** and completes naval engineering and gunnery courses at **Carquinez**. At **nineteen (1907)** he watches his father petition for Pacifica's crown (`L1248`) and get turned back by *both* capitals (Saionji refusing a Japanese count's elevation; Hawaiʻi and Thalassia standing for Julian). That failure fixes his lifelong constitutional discipline: **dual loyalty survives only when you guard the boundary between the two crowns.**

**A6.3. Kronstadt (1920) and the Tokyo intelligence crucible (canon synthesis + PROPOSED detail).**
At **thirty-two (May 1920)** Kiyotada inherits his father's branch countship and the crushing weight of his father's enshrinement as a Japanese *gunshin* (war god) after Kronstadt. Posted to the **Navy General Staff intelligence division in Tokyo** (`CR-045`), he becomes **Maria Grishina-Almazova's reader** from 1920 (`L65`). When Maria marries **Commander Kashiwagi**—a destroyer officer who served *under Kiyoshi at Kronstadt* and came home convinced Japanese sailors died there for a Pacifican's hostages—Kiyotada quietly reads reports on his own father's embittered subordinates and the British naval attaché, forwarding them to Thalassia. Per `CR-046`, **he never learns that British gold paid the Chekists on Kronstadt in his father's last weeks.**

**A6.4. May to August 1932, and what his descent means in the Navy seat (APPROVED seat & loyalty; cultural meaning PROPOSED).**
At **forty-four**, commanding Japanese units of the western squadron on **15 May 1932**, Kiyotada receives Maria's warning of which ships and which day (`CR-045`) and holds the Japanese fleet units loyal in his father's name (`L1780`). Appointed **Common Navy Minister** by Julian with Saitō's countersignature effective **1 August 1932** (`L1794`), his first official act is **presiding over the dissolution of his own father's reserved western squadron** into one consolidated Concord command.

## Appendix A — Rules of *Shōkin-jan* (正金雀 — Specie Mahjong)

*Playable high-stakes Pacifican Riichi variant (PROPOSED mechanics; original to Pacifica, playable with any standard 136-tile set).*

1. **The Two Currencies (50% Gold Cover):** Each player starts a *hanchan* with **30,000 points**: **20,000 in Gold Sticks (*Shōkin*)** (brass/gilt) and **10,000 in Paper Notes (*Tegata*)** (thin bamboo/ivory slips). Ordinary wins may be paid in Paper Notes first, then Gold; **Gold-backed wins and Bank Run penalties must be settled in Gold first**. Players may borrow extra Paper Notes from the Center Treasury ("Discounting Bills") on their turn, but any Paper Notes held above a player's Gold Sticks (breaking 50% cover) bleed **1,000 points per 2,000 excess** to the Center Pot at hand-end.
2. **Gold *Riichi* vs. Paper *Riichi*:**
   - **Gold *Riichi* (*Shōkin Riichi*, 1,000 Gold):** Standard legal *tenpai* only, hand locked (*tsumogiri* only). Optional **Open Assay (*Kōkai*)**: flip **two tiles of your own choice** face-up on declaration for **+2 *han*** and Gold-first payout.
   - **Paper *Riichi* (*Tegata Riichi*, 1,000–3,000 Paper Notes, +1 *han* per note):** May be declared **even while *noten* (unbacked)**—or on a genuine monster hand as bait. Permits **up to two hand-swaps (*tedashi*)** after declaration to scramble into *tenpai* or upgrade a wait. Reaching exhaustive draw (*ryūkyoku*) still *noten* triggers instant **Insolvency (*Tōsan*)**: pay *mangan* (8,000 Gold; 12,000 if dealer) split among the standing players.
3. **Soft Assay (*Kantei*, 1,000 Gold):** Before drawing, any opponent facing an active Paper *Riichi* may toss a **1,000 Gold Assay Stick** into the pot to force the declarer to flip **two tiles of the declarer's own choice** face-up (declarer keeps the 1,000 Gold if they win or survive the hand). Every shown tile is true, yet the declarer chooses which truth to weaponize.
4. **Calling Cover (*Shōkin Seikyū* — The Bank Run):** Before drawing, any opponent facing a Paper *Riichi* may slam the felt and call ***"Shōkin!"* ("Cover!")**. The round ends immediately and the declarer lays all 13 tiles flat:
   - **Insolvent (*noten*):** Declarer forfeits all Paper Notes on the felt and pays **8,000 Gold (12,000 if dealer)** directly to the caller.
   - **Solvent (*tenpai*):** Caller pays the **full leveraged value of the declarer's hand in Gold (minimum *mangan*)** directly to the declarer—as if dealing straight into *ron*.
5. **The Discount Window (Rescue Loan or Refusal to Print):** Hitting zero Gold Sticks does not automatically end the match. Any rival at the table may offer a **private Gold rescue loan** on explicit table terms (repayment with interest plus a binding condition, such as a bar on Paper *Riichi* for the rest of South round)—or all three rivals may **Refuse to Print (*Hakkō Kyohi*)**, liquidating the player out of the *hanchan*.
