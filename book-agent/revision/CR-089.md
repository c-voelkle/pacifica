## A. The change

### A1. Work 4: why the Realm eats leaner, and how the Office prints it (two columns APPROVED, 24 November 2026, 21:40; happenstance APPROVED as direction, 25 November 2026, 06:58; the particulars PROPOSED)

**Nobody plans the Realm's table.** No ministry recommends a diet, no school teaches one, and the Office of Public Measures, which exists to measure (`L939 ("The Office of Public Measures, established in 1888")`), measures this as it measures everything else and says nothing about it. The table comes out leaner than the Union's for four reasons, none of them a policy (PROPOSED):
1. **The ground.** The Realm has a long, cold-current coast where fish and shellfish are cheap, a Mediterranean climate where vegetables, fruit, olives and vines grow most of the year, rice soils in the Sacramento Valley and the Delta, and open range for cattle and sheep that never enters the cropland account (`CR-082`). It has no corn belt. In the Union, maize fed to hogs makes pork and lard the cheapest food on the table; in the Realm they are dearer, and fish, fruit and rice are cheaper.
2. **Who came.** By 1932 about three in ten of the Realm's residents are of Chinese, Japanese or Filipino origin (`population/outputs/workbook_changes.md`: about 4.8 million Chinese-origin, 5.8 million Japanese-origin and 1.0 million Filipino-origin, MODEL), beside Californios and Hispanos, Hawaiians, and Mediterranean and Atlantic Europeans: Genoese, Basques, Portuguese, Dalmatians, French. Most of them came from tables built on grain, beans, vegetables and fish, and a cook keeps the habits of her mother's kitchen when the prices let her.
3. **Prices.** Households follow them. When fish is cheaper than salt pork, fish wins, and the men who lose are real: the Realm's lard renderers and hog feeders, who sell less every decade from the 1890s and blame the fish canners, the rice importers and the Chinese, in that order.
4. **Fashion.** The kitchens that win the Realm's contests (`A4`) prize the season, the sea and the garden, and, through the French school, butter (`A5`). What the best restaurants cook, the halls and the households copy a decade later, so fashion makes the table leaner in grain and meat and richer in dairy at the same time.

**When it happened.** The table thins over two generations, not at once. In the 1880s Pacificans eat much as the Union's West does. By the late 1920s the Office's household budget survey (PROPOSED: 1925–27, the counties' first sample of what households buy and eat) shows a table using about a fifth less crop land a head than the constant figure of 2.1 acres (`diet_saving = 0.22`, MODEL). The Office's earlier tables were not wrong by its own rule: they held the diet constant because no survey measured it in land. The Office's household inquiries from the 1890s counted what families spent, not what their food took to grow, and only the survey of 1925–27 weighed the food and converted it (`L1030 ("Powell's office reports")`, `CR-082` F-2). With hindsight the margins of 1890–1930 were somewhat wider than printed. The Office says so in one footnote to the 1930 table and does not recompute the old ones. This stays narrative: the model's rows to 1930 are on 2.1 acres, as `CR-082` built them (`## D`).

**Why the Office keeps the first column: butter (APPROVED as the reason, 07:30; the particulars PROPOSED).** The survey of 1925–27 catches two movements going opposite ways. Meat a head is falling. Dairy is rising faster than anything else the households buy: butter and cream from the French school's kitchens (`A5`), cheese, fresh milk on the refrigerated trains, ice cream. Dairy is the most land-hungry food the Realm eats, because its cows eat irrigated alfalfa and grain. The gap between the columns is about 0.46 standard acres a head (2.1 less 1.638, MODEL), and the Office's note to the table of 1930 says plainly that that much more dairy land a head would bring the Realm back to the first column, and that the survey's dairy series is climbing fast enough to close it within a generation. So the first column is not an Atlantic table the Realm never ate. It is the table the Realm may yet eat, and the Office keeps it as its measure until the census of 1940 shows which way the butter went. How much butter, cream and milk a head that gap stands for is not computed here (OPEN; it needs a dairy line in `provisioning.py`, which this CR does not add).

The second column's figures were checked by re-running `population/provisioning.py` on `main` at 1.638 acres a head (25 November 2026; the run was not committed). The first column's are those already in `population/outputs/provisioning.md`.

**What the columns do to the argument of 1930–32 (PROPOSED).** The provisioners, Hoover first, cite the second column in the papers: on the Realm's real table the century needs three-fifths of the sellers' trade, not three-quarters. The sufficers answer from the first, which remains the measure, and ask what the butter will do to the second. Their case in 1932 (`L1910 ("three-quarters or more")`) and Mead's (the year the margin closes, a law made in surplus binding a successor in want) stand on the Office's own first column, and the second does not save the provisioners from them. Even the second column needs three-fifths of every major seller's surplus. A thriftier table is a reason the plan is less mad, not a reason it is safe. That keeps the irony of Article II whole: the Realm needs the sellers so badly that a promise never to withhold is what makes them go on selling.

### A2. Work 5: the farmed sea on two lines (two lines APPROVED, 24 November 2026, 21:40; their reading PROPOSED)

The century's plan of 1930 already counts the farmed sea on `CR-082`'s whole-diet figure (`L1720`; `population/outputs/provisioning.md` §8). The supplement to the plan laid before the Senate's committee in July 1932 with the kelp compact is the first paper to print it on two lines (PROPOSED). Both lines are projections of a sea not yet farmed; the diet tables of 1932 are unchanged, so `L1722 ("Japan's rice keeps pace")`, where the compact "changes nothing in the diet tables of 1932", stands as written. The supplement reports the sea twice:
- **Line 1, whole diet.** `CR-082`'s figure: a dry tonne of kelp counts as 0.3 tonnes of grain-equivalent, "food, feed and the fertilizer it saves together", with shellfish grown among the kelp folded in. Central case 50,000 km², about 50 million dry tonnes, 14.3 million mouths (low 5.7, high 28.6; MODEL). This is the line the supplement sets beside the grain balance, and the line the table of 1940 would carry if the sea were farmed by then.
- **Line 2, protein and what it displaces.** The same tonnage read another way: about 5 million tonnes of crude protein a year in the central case (MODEL, `CR-082` Part F), and the feed grain and potash that kelp meal replaces. Line 2 is not added to Line 1 or to anything else. It exists so that a reader can see why the sea is worth farming although it grows no bread.
- **What neither line counts.** The wild fisheries (held at their present catch and never projected upward to close a gap), the Hawaiian fishponds, abalone and the inshore cooperatives are outside the 50,000 km² and outside both lines. They matter to the table (`A3`) and not to the century's arithmetic.

**The kelp compact is unchanged.** `C-1932-KELP-RESEARCH` stays what `CR-085` made it, a research compact between Hokkaido Imperial University, Hokkaido's kombu growers and the Realm's marine stations. The domestic cooperatives at Point Alones and China Camp are licensed under the members' fishing law and, after 1887, the counties'; the fishponds are under the Hawaiian Crown. Neither is a party to the compact.

### A3. The regional kitchens (direction APPROVED, 06:58; the map PROPOSED)

The Realm's cooking is not one cuisine with variations. It is a dozen regional kitchens, and inside the cities neighbourhood kitchens, which know one another, borrow from one another and compete. A cook from Monterey and a cook from Boise share a language and a coin and little else at the stove. The map below is the drafter's; each entry names what the place has that the others lack.

- **San Francisco, the stakes city.** The French colony of the Gold Rush gives the city its first restaurants of rank (*Hist.*). North Beach cooks Genoese and Ligurian fish stews (cioppino is its child, *Hist.*); Chinatown is the Realm's first Cantonese kitchen (`A12`); the Mission keeps Californio cooking; after the earthquake of 1906, which canon has (`L1377 ("the city he held together after the earthquake")`), the Japanese quarter moves to the Western Addition, as it did in our timeline (PROPOSED). The hotel kitchens, Victor Hirtzler's at the St Francis from 1904 among them (*Hist.*, background), are where the city's cooks learn the French brigade. Every serious cook in the Realm wants to have cooked here and to have beaten someone here.
- **The Central Coast and Monterey.** Live fire, and Monterey Jack from the 1880s (*Hist.*). The rancho barbecue of the Santa Maria valley, beef over red oak, is a Californio tradition (*Hist.*). At Monterey the sea: Point Alones's Chinese squid and abalone fishery from the 1850s (*Hist.*), Kodani's divers at Point Lobos, some ten kilometres down the coast, from 1897 (*Hist.*), the sardine canneries (*Hist.*), the abalone steak (*Hist.*: Pop Ernest's, about 1910, background). Here, without the bans and without the fire of 1906, Point Alones survives as a licensed cooperative.
- **Los Angeles and the south.** Citrus, chile, the Plaza's Mexican kitchens, the irrigated south of the valley. A Chinese-Mexican borderland cooking runs from here to Sonora: wok-seared beef with tomato, chile, soy and vinegar over potatoes or rice (PROPOSED, the Realm's parallel to Lima's Chinese-Peruvian *lomo saltado*, *Hist.*).
- **Napa, Sonoma and the Russian River.** Wine and its labels (`A6`); the Swiss-Italian dairies of Marin and Sonoma.
- **The San Joaquin Valley.** Raisins and figs at Fresno, the Armenian and Japanese vineyard families, Bakersfield's Basque hotels (*Hist.*), the cotton and the cattle.
- **The Delta and the Sacramento Valley.** The Delta's levees were raised by Chinese crews in the 1860s and 1870s (*Hist.*), and its first potato fortune was Chin Lung's before it was George Shima's (*Hist.*, Chin Lung as background). Chinese and Japanese farm cooking, asparagus, celery, pears, short-grain rice on the alkali soils (*Hist.* for Biggs, 1908–12; here a decade or two earlier with Hilgard, PROPOSED). This is also the ground of the Feather River case of 1886 (`L924 ("a night mob on the Feather")`): the valley where Chinese families bought their district farms and were burned out of their packing sheds. Its kitchens are contested from the start (see below).
- **The Columbia and the Willamette.** Salmon, halibut, Dungeness crab, berries, orchards; Portland's Chinatown, one of the coast's largest (*Hist.*); Ah Bing's cherry rows at Milwaukie.
- **The interior: Deseret, Idaho, Washoe, western Montana.** Range beef and lamb, sourdough, mountain trout. The Basque boarding houses of Boise and Winnemucca, with their long tables and set courses for sheepherders (*Hist.*), are the working man's multi-course dinner. Deseret keeps the Saints' habit of storing a year's food (*Hist.* for the later doctrine; PROPOSED as a habit before 1900), and with it the Realm's strongest tradition of preserving, curing and bottling.
- **New Mexico, Arizona and the Diné land.** Pueblo and Hispano cooking, chile, blue corn, piñon, Diné mutton stew from churro sheep (*Hist.*), Sonoran wheat tortillas. The Filipino families of Arizona and New Mexico (`L785 ("Filipino reformers and families begin arriving after 1876")`, itself PROPOSED in canon) add vinegar and garlic.
- **Hawaii.** Kalo and poi, the fishponds, reef fish, the plantation camps' shared lunch, saimin, manapua; Chinese rice on old taro land, Hawaii's second crop after sugar in the 1880s (*Hist.*); Portuguese sausage and sweet bread from the Madeiran and Azorean families who come from 1878 (*Hist.*). The fishpond keepers sell to whom they choose, and the Crown's ponds refuse the Honolulu hotels in the years the hotels will not pay the ponds' price (PROPOSED).
- **Manila, under its own crown.** A Spanish-Filipino kitchen: adobo, escabeche, lechón, kinilaw. It belongs to the Philippine crown's ledger and is the Pacific's Spanish capital. Its cooks at Thalassia refuse the French brigade's stations and cook the crown's own dinners in their own way (PROPOSED).

**The quarrel over rice (PROPOSED).** The Union's labour leaders argue in our timeline that rice-eating labour undercuts meat-eating labour (*Hist.*: the American Federation of Labor's pamphlet "Meat vs. Rice", 1902). In Pacifica the same grievance belongs to the settlers of the interior in the 1880s: the Feather River case is about land, but its language is about the table, and the sheds that burn are packing sheds. Canon's answer to the settlers is not suppression but arenas in which their virtues count (`CR-071` H6.4), and the kitchen is one of them. The interior's range beef, lamb, sourdough and smoked trout get the same standing as the coast's fish and rice: their own contests at the county fairs, their own brands and places on the label (`A6`), their own masters (`A4`). The Office's second column (`A1`) is never a lecture to the settler about what he eats; the Realm's beef stays the Realm's pride. The fair answers the settler's pride; it does not end the fight over land and schools, which `CR-071` H6.4 leaves to the nativists of 1906 and the county oligarchies of 1910–11 (`L1226 ("Silas's nativists raise a school-segregation crisis in 1906")`). The judging table is a second front, not a peace. The men who burned the sheds on the Feather are in the crowd at the Sacramento fair; the families they put on the barge must decide each year whether to cook in front of them (`C5`), and the blind tasting is never quite blind, because everyone knows whose kitchen roasts the duck that way.

**Neighbourhoods.** Inside each city the rivalry is local before it is regional: North Beach against Chinatown, Sam Yup's banquet houses against Sze Yup's country cooking inside Chinatown itself (`A12`), Honolulu's Chinatown against the plantation towns, Sacramento's Japantown against its hotel kitchens. The press estate's restaurant lists, from the 1890s (PROPOSED; the estate is chartered in 1887), rank houses by neighbourhood and by kitchen, and a house that falls in the list loses its customers within a season (`A12`).

### A4. The cooks' agon (direction APPROVED, 06:58; the master's title APPROVED, 11:43; the rest PROPOSED)

Cooking in the Realm is a contest with published results, as engineering is. It is not a civil one.

- **The kitchens are rough.** A professional kitchen in the Realm is hot, fast, foul-mouthed and full of contempt. Every brigade despises the next: the French-trained hotel cooks call the Cantonese houses' Western food a swindle and the Japanese cafés a joke; the Cantonese cooks call the Japanese thieves of other men's dishes; the Basque and Californio fire cooks call all of them cooks of sauces; the Japanese call the French slow; everyone calls the ranch and camp cooks, most of them Chinese (`A12`), nothing at all. Commis are hazed, burned and sacked, stations are fought over, rivals' stocks are spoiled the night before a proving, and recipes are stolen as a matter of course. Drink is forbidden on the line by Escoffier's rule and by the Realm's (`L889 ("The cup belongs to a thing done")`; never by day). The licit cups of the trade are the guild's dinner, the night of a proving and the toast at a state dinner, poured by others for the occasion. Many brigades drink together after service anyway, and that is the trade's known breach: the Realm reads a brigade that drinks every night as men in trouble, a hotel sacks a cook it finds with drink in him at the next service, and a hall's examined cook loses the post. The blind tests, the posted scores, the open purchase books and the sealed deposits exist because nobody in a kitchen trusts anyone else, not because cooks are virtuous. The Realm's transparency is the cooks' distrust made into rules.
- **Abroad, on the Meiji model.** From the 1880s the hotel houses, the railways' dining-car companies, the steamship lines and the cooks' own guilds send their best young cooks abroad on bonded fellowships, the trade's copy of the Polytechnic's travelling fellowships of 1854 (`L335 ("a meal that names the teacher")`). They go to Escoffier's kitchens (*Hist.*: the Savoy, 1890–98; the Ritz in Paris, 1898–99; the Carlton in London, 1899–1920; Escoffier as background), to Lyon, to the Basque gastronomic societies of San Sebastián (*Hist.*, to verify), to Bologna, to Canton and Hong Kong, and to the kaiseki kitchens of Kyoto. They come home bound to cook at home for five years and to teach. The employers and guilds pay; the state pays for none of it.
- **Akiyama Tokuzō** (CT-AKIYAMA-TOKUZO, 1888–1974, *Hist.*) is the Japanese case of the same model and the Realm's measure of itself abroad. In our timeline he goes to Europe in 1909, works in the Majestic, the Café de Paris and the Ritz kitchen Escoffier had built (the usual account says under Escoffier; to verify, since Escoffier was at the Carlton from 1899), has to fight in the kitchen against contempt for a Japanese cook, and returns in 1913 to be the Emperor's chef (*Hist.*). PROPOSED: in the Ritz kitchen his fellows include Pacifican cooks on their guild fellowships, two kinds of foreigner in a French brigade, rivals before they are allies; he goes home in 1913 by the Pacific route through San Francisco. From then on the Imperial Household's kitchen and the Realm's best kitchens measure themselves against each other, by the menus printed at state dinners (*Hist.* for the imperial menu cards).
- **Technique as engineering.** The Realm's kitchens take Escoffier's codification (*Le Guide culinaire*, 1903, *Hist.*) and Yuan Mei's rules (`A12`) the way the Polytechnic takes a method: they write it down, weigh it and test it. Recipes are by weight, ovens by thermometer; the county grids carry electric cooking to almost every home by 1929 (`L81 ("county grids carry electric cooking")`). The work the author names is the work of the best kitchens: stocks that draw the savour out of bones, shells and kelp; emulsions that bind it with butter and cream; laminated pastry; long braises; contrasts of texture. The guild fellows of the 1890s bring Escoffier's brigade home (*Hist.*: the Savoy, 1890: stations, order to table in minutes, no shouting at the pass and no drink). The Realm's professional kitchens are electric, cooler and above ground; the shouting stays.
- **Ikeda Kikunae** (CT-IKEDA-KIKUNAE, 1864–1936, *Hist.*) isolates glutamate from kombu at Tokyo and names its taste umami in 1908 (*Hist.*). The Realm's chemists and cooks read the paper at once. PROPOSED: it starts the kitchen's longest quarrel, "the powder or the pot". One school puts the crystalline seasoning in everything because it is honest chemistry. The other refuses it because a cook should draw the savour out of the ingredient by technique, and holds that a stock is only proved by tasting. A blind tasting at Carquinez in about 1912, Ikeda present, splits the jury and is reported in every paper (`C4`). The pot keeps the prizes. The powder wins the market: by the 1920s the county halls, the railway kitchens and most eating houses season with it, because it is cheap and a hall cook has three hundred plates to send, and the pot school's masters lose the halls' kitchens they had trained. From 1925 the powder itself is two powders, Japan's and Wu Yunchu's (`A12`), and the fight is three-sided.
- **The master's title (APPROVED, 11:43).** The master cook's title is a Polytechnic trade test like any other trade's, set and marked by the Polytechnic with candidates known by number (`L857 ("it cannot pass one")`: a guild may recommend a candidate and cannot pass one) and, from the congress's charter in 1895, registered by it (`L1082 ("the register of tradesmen who have passed the Polytechnic's trade tests")`; `L51 ("the trades congress of 1895")`). The test has a written paper on method, a practical day in the Polytechnic's test kitchen and a set dinner tasted blind by examiners from outside the candidate's county; its syllabus takes Escoffier's *Guide* and Yuan Mei's *Recipes* as its two books (PROPOSED). The test is first set in 1895; before it a master's title was the guild's alone. Most candidates fail at least once; a failed candidate goes back to the line as a commis or a chef de partie, and the result is posted by number with everyone else's. Cooks learn in kitchens, by paid apprenticeship under the labour article; no school teaches cookery.
- **The guild's dinner (PROPOSED).** On top of the trade test the cooks' guilds keep their own honour: a blind dinner before the guild's masters, after which the others fill the new master's cup (`L889`). It confers no qualification. It is also where the trade's prejudice lives, because a guild may refuse to hold the dinner for a candidate it dislikes, and until the 1900s the French-led guilds of San Francisco refuse it to Chinese masters who have passed the Polytechnic's test. The Cantonese cooks found their own guild and hold their own dinner (`A12`). France's own examined title comes in 1924 (*Hist.*, background).
- **Against the world.** The Realm's wines and cooks go to the great exhibitions. California wines took medals at Paris in our timeline too (*Hist.*, to verify which and when). A Pacifican kitchen measures its standing by what Paris, Tokyo, Canton and San Sebastián say of it; Paris mostly says nothing until the 1900s.

### A5. The French and Spanish schools in the Realm (direction APPROVED, 06:58; particulars PROPOSED)

Two schools of technique set the tone of the Realm's best cooking, and both find ground here that suits them.

- **The French school: codified technique and the formal dinner.** Stocks, mother sauces and their emulsions, pastry, the brigade, the set sequence of courses. It is the language of the hotel kitchens, of the trade test and of state dinners. Butter and cream begin at this table and do not stay there. From the 1900s the bakeries, the eating halls and then the households take them up with the method: laminated pastry in every town, cream sauces on the hall's Sunday plate, butter where the Californio kitchen used lard and the Japanese kitchen used nothing. The Realm's butter comes from dairies on irrigated alfalfa, with wild kelp meal in some herds' feed, cut by hand before the wartime harvest at San Diego and by machine after it (`L1722`), and it costs what that land costs; the better the cooks, the more of it the Realm eats (`A1`).
- **The Spanish and Mediterranean school: honest ingredients, live fire, olive oil, time.** Little intervention: the best fish of the season, grilled; meat over oak; vegetables at their peak; olive oil in quantity, from the mission olives and the California groves of the 1870s onward (*Hist.*). New World staples (maize, beans, chile, tomato, potato) beside Old World curing. Time is the main ingredient: hams cured for two years, chorizo, Californio carne seca, Basque and Portuguese sausages, Japanese pickles, Chinese dried shellfish and cured pork, Deseret's bottled fruit. Yuan Mei's rule that four parts in ten of a fine meal are the buyer's, not the cook's (`A12`), is this school's Chinese ancestor.
- **Oak-savanna hams (PROPOSED).** The blue-oak and valley-oak savannas of the Coast Ranges and the Sierra foothills fatten hogs on acorns in autumn, as the Iberian dehesa does. The Realm's cured hams come from range that never enters the cropland account. They are among the first goods sold under a growers' brand that states the field and the curing time (`A6`).
- **Lean, and not safely so.** The Spanish school's everyday base of fish, oil, beans, vegetables and fruit is the leanest table in Europe and the main reason for the second column. The French school's butter is the main reason the Office will not yet trust it. The two schools pull the Realm's table in opposite directions, and the columns print the contest.

### A6. The Truth of Labels Act (`T-MARK-1` Case C APPROVED, 13:36; the name and date and the particulars PROPOSED)

The Realm does not borrow France's marks of origin. It protects the truth of what a label says and lets reputation be earned in public.

- **The act.** The Truth of Labels Act of 1897 (name and date PROPOSED) extends to every good the rule of the drink label of the members' acts of 1866–67 and the Spirits Act of 1888, that a label says only what is true (`CR-076` A4; `L669 ("the assay standard")`, the Assay Office's standard as the measure): a label is a statement, and a false statement about a good is fraud. Every statement on a label must be true and testable: place, grower or maker, year, grape or breed, content, method, age, and for stored food the date it went into store (`A9` item 2).
- **Place to the field.** A label may name its origin as finely as the maker likes (the county, the valley, the ranch, the vineyard, the slope), and every word must be true of all of it. No fruit from outside may go under a place-name (*Hist.* for contrast: the Union's later viticultural areas allow 15%).
- **No district belongs to anyone.** Every grower in Napa may write "Napa", and the grower over the ridge writes his own county's name. Place-names mean what public maps already say: a county's name means its lines; a valley's or river's name means its watershed as drawn on the land and water office's maps, since Powell's districts are drawn by watershed (`L703`); a ranch, vineyard or slope means what the maker's title deed shows. The fights are over whether a statement is true, decided by the courts on complaint against that map, and nobody gains a right by winning one: about 1905 a Pope Valley grower who writes "Napa Valley" on wine from the next watershed loses his case and his stock (PROPOSED).
- **Who enforces it.** The courts, on complaint. The State Assay Office, the same that exposed the diamond field in 1872 (`L99 ("The State Assay Office exposes the diamond field")`), tests wines, oils, hams, sauces and cheeses on complaint and publishes what it finds. There is no subsidy, no quota and no inspector in the cellar beyond the gaugers at the licensed stills (`CR-076`), so a false label lasts until a rival or a customer complains.
- **The register of results.** The Office prints, county by county, the scores of the fairs' blind juries and of the press estate's tasters beside each grower's or maker's name, place and year. On a producer's request the Assay Office prints the measures of a lot: sugar at harvest, acidity, age in wood, additives (*Hist.* for the idea of measured grades: Germany's must-weight classes, fixed in law only in 1971). A name earns its price each year in public, and a famous valley's weak grower sells at what the register shows of him.
- **Brands are private.** Growers who want a shared standard form a brand with a private rule, as any trader may, and register it as a trademark: the oak-savanna ham growers' brand (`A5`), the Delta rice growers', the Kona cooperative's, which sits inside the Hawaiian Crown's own label law (`L1038 ("Coffee is the working day's drink")`). A brand competes with other brands and with single growers, and the law gives it nothing a trademark does not give.
- **No borrowed names.** In our timeline California sold "Burgundy", "Sauterne" and "Champagne" (*Hist.*). Under the act a Napa wine called "Burgundy" makes a false statement of place. The shippers who built their trade on the Union's taste for French names lose it: the Union's buyers want "Burgundy", not Napa, and for a decade the Realm's bulk wine sells for less in New York than the Union's own. Two of the largest San Francisco shipping houses fail by 1905 (PROPOSED), and their lawyers argue to the end that a style is not a place.
- **The crowns and the nations.** The Hawaiian Crown, the Philippine crown and the homelands keep label laws of their own, and the Realm's courts recognise them: Kona's coffee, Batangas coffee, Diné churro mutton and Hopi blue corn are labelled under their own governments' rules.
- **Abroad.** The Realm protects its goods abroad by reciprocity clauses in its own commercial treaties, which bind a partner to stop false Realm names at its customs: Japan's first, in the compact's renewal of 1897 (PROPOSED; Japan joins the Paris Convention, with its article on false indications, in 1899, *Hist.*, to verify), carried into the Concord's label standard at Hayama in 1928 (`CR-076` G4, PROPOSED). The Madrid Agreement of 1891 (*Hist.*; the Union never signed it) is not needed at home, and accession is left OPEN. Foreign houses register their trademarks like anyone else (Kikkoman's in California in 1879, before the act; Lee Kum Sheung's, `A12`).
- **The first fight.** In 1898 the Napa valley's leading growers form a syndicate and petition the county board for an exclusive line inside which only its members may write "Napa". The board refuses; the syndicate appeals, and in 1900 the Court holds that a place-name is a statement anyone may make truly and that the act grants no exclusive right (PROPOSED; `CR-057`'s distrust of exclusive rights). The syndicate's members lose the premium they had expected the line to fix. Within ten years the register of results puts several of them below growers from Sonoma and the Russian River, and their price follows the register (PROPOSED).
- **What it costs.** A small grower cannot easily build a name alone. Private brands and the fair results answer that only in part, and the cost is real.

### A7. Public health (historical anchors APPROVED, 21:40; the *Tsukuba* correction APPROVED, 09:47; Pacifica's part PROPOSED)

1. **Beriberi.** In 1884 Takaki Kanehiro's training ship *Tsukuba* sails the route on which *Ryūjō* had lost 25 of 376 men to beriberi in 1882–83, with 169 sick, carrying his ration of barley, meat, fish, milk and vegetables beside the rice (Takaki Kanehiro, CT-TAKAKI-KANEHIRO, 1849–1920, *Hist.*). She leaves Shinagawa in February and is back there in November with 14 mild cases among her 333 men and no deaths, all of the cases among men who would not eat the new food (*Hist.*). Honolulu is her last port before home, and she comes in with no case worse than mild. Her officers cable Tokyo that there is no beriberi to speak of, and two of Pacifica's naval surgeons go aboard and copy her sick lists (PROPOSED). Takaki is not aboard; he receives the cable in Tokyo. Pacifica's navy and its merchant training ships adopt the ration on their own authority (PROPOSED); no treaty is needed, since the compact of 1887 is three years off and the Concord forty-three. The ration comes ashore in the port towns, and its curry later (`A10`). The Japanese Army refuses the ration for a generation in our timeline (*Hist.*; Mori Ōgai as background), and Pacifica's surgeons watch the dispute and say nothing in public.
2. **No goitre belt in the interior.** In our timeline the Great Lakes, the mountain states and the Northwest are a goitre belt until iodised salt from 1924 (*Hist.*). In the Realm, kombu stock, sea fish carried inland by rail, keep endemic goitre down a generation early, with kelp meal in the interior's feed from the wartime harvest onward (`L1722`). Kaʻiulani's goitre of 1896 (`L852 ("an exophthalmic goitre")`) is a different disease and is unaffected.
3. **No endemic pellagra.** The rump's mill villages live with pellagra and hookworm (`L1131 ("pellagra and hookworm")`). The Realm's maize is eaten as masa, treated with lime, which frees its niacin (*Hist.*), beside fish, beans and rice. The Office prints both banks' tables and does not comment.

### A9. The innovations the Realm champions, and who lost (APPROVED, 09:47; particulars PROPOSED)

Each of these began in our timeline as a fight over trust: the stored egg, the label, the cook's bill, the waiter, the health claim. In the Union and Europe they were settled late and partly. In the Realm each was fought by people with money at stake, and each has a winner and a loser; some were settled early, some late, and one is never settled. Sources are the books listed under `## Sources`.

1. **The electric cold chain.** The Realm's ammonia (`CR-054`, `CR-082`) and its electrified homes (`L81`) make small electric refrigerating machines common in its towns by the 1900s, a decade before the Union (*Hist.*: ammonia compression from Linde, 1877; 86% of American ice plants by 1909). *Lost:* the Sierra's natural-ice companies on the Truckee, which harvest their last commercial winter about 1910 (PROPOSED, a divergence; *Hist.*: the Truckee harvest ran into the 1920s). The chartered trunk lines (`L703 ("The trunk lines are chartered by the confederal chambers on Land Fund grants")`) must carry refrigerated cars as common carriers under their charters, but the Salt Lake–Snake–Columbia line runs its own car company and refuses other shippers' cars until a ruling of the Court of about 1904 (PROPOSED; *Hist.*: the Pacific Fruit Express from 1906; precooling from 1904). Refrigerated steamers cross to Japan from the 1890s. **Story facts (APPROVED, 09:47):** household electric refrigerators from the early 1920s, in perhaps a third to a half of wired homes by 1932 (*Hist.*: 17% of wired American homes in 1932), and quick-frozen salmon, halibut and tuna for the interior and Japan in the late 1920s (*Hist.*: Birdseye's multiplate freezer, 1927). Fresh milk, butter and ice cream everywhere are what drive `A1`'s dairy line. Leo Szilárd's refrigerator, which in our timeline failed for noise, may be a small detail (background).
2. **The truthful label and the published store.** *Settled early, after a fight.* The cold-store owners hold eggs and butter for months and sell them as fresh until a rotten-egg case in San Francisco about 1896 (PROPOSED), after which the Truth of Labels Act of 1897 requires a date on stored food (*Hist.*: the Union's "cold storage evils" press of about 1911 and the state labelling laws after it). The owners fight the clause in the courts for three years and lose. The Office prints the stocks held in store each quarter from 1901 (monthly from 1931), and a cold-store syndicate that had cornered the city's butter in the winter of 1900 loses its corner. *Never settled:* health claims are tested only on complaint, so a false one lasts until a rival complains, and in the 1920s the vitamin claims are fought case by case, most of them never brought (*Hist.*: the Union's vitamin claims; Japan's saccharin-laced quick soy sauce, exposed by inspection in 1910). Hoover's food administration in the war licenses stores and takes their returns, and drops its pledge campaign after one try.
3. **The food sciences (reshaped by `A4`'s master's title, 11:43).** The Polytechnic examines the cook's trade (`A4`). It does not teach cookery. Its food chemistry, refrigeration engineering and fermentation work sit in its existing schools and the Polytechnic's agricultural school and fermentation station (item 7), which admit by examination; women are among the cooks who pass the test from its start in 1895, and the hotel kitchens refuse to hire them until the 1910s. *Lost:* San Francisco's French cooks' society, which wanted a closed examination of its own and the right to pass its own members, and does not get it. *Hist.:* France's first professional cooking school (Paris, 1893–94) admitted French citizens only, competed with unpaid apprenticeship and closed within two years.
4. **The cook's bill.** The Savoy's scandal of 1897 (*Hist.*: Escoffier's commission from suppliers, deliveries short of what was paid for) reaches the Realm's kitchens in the trade papers. The guilds' masters swear never to take a supplier's commission. The county halls' purchase books are open to audit like all county accounts (`CR-038`), and the halls' cooks are the first to be caught. Two San Francisco hotel chefs are struck from their guild's roll for commissions about 1900, and one of them keeps cooking at the same stove, because the hotel does not care (PROPOSED).
5. **The honest restaurant.** *Half won.* No tipping and no surcharges, and a menu in the diners' languages that states what is in each dish and what it costs (*Hist.*: D'Oyly Carte's "No charge for Baths, Lights, or Attendance", 1889; the failed American campaigns against tipping). It is a custom of the guilds' houses and the county halls, not a law. The waiters of the French houses, who earn more on tips than the halls pay, fight it through their own society and win: the luxury trade keeps its tips to 1932, and the honest house takes the halls and the mid-price trade. A waiterless electric curiosity or two comes out of the Calculation Office (background).
6. **The cosmopolitan city.** Left to the destination CR (D-26), except that the Realm's coastal cities, with no Exclusion, have every kitchen of the Pacific in them (`A3`, `A12`).
7. **Fermentation and the Pacific's sauces.** The Polytechnic's agricultural school and its fermentation station (PROPOSED), grown out of Hilgard's wine work of the 1880s (PROPOSED; *Hist.*: the University of California's viticulture work under Hilgard from 1880), works on wine, beer, sake, shōyu and miso cultures from the late 1880s (*Hist.* for comparison: Japan's Brewing Experiment Station, 1904; Noda's joint laboratory, 1904, distributing a pure koji mould). Pacifican and then Realm breweries make shōyu in Hawaiʻi and the Sacramento Valley, with Noda's houses as partners and rivals (*Hist.*: Kikkoman's mark registered in California in 1879, before Japan had trademark law). Takamine Jōkichi (CT-TAKAMINE-JOKICHI, 1854–1922) comes to the station as a fellow in about 1890, so what he finds there is licensed at the one royalty (`CR-057`); Takadiastase, his koji enzyme of 1894, is the station's and the Realm's (*Hist.*: Takadiastase, 1894, licensed to Parke-Davis). *Won, and lost by others:* his koji process for whiskey, which replaces malt in the mash. In our timeline the Whiskey Trust at Peoria backed it, the maltsters fought it, and his plant burned (*Hist.*; 1891 is the usual year, to verify). In the Realm the distilleries take it up from the 1890s because it is cheaper, and the Sacramento Valley's barley maltsters lose most of the distillers' trade within a decade (PROPOSED). The Spirit Stores' label states strength, not mash (`CR-076`), so no drinker can tell. Where his adrenaline work of 1900–01 falls is left to the science CRs (OPEN). Glutamate by fermentation stays as in our timeline, after 1932 (OPEN).
8. **The northern canners.** Pacifica's advisers in Kuroda's Hokkaido (`L747 ("Kuroda Kiyotaka's Hokkaido development brings Pacifican advisers north")`, itself PROPOSED in canon) and the northern school of Sapporo and Sitka (`L741 ("Sapporo's agronomists and Sitka's assayers")`) include Columbia and Alaska salmon canners at Ishikari and Bekkai from 1877 (*Hist.*: a Maine canner under the Capron mission). Hokkaido's kombu comes back the other way.
9. **Cooperatives that must tell the truth.** The growers' cooperatives advertise under the labels act (`A6`), so their claims are open to complaint. *Lost:* a raisin cooperative's iron claim, struck by the Assay Office in about 1926 on a rival's complaint, and the cooperative pays its rival's costs (PROPOSED; *Hist.*: Sun-Maid's "Had Your Iron Today?"; Sunkist).

### A10. Signature dishes, experiences and the forms of the agon (APPROVED in direction, 09:56; particulars PROPOSED)

How the books say signature dishes are made: named for a person or an occasion (Pêche Melba; Poularde Édouard VII with its curry for the maharajas); a nation's wild luxury dressed in the ruling style (the Union's terrapin and canvasback, named in French); spectacle from ice and fruit; hybrids where two kitchens meet (Japan's cutlet and curry rice; ramen from Cantonese cooks in Tokyo, 1910).

**Dishes**
1. **The Pacific consommé.** French fond and Japanese dashi clarified together: crab and fish bones, kombu, katsuobushi. It is the set dish of the trade test's blind dinner (`A4`) and the field of "the powder or the pot".
2. **Abalone, the Realm's terrapin.** Three ways, one from each of the coast's peoples: Chinese dried and braised (Point Alones), Japanese raw and steamed (Kodani's divers), the pounded Monterey steak (*Hist.*: Pop Ernest, about 1910; to verify). The three-way contest is Monterey's annual day. The beds are protected by county fishing law and labelled to the bed they came from, and are not stripped as in our timeline.
3. **The navy's curry.** Takaki's ration (`A7`) as the mixed crews' dish, beef or fish with barley rice; it comes ashore in the port towns, and a ship's own curry is the crew's pride (PROPOSED; *Hist.*: Japan's navy curry from about 1908; a weekly curry day is post-war).
4. **The Pacific noodle soup.** Cantonese and Japanese cooks meet in the eating houses of Honolulu and San Francisco from the 1880s, decades before Tokyo, and in the county halls from the 1890s; a noodle soup on a dashi-and-pork broth becomes the working day's lunch, and each county's version is a fair class (*Hist.*: Hawaii's saimin; to verify).
5. **Japanese-Western diner food.** Japanese cooks run cheap Western-style cafés on the coast: crab croquettes, pork cutlets with shredded cabbage, omelettes over rice (*Hist.*: Japan's yōshoku). They fight the Cantonese-Western houses (`A12`) for the same clerk's lunch, street by street.
6. **The founders' breakfast.** Hangtown fry (oysters, eggs and bacon, the Gold Rush's luxury; to verify) served on 1 August: a miners' and settlers' dish on the Constitution's day, not a court's.
7. **Shave ice and the people's ice cream.** Machine ice in every town, Hawaii's *kakigōri* with fruit syrups (to verify), dairy ice cream from the household refrigerator; the pineapple, Delmonico's exotic of 1880, is the Realm's commonest fruit.

**Experiences**
8. **The station box.** A boxed meal at each railway division point with that county's speciality labelled to its place: salmon at The Dalles, lamb at Winnemucca, chile at Albuquerque, abalone at Salinas. The press estate ranks the station caterers each year, and the railways' own contracts with their caterers end when a caterer falls two years running (*Hist.*: Japan's ekiben from 1885).
9. **The blind supper.** A dinner served without a menu; guests guess each dish's county and grower before the register is read. The Realm's game of transparency and terroir.
10. **The fire contest.** Californio beef over red oak (*Hist.*: the Santa Maria barbecue; to verify), Japanese charcoal grilling and the Basque asador, judged blind on one day, with a public vote posted beside the jury's: the interior's and the settlers' arena (`CR-071` H6.4) and the county fair's largest crowd. The rancho's entry is usually cooked by its Chinese cookhouse cook and entered under the rancho's name. The fair's rule lets an entry carry its cook's name beside the house's, if the cook asks (`C9`). The coast's grill counters are its everyday school (*Hist.*: the West Coast's beefsteak counters of the 1880s, steak, chips and coffee for two bits).
11. **The cooks' challenge evening.** The form of `A4`'s powder-or-pot tasting (`C4`), used for any stated claim.

### A11. Desserts and pastry (APPROVED in direction, 11:00; particulars PROPOSED)

Paris judged its pastry by the eye: sugar temples on wooden frames, prizes for display, nothing tasted. The Realm's pastry rule is the opposite: **everything is edible and tasted, and structure is admired only if it survives the spoon.** Escoffier's theatre (Pêche Melba on a swan of carved ice; peaches under spun sugar; the omelette *en surprise*) is the inheritance, technique the foundation, and Yuan Mei's hatred of showmanship (`A12`) the check on it.

1. **The Sitka.** Meringue over Kona coffee ice cream, browned at the table with a hot iron. Named for the flag change at Sitka in October 1867, since in canon Pacifica, not the Union, bought Alaska (*Hist.*: Delmonico's "Alaska, Florida").
2. **Azuki cream.** Azuki bavarois, azuki and chestnut, azuki ice cream. A Japanese recipe of the 1920s for azuki with milk and vanilla "never really caught on" in milk-shy Japan (*Hist.*); in the Realm's dairy towns it does.

### A12. The Chinese kitchens (APPROVED, 12:00; particulars PROPOSED)

The Realm's Chinese residents are about 4.8 million by 1932 (`A1`, MODEL), most of them Cantonese from the Pearl River delta: Sam Yup city people, Sze Yup country people from Taishan and its neighbours, and Hakka. After 1912 the Republic's officials, students and exiles add northerners and Shanghai people. With no Exclusion, these kitchens grow for eighty years in the open, and they fight among themselves as hard as with anyone.

1. **Yuan Mei's book.** Yuan Mei's *Recipes from the Garden of Contentment* (1792; *Hist.*) begins with what the cook must know (the nature of each food, flavour, colour, season, utensils) and then lists the cook's sins: artifice and showmanship, carelessness, following the set banquet formulas, keeping dishes hot for show, overcooking, throwing everything into one pot, and drinking so much that the taste is dulled (*Hist.*). It gives a fine meal's credit six parts to the cook and four to the buyer (*Hist.*). An English version is made at San Francisco in the 1890s by the Chinese consulate's interpreters with Chinese Pacifican graduates of the Polytechnic (PROPOSED; the Educational Mission's own men are in Li's and Sheng's offices in China by then, `L764 ("By 1894 the mission's men")`). It becomes the trade test's second book beside Escoffier's *Guide* (`A4`). The French cooks complain that a poet who could not cook has been set beside the man who wrote the brigade; the Cantonese cooks complain that a Hangzhou gentleman's taste has been set above Canton's. Both books stay.
2. **The Cantonese-Western houses.** In Shanghai in the 1890s the Chinese-owned Western restaurants were run mostly by Cantonese owners with cooks from Hong Kong, and one street had as many of them as all the regional Chinese restaurants together (*Hist.*). Pacifica has the same houses earlier and on a larger scale: Cantonese-owned dining rooms in every coastal city from the 1870s, serving roast beef, oxtail soup and chops in a Hong Kong style, with soy on the table and chopsticks for those who want them. They are the coast's mid-price Western table, and they fight the Japanese cafés (`A10` item 5) for the same lunch trade. The French-trained hotel cooks call their food a counterfeit. The Jiangsu and Zhejiang merchants who come after 1912 resent the Cantonese money behind them, as their counterparts in Shanghai did (*Hist.*: the Fourth Avenue fire of 1899 and the tabloid's "Fire God eats Western food").
3. **The ranking that moves.** The press estate's lists (`A10`), from the 1890s, rank the Chinese houses with everyone else's, and the ranking moves with the Realm's own Chinese politics and fashion, as Shanghai's guidebooks moved with Shanghai's (*Hist.*). In the 1890s the Sam Yup banquet houses of San Francisco and Honolulu stand first. After Sun's visit to Thalassia (`L1334`) and the Republic, the palace cooks' house (item 8) is where the Republic's consulate, its students and the exiles of Yuan's years are seen to dine, from 1914. Through the 1920s the Cantonese houses rebuild, the lists follow them, and by 1928 they are first again; a team of Japanese chefs sent to study Chinese cooking judges Cantonese the best cooking in the world (*Hist.*), and the French cooks do not forgive it. The Fujian houses, first among the dock houses in 1910, are called complacent by 1930 and "fallen behind in the ranks" (*Hist.*, Wang Dingjiu's phrase), and lose their non-Fujianese customers within two seasons.
4. **The Fujian dock houses.** China's sailors were mostly Fujianese, and Shanghai's dockside Fujian restaurants were cheap, dirty and superb, with cooks who had cooked for admirals (*Hist.*). Fujianese cooks work the galleys of the China Merchants' Pacific line, which runs from 1880 (`CR-081`; its officers are the mission's naval graduates by the 1890s, `L798 ("Most of the mission's naval graduates go to the merchant line")`; the cooks' origin PROPOSED), and from the 1880s some of them leave the ships at San Francisco and Honolulu and open dock houses along the waterfront, where the fish comes from the crews' own nets and the customers are stevedores, sailors and the hotel cooks on their nights off (PROPOSED).
5. **The camp, ranch and ship cooks.** In our timeline Chinese cooks fed the West's ranches, lumber and mining camps, railway gangs, cannery bunkhouses and ships' crews (*Hist.*, general knowledge). In the Realm they are the largest body of cooks in the trade and the least regarded. Their kitchens are the hardest: one cook for forty men, a wood stove, the men's complaints, the foreman's fists, the slurs in every language of the camp. They cook beef, beans, sourdough and pie for the men and their own food for themselves. The trade test is open to them from its start in 1895 and very few sit it before 1900, because it costs a week's absence the foreman will not give. The rancho's fire-contest entry is usually theirs and carries the rancho's name (`C9`).
6. **Lee Kum Sheung's oyster sauce.** Lee Kum Sheung (CT-LEE-KUM-SHEUNG; dates to verify) makes oyster sauce at Nanshui in 1888 and moves his house to Macao in 1902 (*Hist.*, to verify). In the Realm it is sold in every Chinatown from the 1890s, and its success brings imitators: brown sauces of starch, caramel and salt sold as oyster sauce by San Francisco makers. About 1902, the year the house moves to Macao after the fire at Nanshui (*Hist.*, to verify), Lee's agents complain to the State Assay Office, which finds no oyster in three of the five brands tested and publishes the finding (`C11`, PROPOSED). The imitators lose the name and two of them their business, and Lee's house registers its trademark in the Realm, with Macao stated as its place on every bottle (`A6`). In the 1920s the Realm's Chinese canners make an oyster sauce of their own from Tomales and Willapa oysters under a Realm mark, and the two compete under their own names.
7. **Wu Yunchu's powder.** Wu Yunchu (CT-WU-YUNCHU, 1891–1953; to verify), a chemist at Shanghai, makes glutamate by the acid hydrolysis of wheat gluten, the same raw material as Ajinomoto's, by a process he works out for himself outside Ajinomoto's patents, and sells it from 1923 as Tianchu, the "Heavenly Kitchen", under a Buddha's-hand mark (*Hist.*, to verify), and takes patents abroad from 1926 (*Hist.*, to verify). In our timeline he sold it as China's own against Japan's in years of boycott. Canon's boycotts are short (`L1350 ("the boycott of 1915 is short")`; `L1573 ("a short boycott answers it")`, 1928; ended by the notes of 1930, `L1770 ("an end to the boycotts")`), so he sells mainly on price and on the Chinese cook's loyalty to a Chinese maker (PROPOSED). From 1925 his powder reaches the Realm through the Chinese grocers. Ajinomoto's agents ask the Assay Office to test it, expecting impurity; it passes, and the finding is printed. The grocers decide (`C13`): most of the Realm's Chinese grocers change supplier, and when China's boycott of May 1928 is echoed in the Realm's Chinatowns, Tianchu's sales there double for a season (PROPOSED). The echo costs the Japanese Pacifican grocers who sell Ajinomoto in mixed neighbourhoods a summer's trade and leaves a quarrel in two county halls that outlasts it, and Tianchu loses some of the gain again when the boycotts end in 1930. Japan's powder loses most of the Chinese kitchens' trade and keeps the halls'. The pot school loses again to both (`A4`).
8. **The palace cooks at Thalassia (in direction, 12:00; particulars PROPOSED).** Kaʻiulani stays with the Empress Dowager Longyu until the abdication of 12 February 1912 (`L1334 ("the Emperor and the Empress Dowager are to be safe")`; she was at the Lake Palace in 1908, `L1318 ("Kaʻiulani is at the Lake Palace when both die")`), and Victoria writes to Longyu until Longyu dies in February 1913 (`L1337 ("Victoria writes to Longyu until Longyu dies")`). The court keeps the Forbidden City and its kitchens after the abdication, but Longyu's own household is dispersed at her death. PROPOSED: Victoria, through Kaʻiulani, asks Julian to take Longyu's kitchen cooks into the court kitchen at Thalassia (`L1356 ("Thalassia stands on Kāneʻohe Bay")`), and he does; about a dozen come in 1913 with their apprentices. They join a court kitchen that already has a French brigade and the Japanese cooks of Chiyo's household, and with the Manila kitchen (`A3`) and the Hawaiian Crown's own cooks, who keep the Hawaiian dinners (`A11` item 8), the court's kitchens fight over the state dinners for twenty years. The cooks arrive in the year Victoria blames Julian for backing a man without an army while her friend's house fell (`L1337 ("Victoria blames Julian for backing a man without an army")`), and they come as her reproach; Julian's yes is his answer to it, and neither says so. The court's steward offers them what the register offers: their dishes printed on the state dinner menus under their own names, at the price of depositing their methods under `T-METHOD-1`. The master's title is open to them by the trade test, which costs them nothing but the test. They refuse the names, because the recipes belong to the household that taught them, and their dishes are printed on the menus as "from the kitchen of the Empress Dowager", unnamed. None of them sits the test. In 1914 two of them leave for San Francisco and open a palace-cuisine house (item 3), which closes in 1928 when the lists move to the rebuilt Cantonese houses (PROPOSED). A second, smaller group could follow after Feng's men drive Puyi from the Forbidden City in November 1924 (`L1568 ("Feng Yuxiang turns back from the Rehe front")`; OPEN); in our timeline cooks released then opened Fangshan in Beijing in 1925 (*Hist.*).
9. **Macao's sweets.** `A11` item 4.

### A13. The chronology, 1846–1932 (APPROVED, 14:06; particulars PROPOSED)

The Realm's table changes less in its recipes than in where people eat, who cooks, how the food is kept and how the cook is judged, as sushi did in Tokyo between the stall and the restaurant (*Hist.*: Rath) and the Union's restaurants did between Delmonico's and the cafeteria (*Hist.*: Haley). Six periods:

1. **The bachelors' table (1846–about 1855).** A city of single men eats out, until the assisted passages of women and families from 1852 (`L326 ("From 1852 the Superintendency pays the passage")`, itself PROPOSED in canon; `L329 ("a country of single men is a camp, not a republic")`) fill it with households. San Francisco's eating houses are French, Chinese, German and Italian from the 1850s; there is no local dish, only a cosmopolitan fare (*Hist.*: Noah Brooks, 1868), and the luxury is oysters, eggs and bacon in a pan (Hangtown fry). The ranchos cook beef over oak; Hawaii eats poi, fish and the first Chinese rice; ice comes south by ship from Sitka and Kodiak (*Hist.*: the San Francisco ice company of American founders, the American-Russian Commercial Company, 1850s). Chinese cooks already feed the mining camps. Cooking is a job, not a trade; nobody judges it.
2. **The households, the eating houses and the railway (about 1855–1887).** The families the passages bring cook at home, and the peace brings the trunk lines (`L703`). The dining station at each division point feeds the line; the Cantonese-Western houses open in every coastal town from the 1870s (`A12`); Japanese eating houses cook for Japanese customers in San Francisco, Honolulu and the Wakamatsu country from the 1860s, since Pacifica has about 35,000 Japanese-origin residents by 1870 and 107,000 by 1880 (`population/outputs/workbook_changes.md`, MODEL), and from the 1880s for anyone (PROPOSED; *Hist.* for comparison: in our timeline, with a few dozen Japanese, Kame in Los Angeles, 1885, cooked Western food, and the Yamato in San Francisco, 1887, was the first to serve Japanese food). Kikkoman's mark is registered in California in 1879; Linde's machines make ice from 1877; the first guild fellows go abroad in the 1880s (`A4`). The *Tsukuba* comes into Honolulu in 1884, and the Feather River sheds burn in 1886. The kitchen is still the master's and his eye.
3. **The measures reach the kitchen (1887–1900).** The Spirits Act of 1888 gives the halls their coffee counters. In the 1890s every county opens its hall (`L1027 ("In the 1890s every county opens a hall")`), and the halls' kitchens (PROPOSED, an addition to `CR-038` F8) cook for hundreds a day and sell their meals at cost from their published accounts, with no grant in the price. The fellows bring Escoffier's brigade home in the 1890s. The press estate's lists begin to rank houses. The trade test is first set in 1895 and the guilds lose the power to pass a master. The Truth of Labels Act comes in 1897 (`A6`), and the Napa syndicate's petition and Lee Kum Sheung's sauce are its first fights. Yuan Mei is translated. Refrigerated steamers carry fruit to Japan.
4. **The electric kitchen and the stall (1900–1914).** Electric ranges and small refrigerating machines spread through the professional kitchens; the halls and the railway commissaries keep their recipes on cards (`A14`). On the waterfronts of Honolulu, San Francisco and Seattle, Japanese stalls sell nigiri on ice to clerks at lunch, and the label law catches the stall frauds that Tokyo's restaurateurs complained of (*Hist.*: dyed tofu sold as tuna and fishcake as squid at Tokyo's stalls, 1910) (PROPOSED). Julian's wedding dinner of November 1907 puts the Kuroda household's cooks and the Realm's brigade at one table and the halls' set dinner on the same night (`C16`). The earthquake of 1906 moves San Francisco's Japanese quarter; Sacramento's fair of that autumn shows the year's hatred (`C9`). Ikeda names umami in 1908, the powder-or-pot tasting is about 1912, Akiyama's fellows come home in 1913, and Longyu's cooks reach Thalassia the same year.
5. **The war kitchen (1914–1922).** The war and the Russian campaign feed armies, the Entente's bread and the forces' kitchens by the Office's tables. Pacifica's navy has kept central control of its rations since the 1880s, as Japan's has since 1890 (to verify) (*Hist.*: the Imperial Navy's rations standardized by nutrient from 1890; the army's catering reformed only from 1920); the army's field kitchens follow in 1914–18, and the cooks they train come home in 1919–22 with the habit of weights, yields and schedules. Hoover's food administration licenses the stores. The cafeteria spreads from Los Angeles (*Hist.*: the 1900s), and Japanese cooks open Cantonese-style houses and Cantonese cooks Japanese-style cafés wherever the other kitchen's clerk lunch sells better (`A10` item 5, `A12` item 2; PROPOSED; *Hist.* for comparison: some sixty Japanese-owned "chop suey" houses in Los Angeles by 1927, filling a trade that Exclusion had thinned). Dairy begins its climb.
6. **The cold kitchen and the counter (1922–1932).** Household refrigerators from the early 1920s and quick-frozen fish from the late 1920s (`A9`) change the household table more than anything since the railway: fresh milk and butter everywhere, fish inland, ice cream at home. Nigiri reaches Boise and Salt Lake on fast-chilled fish a generation before Japan's own inland towns had it (*Hist.*: in Japan nigiri waited for post-war refrigeration away from the coast) (PROPOSED). The household survey of 1925–27 measures the result (`A1`). Wu's powder arrives in 1925, the Cantonese houses are first again by 1928, the snowball is the decade's sweet, and the slump of 1929–32 closes the luxury houses and fills the halls. In 1930 the Office prints its two columns; in June 1932 Akiyama's kitchen serves Julian at Hayama.

### A14. Engineering and the brigade: how the method develops, and who wins (APPROVED, 14:06; particulars PROPOSED)

The Realm's cooks take the engineer's method the way the Realm takes everything: as a contest. Each stage makes some cooks rich and some redundant.

**The guilds: some keep up, most lose.** The guilds lose everything the Realm's institutions do better. The Polytechnic's test takes the passing of masters in 1895. The kitchens' own training and the test take the teaching. The halls and commissaries hire from the congress's register of passed cooks, by number and by the posted results (`CR-037`; the congress itself never hires, `L1082`). What is left to a guild is what no institution does: the fellowships abroad, the blind dinner, the trade journal and the honour. The French-led guild of San Francisco refuses the thermometer ("a cook is made at the stove") and is by 1920 a dining society of old masters with the most coveted dinner on the coast and no power over anyone's career. The Cantonese guild, founded because the French guild would not dine Chinese masters (`A4`), adapts fastest. It coaches its members for the written paper in its own rooms and recommends them, as `L857` lets any guild do; its members win more of the halls' and commissaries' posts than any other guild's because more of them pass; and it sends its fellows to Canton and Hong Kong. By the 1920s it is the trade's strongest guild. The halls' cooks found an association of their own about 1910 (PROPOSED), with a journal on costs, yields and machines. It is not a guild and gives no title, and by 1932 it speaks for more cooks than all the guilds together. The Japanese cooks' association of the coast keeps its tie to Tokyo through Akiyama's kitchen and is the guild that sends most fellows abroad.

**Sophistication over time.** By 1932 the Realm's best cooking is neither Escoffier's nor Yuan Mei's. It is a kitchen that measures like the Polytechnic, works like a brigade, holds and freezes like a commissary and finishes at the counter, in front of the diner, like a Tokyo stall. Each stage was won by someone, against someone, and the losers are still in the trade, cooking in the camps, the eating houses and the old guild's dining room.

> ### The table beneath the measures
>
> Nobody plans the Realm's table. It grows from the ground (a cold-current coast, a Mediterranean climate, rice soils, open range and no corn belt), from the people who came, and from prices and fashion. By 1932 it is a dozen regional kitchens, and inside the cities neighbourhood kitchens, that borrow from one another and compete: San Francisco's French, Genoese, Cantonese and Japanese houses, Monterey's abalone and live fire, the Delta's Chinese and Japanese farm cooking, the interior's range beef and Basque tables, Hawaii's poi and plantation lunches, and Manila's Spanish kitchen under its own crown. Two schools of technique set the tone of the best of them: the French, with its stocks, sauces, pastry and brigade, and the Spanish and Mediterranean, with its honest ingredients, live fire, olive oil and time. Yuan Mei's *Recipes from the Garden of Contentment*, translated at San Francisco in the 1890s, stands beside Escoffier's *Guide* as the trade test's second book.
>
> Cooking is a contest with published results, and its kitchens are rough. Brigades despise one another, commis are hazed and burned, stocks are spoiled before a proving, and recipes are stolen. The blind tests, the posted scores and the sealed deposit of a named dish's method exist because no cook trusts another. From 1895 the master's title is a Polytechnic trade test, by number; the guilds keep only their blind dinners, their fellowships abroad and their honour, and the French guild of San Francisco, which refuses the thermometer, is by 1920 a dining society with no power. The Cantonese guild, founded because the French would not dine Chinese masters, becomes the strongest. The kitchen's methods move from the master's eye to Escoffier's stations, to weights and thermometers, to the halls' recipe cards and costs per plate, to the cold room, and at the top to the counter where the cook works in front of the guest. At each step someone wins and someone loses: the eating houses to the halls, the porters to the machines, the pot to the powder, the ice dealers to the cold chain.
>
> The Truth of Labels Act of 1897 makes every statement on a label true and testable and reserves no place to anyone. A valley's name belongs to everyone who grows in it, a field's to the deed that holds it, and a grower's reputation to the register of results that the Office prints each year. The Napa growers who wanted a line of their own are refused by the Court in 1900, and the shippers who sold "Burgundy" lose their trade.
>

## C. Set pieces (candidates, not drafted)

1. **The *Tsukuba* at Honolulu (1884; Pacifica's surgeons).** The training ship comes in with a handful of men in the sick bay, every one of them a man who threw the barley over the side. Two of Pacifica's naval surgeons go aboard, lay her sick lists beside the *Ryūjō*'s, and eat the new ration in the wardroom. Her officers' cable to Tokyo says there is no beriberi to speak of. Takaki is off the page until he reads it.
2. **The boat home (China and Portland, 1889–90; Ah Bing).** Ah Bing goes home in 1889 to a wife he has not seen since he was a young man and a village that expects him to stay. The Realm will let him back; nothing will make him go. He chooses the rows at Milwaukie, and at Portland nobody asks him anything.
3. **The Ritz kitchen (Paris, about 1910; Akiyama).** A Japanese commis and a Pacifican commis on guild fellowships, in the kitchen Escoffier built and has left, both treated as intruders by the French brigade, fight the same men and then each other for the same station.
4. **The powder or the pot (Carquinez, about 1912; Ikeda).** A blind tasting of two consommés, one drawn from bone and kombu, one seasoned with the crystal. The jury splits; Ikeda declines to say which he prefers; every paper prints the score. The next week a hall cook in Fresno orders a hundredweight of the powder.
5. **The judging table (the Sacramento fair, about 1895).** A cook from one of the restored Feather families decides whether to enter her roast duck in the same class as the settlers' range beef, in front of the men who put her family on the barge nine years before. She enters. The tasting is supposed to be blind, and everyone knows whose duck it is. The beef wins. She does not enter again; her son does, ten years later, under the county hall's name.
6. **The Napa petition (1898–1900).** The syndicate's map of an exclusive line, the growers outside it packing the county board's hearing, the board's refusal, the Court's ruling that a place-name belongs to whoever can state it truly. Ten years later a syndicate founder reads the register of results and finds his wine ninth, below a Sonoma grower who was in the hearing room.
7. **Hayama (June 1932; Akiyama, Julian).** Between the sittings of the Council of the Two Crowns, Akiyama's kitchen gives the Emperor's dinner for Julian: French in its method, Japanese and Pacifican in its produce, a Napa wine labelled to its vineyard beside a Kyoto course. Nobody mentions the treaty at table.
8. **Reading both columns (Thalassia, May 1932; Mead, Hoover, Keawe).** Hoover lays the second column on the committee table; Mead turns the page back to the first, asks what the dairy series will do to the second by 1940, and asks in what year the margin closes on the Office's own measure.
9. **The fire contest (the Sacramento fair, autumn 1906).** Weeks after Tate's board has segregated the Japanese pupils, three fires in one ring: a Santa Maria rancho's entry, cooked by its Chinese cookhouse cook, a Basque asador from Winnemucca and a Japanese charcoal griller from Fresno. The jury eats blind and gives it to the Japanese cook; the crowd votes for the Basque; both results are posted side by side. The rancho's owner collects his second place. The cookhouse cook asks the jury to post his own name under the rancho's, as the rule allows, and is fired the next week.
10. **The New Year pounding (a county hall, 1 January, about 1925).** Pounder and turner teams from the hall's Japanese, Portuguese and settler families compete at the mortar. The winning team's mochi is wrapped round ice cream from the hall's freezer and handed out to the queue; the team that lost last year has trained since March and loses again.
11. **The label case (San Francisco, about 1902; Lee Kum Sheung's agents).** The Assay Office opens five bottles of oyster sauce before a room of grocers and makers and reads out what is in each. Three contain no oyster. One of the three makers is in the room, and he is ruined by the evening papers.
12. **The palace cooks (Thalassia, 1913).** The Empress Dowager's cooks unpack their knives in a kitchen where a French chef de cuisine and Chiyo's Japanese cooks already hold the stations. The steward offers them their names on the state dinner menus and reads them the deposit rule that comes with the names. They refuse, all of them, in a language the steward does not speak, and cook the dinner.
13. **Two powders and a pot (San Francisco, 1925; the Chinese grocers).** Ajinomoto's agents bring Tianchu to the Assay Office expecting it to fail. It passes, and the finding is printed under both marks. At their association's meeting the city's Chinese grocers vote on whether to keep Ajinomoto's agency, which gives them credit, or take Tianchu's, which does not; they take Tianchu's, and the agency's credit is called in that month.
14. **The ranking (San Francisco, 1930).** The press estate's list moves a Fujian house that has been first among the city's Chinese houses for twenty years to ninth, below two rebuilt Cantonese houses. Its owner reads the list in his kitchen and changes nothing, and by spring the tables are empty except on Fujianese feast days.
15. **The thermometer (San Francisco, 1902; the French guild).** At the guild's annual dinner a young master who passed the Polytechnic's test proposes that the guild's own dinner be cooked to the test's weights and temperatures. The old masters vote him down, and the president says that a cook is made at the stove. The young master resigns from the guild that night, and in 1920 he is running the largest commissary on the coast.
16. **The wedding dinner (Thalassia, November 1907; Julian, Chiyo, Kiyoshi).** Two kitchens cook one dinner: the Kuroda household's kaiseki-trained cooks and the Realm's French-trained brigade, course for course, each trying to outdo the other. Who cooks which course has been fought over for weeks, and the French guild has threatened to cook nothing at all. The menu says only what is true: every dish with its kitchen, its grower and its place, salmon from the Kuroda estates' waters in Hokkaido carried on a refrigerated steamer, a Napa wine named for its vineyard. The press has asked for a dish named for the new queen; the guild has refused it under the naming rule, and Chiyo ends the quarrel by asking that nothing be named for her. The same evening the county halls serve one set dinner at cost from a single recipe card, cooked by the commissaries across the Realm, and the French masters say so in print. Japanese guests sit at Thalassia two months after Vancouver (`L1162 ("Vancouver riots against them on 7–9 September 1907")`) and a year after Sacramento, and everyone at the table knows what the dinner says.

  - *Case A (the shape approved at 07:16; not chosen, 13:36):* marks of origin on the French model, adapted: syndicates write a district's rule, county boards draw the line, the courts and the Assay Office enforce, rival marks may stand in one county, no borrowed names, accession to the Madrid Agreement. It protects origin and not method, but it still gives a district's incumbents a legal monopoly of its name and a fight over every boundary.
  - *Case B (withdrawn in revision 5's drafting: no protection of place at all) is not offered.*
  - *Case C (APPROVED, 25 November 2026, 13:36):* **the Truth of Labels Act of 1897** (name PROPOSED). Nothing is reserved; every statement is checked.
    1. **Every statement on a label must be true and is testable:** place, grower, year, grape or breed, content, method, age. The Assay Office tests on complaint and publishes. A false statement is fraud, as on the drink label since 1866–67 (`CR-076` A4).
    2. **Place to the field.** A label may name its origin as finely as the maker likes (the county, the valley, the ranch, the vineyard, the slope), and every word must be true of all of it. No percentage of outside fruit is allowed under a place-name (*Hist.* for contrast: the Union's later viticultural areas allow 15%).
    3. **No district belongs to anyone.** Every grower in Napa may write "Napa". No syndicate can stop him, and none can bar the grower outside the valley from writing his own county's name. There are cases over whether a statement is true, decided against the public maps (county lines, the land and water office's watersheds, title deeds), but no franchise to win.
    4. **Reputation is a published result, not a privilege.** The Office prints a register of results, the county fair and blind-panel scores beside each grower's name, place and year. On a grower's request the Assay Office prints the measures of a lot: sugar at harvest, acidity, age in wood, additives (*Hist.* for the idea of measured grades: Germany's must-weight classes, fixed in law only in 1971). A name earns its price each year in public, and a famous valley's weak grower sells as the register shows him.
    5. **Collective brands are private.** Growers who want a shared standard form a brand with a private rule, as any trader may, and it competes with other brands and with single growers. The law gives it nothing a trademark does not give.
    6. **No borrowed names** follows from item 1: a Napa wine called "Burgundy" makes a false statement of place.
    7. **The Madrid Agreement is not needed** at home. The Realm protects its goods abroad by reciprocity clauses in its own commercial treaties (Japan's first, in the compact's renewal of 1897, carried into the Concord's label standard of 1928, `CR-076` G4; both PROPOSED), which bind the partner to stop false Realm names at its customs. Accession is left OPEN for later, when Europe asks.
  - *Why Case C is better for the Realm, and for the food:*
    - It follows the Realm's own habits (measure, publish, examine, no exclusive rights) rather than France's.
    - It never freezes a method or a boundary, so the innovators of a valley need no rival mark to be heard.
    - It puts the signal of quality where the agon already makes it, in tasted, published results.
    - It costs the producers who would have lived on a district's name: the Napa incumbents who wanted the line and a monopoly rent get neither, and their lawyers spend 1898–1900 arguing for one before the county boards and the Court and lose (PROPOSED).
    - Its weakness is the small grower who cannot build a name alone. Private brands and the fair results answer it in part. That cost is real and is the price of Case C.
  - *Changes made in revision 8:*
    - `A6` is rewritten on items 1–7.
    - `C6` is the Napa syndicate's petition for a line, refused.
    - The Napa loss falls on the syndicate's members, who lose their premium.
    - Lee Kum Sheung's case (`A12` item 6) is unchanged: it was always a case of content.
    - The crowns' and homelands' marks are those governments' own label laws, recognised by the Realm.
    - D-9 is superseded and D-19 replaced.
  - No fact change in either case.
