## A. The change

### A1. Work 4: why the Realm eats leaner, and how the Office prints it (two columns APPROVED, 24 November 2026, 21:40; happenstance APPROVED as direction, 25 November 2026, 06:58; the particulars PROPOSED)

**Nobody plans the Realm's table.** No ministry recommends a diet, no school teaches one, and the Office of Public Measures, which exists to measure (`L937 ("The Office of Public Measures, established in 1888")`), measures this as it measures everything else and says nothing about it. The table comes out leaner than the Union's for four reasons, none of them a policy (PROPOSED):
1. **The ground.** The Realm has a long, cold-current coast where fish and shellfish are cheap, a Mediterranean climate where vegetables, fruit, olives and vines grow most of the year, rice soils in the Sacramento Valley and the Delta, and open range for cattle and sheep that never enters the cropland account (`CR-082`). It has no corn belt. In the Union, maize fed to hogs makes pork and lard the cheapest food on the table; in the Realm they are dearer, and fish, fruit and rice are cheaper.
2. **Who came.** By 1932 about three in ten of the Realm's residents are of Chinese, Japanese or Filipino origin (`population/outputs/workbook_changes.md`: about 4.8 million Chinese-origin, 5.8 million Japanese-origin and 1.0 million Filipino-origin, MODEL), beside Californios and Hispanos, Hawaiians, and Mediterranean and Atlantic Europeans: Genoese, Basques, Portuguese, Dalmatians, French. Most of them came from tables built on grain, beans, vegetables and fish, and a cook keeps the habits of her mother's kitchen when the prices let her.
3. **Prices.** Households follow them. When fish is cheaper than salt pork, fish wins.
4. **Fashion.** The kitchens that win the Realm's contests (`A4`) prize the season, the sea and the garden. What the best restaurants cook, the halls and the households copy a decade later.

**When it happened.** The table thins over two generations, not at once. In the 1880s the Realm eats much as the Union's West does. By the late 1920s the Office's household budget survey (PROPOSED: 1925–27, the counties' first sample of what households buy and eat) shows a table using about a fifth less crop land a head than the constant figure of 2.1 acres (`diet_saving = 0.22`, MODEL). The Office's earlier tables were not wrong by its own rule: they held the diet constant because no survey existed (`L1028 ("Powell's office reports")`, `CR-082` F-2). With hindsight the margins of 1890–1930 were somewhat wider than printed. The Office says so in one footnote to the 1930 table and does not recompute the old ones. This stays narrative: the model's rows to 1930 are on 2.1 acres, as `CR-082` built them (`## D`).

### A2. Work 5: the farmed sea on two lines (two lines APPROVED, 24 November 2026, 21:40; their reading PROPOSED)

From 1932 the Office reports the farmed sea twice:
- **Line 1, whole diet.** `CR-082`'s figure: a dry tonne of kelp counts as 0.3 tonnes of grain-equivalent, "food, feed and the fertilizer it saves together", with shellfish grown among the kelp folded in. Central case 50,000 km², about 50 million dry tonnes, 14.3 million mouths (low 5.7, high 28.6; MODEL). This is the line that enters the grain balance.
- **Line 2, protein and what it displaces.** The same tonnage read another way: about 5 million tonnes of crude protein a year in the central case (MODEL, `CR-082` Part F), and the feed grain and potash that kelp meal replaces. Line 2 is not added to Line 1 or to anything else. It exists so that a reader can see why the sea is worth farming although it grows no bread.
- **What neither line counts.** The wild fisheries (held at their present catch and never projected upward to close a gap), the Hawaiian fishponds, abalone and the inshore cooperatives are outside the 50,000 km² and outside both lines. They matter to the table (`A3`) and not to the century's arithmetic.

**The kelp compact is unchanged.** `C-1932-KELP-RESEARCH` stays what `CR-085` made it, a research compact between Hokkaido Imperial University, Hokkaido's kombu growers and the Realm's marine stations. The domestic cooperatives at Point Alones and China Camp are licensed under the members' fishing law and, after 1887, the counties'; the fishponds are under the Hawaiian Crown. Neither is a party to the compact.

### A3. The regional kitchens (direction APPROVED, 06:58; the map PROPOSED)

The Realm's cooking is not one cuisine with variations. It is a dozen regional kitchens, and inside the cities neighbourhood kitchens, which know one another, borrow from one another and compete. A cook from Monterey and a cook from Boise share a language and a coin and little else at the stove. The map below is the drafter's; each entry names what the place has that the others lack.

- **San Francisco, the stakes city.** The French colony of the Gold Rush gives the city its first restaurants of rank (*Hist.*). North Beach cooks Genoese and Ligurian fish stews (cioppino is its child, *Hist.*); Chinatown is the Realm's first Cantonese kitchen; the Mission keeps Californio cooking; the Japanese quarter grows in the Western Addition (PROPOSED; canon does not mention the earthquake of 1906, and this CR does not decide it). The hotel kitchens, Victor Hirtzler's at the St Francis from 1904 among them (*Hist.*, background), are where the city's cooks learn the French brigade. Every serious cook in the Realm wants to have cooked here and to have beaten someone here.
- **The Central Coast and Monterey.** Live fire. The rancho barbecue of the Santa Maria valley, beef over red oak, is a Californio tradition (*Hist.*). At Monterey the sea: Point Alones's Chinese squid and abalone fishery from the 1850s (*Hist.*), Kodani's divers from 1897 (*Hist.*), the sardine canneries (*Hist.*), the abalone steak (*Hist.*: Pop Ernest's, about 1910, background). Here, without the bans and without the fire of 1906, Point Alones survives as a licensed cooperative.
- **Los Angeles and the south.** Citrus, chile, the Plaza's Mexican kitchens, the irrigated south of the valley. The Realm's Chinese-Mexican borderland cooking (wok-seared beef with tomato, chile, soy and vinegar over potatoes or rice) runs from here to Sonora.
- **Napa, Sonoma and the Russian River.** Wine and its marks (`A6`); the Swiss-Italian dairies of Marin and Sonoma; Monterey Jack from the 1880s (*Hist.*).
- **The Delta and the Sacramento Valley.** The Delta's levees were raised by Chinese crews in the 1860s and 1870s (*Hist.*), and its first potato fortune was Chin Lung's before it was George Shima's (*Hist.*, Chin Lung as background). Chinese and Japanese farm cooking, asparagus, celery, pears, short-grain rice on the alkali soils (*Hist.* for Biggs, 1908–12; here a decade or two earlier with Hilgard, PROPOSED). This is also the ground of the Feather River case of 1886 (`L922 ("a night mob on the Feather")`): the valley where Chinese families bought their district farms and were burned out of their packing sheds. Its kitchens are contested from the start (see below).
- **The Columbia and the Willamette.** Salmon, halibut, Dungeness crab, berries, orchards; Portland's Chinatown, one of the coast's largest (*Hist.*); Ah Bing's cherry rows at Milwaukie.
- **The interior: Deseret, Idaho, Washoe, western Montana.** Range beef and lamb, sourdough, mountain trout. The Basque boarding houses of Boise, Winnemucca and Bakersfield, with their long tables and set courses for sheepherders (*Hist.*), are the working man's multi-course dinner. Deseret keeps the Saints' habit of storing a year's food (*Hist.* for the later doctrine; PROPOSED as a habit before 1900), and with it the Realm's strongest tradition of preserving, curing and bottling.
- **New Mexico, Arizona and the Diné land.** Pueblo and Hispano cooking, chile, blue corn, piñon, Diné mutton stew from churro sheep (*Hist.*), Sonoran wheat tortillas. The Filipino families of Arizona and New Mexico (`L783 ("Hakka families lead early Chinese family migration")`) add vinegar and garlic.
- **Hawaii.** Kalo and poi, the fishponds, reef fish, the plantation camps' shared lunch, saimin, manapua; Chinese rice on old taro land, Hawaii's second crop after sugar in the 1880s (*Hist.*); Portuguese sausage and sweet bread from the Madeiran and Azorean families who come from 1878 (*Hist.*).
- **Manila, under its own crown.** A Spanish-Filipino kitchen: adobo, escabeche, lechón, kinilaw. It belongs to the Philippine crown's ledger and is the Pacific's Spanish capital.

**The quarrel over rice (PROPOSED).** The Union's labour leaders argue in our timeline that rice-eating labour undercuts meat-eating labour (*Hist.*: the American Federation of Labor's pamphlet "Meat vs. Rice", 1902). In the Realm the same grievance belongs to the settlers of the interior in the 1880s: the Feather River case is about land, but its language is about the table, and the sheds that burn are packing sheds. Canon's answer to the settlers is not suppression but arenas in which their virtues count (`CR-071` H6.4), and the kitchen is one of them. The interior's range beef, lamb, sourdough and smoked trout get the same standing as the coast's fish and rice: their own contests at the county fairs, their own marks (`A6`), their own masters (`A4`). The Office's second column (`A1`) is never a lecture to the settler about what he eats; the Realm's beef stays the Realm's pride. The quarrel does not end. It moves from the shed to the judging table, where the settlers win their share of the prizes.

**Neighbourhoods.** Inside each city the rivalry is local before it is regional: North Beach against Chinatown, Honolulu's Chinatown against the plantation towns, Sacramento's Japantown against its hotel kitchens. The Realm's restaurant guides, when they come (PROPOSED: the press estate's from the 1900s), rate by neighbourhood.

### A4. The cooks' agon (direction APPROVED, 06:58; the institutions PROPOSED)

Cooking in the Realm is a contest with published results, as engineering is.

- **Abroad, on the Meiji model.** From the 1880s the hotel houses, the railways' dining-car companies, the steamship lines and the cooks' own guilds send their best young cooks abroad on bonded fellowships, the trade's copy of the Polytechnic's travelling fellowships of 1854 (`L332 ("the first precedents and the travelling fellowships")`). They go to the London and Paris kitchens of Escoffier (*Hist.*: the Savoy from 1890, the Carlton from 1899, the Ritz in Paris from 1898; Escoffier as background), to Lyon, to the Basque gastronomic societies of San Sebastián (*Hist.*, to verify), to Bologna, to Canton and to the kaiseki kitchens of Kyoto. They come home bound to cook in the Realm for five years and to teach. The state pays for none of it.
- **Akiyama Tokuzō** (CT-AKIYAMA-TOKUZO, 1888–1974, *Hist.*) is the Japanese case of the same model and the Realm's measure of itself abroad. In our timeline he goes to Europe in 1909, works under Escoffier at the Ritz, has to fight in the kitchen against contempt for a Japanese cook, and returns in 1913 to be the Emperor's chef (*Hist.*). PROPOSED: in the Ritz kitchen his fellows include Pacifican cooks on their guild fellowships, two foreigners in a French brigade, rivals and allies; he goes home in 1913 by the Pacific route through San Francisco. From then on the Imperial Household's kitchen and the Realm's best kitchens measure themselves against each other, by menus printed at state dinners (*Hist.* for the imperial menu cards) and by the visitors each side receives.
- **Technique as engineering.** The Realm's kitchens take Escoffier's codification (*Le Guide culinaire*, 1903, *Hist.*) the way the Polytechnic takes a method: they write it down, weigh it and test it. Recipes are by weight, ovens by thermometer; the county grids carry electric cooking to almost every home by 1929 (`L81 ("county grids carry electric cooking")`). The work the author names is the work of the best kitchens: stocks that draw the savour out of bones, shells and kelp; emulsions that bind it with butter and cream; laminated pastry; long braises; contrasts of texture.
- **Ikeda Kikunae** (CT-IKEDA-KIKUNAE, 1864–1936, *Hist.*) isolates glutamate from kombu at Tokyo and names its taste umami in 1908 (*Hist.*). The Realm's chemists and cooks read the paper at once. PROPOSED: it starts the kitchen's best quarrel, "the powder or the pot". One school puts the crystalline seasoning in everything because it is honest chemistry. The other refuses it because a cook should draw the savour out of the ingredient by technique, and holds that a stock is only proved by tasting. A blind tasting at Carquinez, Ikeda present, decides nothing and is reported in every paper. Both schools keep their customers.
- **Proving and judgment.** The guilds examine for the title of master cook. The candidate cooks a set dinner before a jury that tastes blind, and the result is posted. At a master's proving the others fill the candidate's cup (`L887 ("The cup belongs to a thing done")`): the formal dinner is the earned occasion par excellence, never a display of rank. Counties and fairs hold blind competitions by region and by dish; the press estate prints the results; the Office prints the fair results in its county tables with everything else and comments on none of them. France's own examined title comes in 1924 (*Hist.*, background); the Realm's guild masters are a generation earlier (PROPOSED: from the trades congress of 1895, `L51 ("the trades congress of 1895")`).
- **Against the world.** The Realm's wines and cooks go to the great exhibitions. California wines took medals at Paris in our timeline too (*Hist.*, to verify which and when). A Pacifican kitchen measures its standing by what Paris, Tokyo and San Sebastián say of it, and by whether their cooks come to work in it.

### A5. The French and Spanish schools in the Realm (direction APPROVED, 06:58; particulars PROPOSED)

Two schools of technique set the tone of the Realm's best cooking, and both find ground here that suits them.

- **The French school: codified technique and the formal dinner.** Stocks, mother sauces and their emulsions, pastry, the brigade, the set sequence of courses. It is the language of the hotel kitchens, of the guild examinations and of state dinners. Butter and cream are for this table. Everyday cooking takes the method and leaves most of the butter, because the Realm's butter comes from irrigated alfalfa dairies and costs what that land costs.
- **The Spanish and Mediterranean school: honest ingredients, live fire, olive oil, time.** Little intervention: the best fish of the season, grilled; meat over oak; vegetables at their peak; olive oil in quantity, from the mission olives and the California groves of the 1870s onward (*Hist.*). New World staples (maize, beans, chile, tomato, potato) beside Old World curing. Time is the main ingredient: hams cured for two years, chorizo, Californio carne seca, Basque and Portuguese sausages, Japanese pickles, Chinese dried shellfish and cured pork, Deseret's bottled fruit.
- **Oak-savanna hams (PROPOSED).** The blue-oak and valley-oak savannas of the Coast Ranges and the Sierra foothills fatten hogs on acorns in autumn, as the Iberian dehesa does. The Realm's cured hams come from range that never enters the cropland account. They are among the first goods to be given a mark (`A6`).
- **Why this is still a lean table.** The French school's richest dishes are for occasions, and the Spanish school's everyday base of fish, oil, beans, vegetables and fruit is the leanest table in Europe. Together they are a reason for the second column, not against it.

### A6. The marks of origin (shape APPROVED, 07:16, "Sounds good"; the act's name and date and the particulars PROPOSED)

- **The act.** The Marks of Origin Act of 1897 (name and date PROPOSED) extends the principle of the Measures Act of 1866, which already holds the assay standard by which strength is measured (`L667 ("the assay standard")`): a label is a statement, and a false statement about a good is fraud.
- **Who writes the standard.** The producers of a district form a syndicate and write the rule for their mark: grape, yield, ageing, curing time, the hogs' feed. The mark is theirs.
- **Who draws the line.** The county commissioners register the mark's boundary on the county's survey, in public hearing, and anyone may object.
- **Who enforces it.** The courts, on complaint. The State Assay Office, the same that exposed the diamond field in 1872 (`L99 ("The State Assay Office exposes the diamond field")`), tests wines, oils, hams and cheeses on complaint and publishes what it finds. There is no subsidy, no quota and no inspector in the cellar.
- **What the Office does.** It prints the register of marks, the Assay Office's findings and the fair results.
- **Origin, not method.** French law comes to freeze a district's method (*Hist.*: the laws of 1905 and 1919, the national institute of 1935). The Realm's mark protects the place. Inside one county a syndicate of innovators may register a rival mark beside the classic one, and the two compete under their own names. Anyone inside the boundary who keeps a syndicate's rule may use its mark.
- **No borrowed names.** In our timeline California sold "Burgundy", "Sauterne" and "Champagne" (*Hist.*). The act forbids a foreign place-name on a Realm good. A Realm wine is named for its own valley or for nothing.
- **Beyond wine.** Olive oil, the oak-savanna hams, cheeses, Delta rice, Willamette cherries, Basque lamb from the interior, Kona coffee.
- **The crowns and the nations.** The Hawaiian Crown registers its own marks under its own law (Kona's cooperative mill is canon, `L1036 ("Coffee is the working day's drink")`). The homelands hold theirs under their own law, Diné churro mutton and Hopi blue corn among them, and the counties recognise them in return. The Philippine crown registers Batangas coffee first.
- **Abroad.** The Realm accedes to the Madrid Agreement of 1891 against false indications of source (*Hist.* for the agreement; the Union never signed it, *Hist.*; the Realm's accession and its date PROPOSED).
- **The first fight.** The boundary of the Napa mark, drawn about 1898–1900 on the model of France's Champagne riots of 1911 (*Hist.*): the valley's syndicate against the growers just outside the line it wants. It is settled in a county hearing, with the Assay Office's soil and wine reports in evidence, and the losers form a mark of their own.

### A7. Public health (historical anchors APPROVED, 21:40; the Realm's part PROPOSED)

1. **Beriberi.** In 1884 Takaki Kanehiro (CT-TAKAKI-KANEHIRO, 1849–1920, *Hist.*) proves on the training ship *Tsukuba*, sailing the route on which *Ryūjō* had lost 25 of 376 men to beriberi in 1882–83 with 169 sick, that barley, meat, fish, milk and vegetables beside the rice prevent it (*Hist.*). The Realm's naval surgeons read his tables in 1885 and visit Tokyo to see the ship's books; the navy and the merchant training ships adopt the ration on their own authority. No treaty is needed: the compact of 1887 is two years off and the Concord forty. The Japanese Army refuses the ration for a generation in our timeline (*Hist.*; Mori Ōgai as background); the Realm's surgeons watch the dispute and say nothing in public.
2. **No goitre belt in the interior.** In our timeline the Great Lakes, the mountain states and the Northwest are a goitre belt until iodised salt from 1924 (*Hist.*). In the Realm, kombu stock, sea fish carried inland by rail, and kelp meal in the interior's dairy and livestock feed keep endemic goitre down a generation early. Kaʻiulani's goitre of 1896 (`L850 ("an exophthalmic goitre")`) is a different disease and is unaffected.
3. **No endemic pellagra.** The rump's mill villages live with pellagra and hookworm (`L1129 ("pellagra and hookworm")`). The Realm's maize is eaten as masa, treated with lime, which frees its niacin (*Hist.*), beside fish, beans and rice. The Office prints both banks' tables and does not comment.

### A8. Traffic with Japan (PROPOSED)

- **From the Realm to Japan.** Citrus, raisins, walnuts, almonds, dried fruit, canned salmon and halibut, wines, Kona coffee for the kissaten of Tokyo and Osaka (`L1036`, "Japanese students who meet in the coffee rooms"), wheat flour for yōshoku. Fresh fruit crosses only once refrigerated steamers run, from the 1890s.
- **From Japan to the Realm.** Kombu and katsuobushi for every stock, shōyu and miso before the Realm brews its own, rice cultivars, Ikeda's seasoning from 1909 (*Hist.* for its sale in Japan).
- **Sapporo.** The college's dairy and Satō Shōsuke's (CT-SATO-SHOSUKE) cold-hardy rice exchange (`CR-085`) bring butter, cheese and condensed milk into Japan's western cooking; the Realm's cooks learn Hokkaido's kombu grades from the same exchange.

## C. Set pieces (candidates, not drafted)

1. **The *Tsukuba* ledgers (Tokyo, 1885; Takaki).** Takaki lays the sick lists of *Ryūjō* and *Tsukuba* side by side for two of the Realm's naval surgeons, over the barley-rice of the new ration. They are convinced. The army's surgeons in the next room are not.
2. **The return to Milwaukie (Oregon, about 1890; Ah Bing).** A Manchurian foreman steps off the steamer at Portland with his family's letters in his coat and walks back into Lewelling's rows. Nobody at the port asks him anything. That is the scene.
3. **The Ritz kitchen (Paris, about 1910; Akiyama).** A Japanese commis and a Pacifican commis on guild fellowships under Escoffier, both treated as intruders by the French brigade, fight the same men and then each other for the same station.
4. **The powder or the pot (Carquinez, about 1912; Ikeda).** A blind tasting of two consommés, one drawn from bone and kombu, one seasoned with the crystal. The jury splits; Ikeda declines to say which he prefers; every paper prints the score.
5. **The judging table (the Sacramento fair, about 1895).** A settler's range-beef barbecue against a Delta Chinese kitchen's roast duck in the same class, judged blind. The beef wins that year. The settlers who burned the sheds on the Feather nine years earlier are in the crowd and cheer their man; the duck's cook is back the next year and wins.
6. **The Napa hearing (about 1899).** The syndicate's map, the growers outside the line, the Assay Office's soil reports, the commissioners' vote, and the losers registering a mark of their own the same afternoon.
7. **Hayama (June 1932; Akiyama, Julian).** Between the sittings of the Council of the Two Crowns, Akiyama's kitchen gives the Emperor's dinner for Julian: French in its method, Japanese and Pacifican in its produce, a Napa wine with a mark beside a Kyoto course. Nobody mentions the treaty at table.
8. **Reading both columns (Thalassia, May 1932; Mead, Hoover, Keawe).** Hoover lays the second column on the committee table; Mead turns the page back to the first and asks in what year the margin closes on the Office's own measure.

- **T-DIET-1. A constant diet (`CR-082`) against the Realm's measured table.**

- **T-DIET-2. Which column is the cabinet's measure in 1930–32 (`L1702`, `L1892`).**
  - *Existing canon:* the century's plan "closes only by buying three-quarters or more"; the sufficers' case of 1930 and Mead's of 1932 rest on it.
  - *Case A (recommended):* the first column stays the Office's measure and the basis of the cabinet's two figures until a second census confirms the second (1940). The second is printed and argued over. `L1702` gains a clause; `L1892` is unchanged; Article II's irony is untouched.

- **T-SEA-1. The farmed sea's measure.**
  - *Case A:* the whole-diet line only.
  - *Case B (APPROVED, 21:40):* two lines from 1932; the second reads the same tonnage as protein and is never added to the first (`A2`).

- **T-MARK-1. Marks of origin against the open licence (`CR-057`).**
  - *Existing canon:* the Polytechnic's patents are licensed openly at one royalty and concessions are auctioned; the Realm distrusts exclusive rights.
  - *Case A (recommended):* no conflict. A mark is a true statement of where a good comes from, open to every producer inside the boundary who keeps a syndicate's rule; rival marks may stand in one county; nothing about method is reserved.
