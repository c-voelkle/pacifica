## The author's request

> In CR-088, we also discussed how fashions were more influenced by Japan in this timeline. Pacifica, as the pinnacle of modernity, has a fashion that integrates elements of Japanese fashion. To what extent does this translate to a higher than historical demand for silk? Is it conceivable that this would have an impact beyond Pacifica's borders as well, in countries that are, consciously or not, trying to emulate Pacifica? (28 November 2026, 08:54)

> I think it's hard to avoid the fibre being ready by the 1920s. The framing should be that artificial silk, and not so much missing demand, is what hurts Japan's silk villages. CR-090-R1 should also explain how Japan's newly successful Rayon and Nylon factories help to bridge the gap for the Concord rather than becoming a target for populist anti-Concord sentiment. (28 November 2026, 09:04)

## A. The change

**A1. The silk boom Pacifica makes (1905–1928) (`APPROVED`, R-5, 09:24).** The Pacific wardrobe of `CR-088` is a silk wardrobe at the top: the *haori* in raw silk, Honolulu's *chirimen* shirts, the couture that runs on Kyoto and Yokohama silk (`L1689 ("couture runs on Kyoto and Yokohama silk")`), and silk stockings, the largest and fastest-growing use of American raw silk by the late 1920s and about three-quarters of it by the late 1930s (*Hist.*). Pacifica is the richest market in North America, and its per-head silk use runs at about one and a half to two times the historical American rate (story assumption, MODEL). From the press estate's films and newsreels the style travels: Japan's own city women wear the Pacific line in Japanese silk instead of Western wool and cotton, and Paris, which ignored San Francisco until the late 1920s (`CR-088` `A3.7`), gives it a season about 1928–30. Japan's mulberry spreads further than in our timeline, where it already covered a tenth of the farmland by 1929 (*Hist.*). More villages live by the cocoon, and live by it more deeply.

**A2. The fibre (`APPROVED`, R-1, R-2, 09:24; R-6, 09:30).** Pacifica's chemistry runs from naval powder to electrochemistry and plastics (`L1174 ("Chemicals develop from licensed dynamite")`), and from 1923 Tacoma makes synthetic rubber from carbide and acetylene (`L1605 ("Tacoma makes synthetic rubber from carbide and acetylene")`). Two fibres follow:
- *Rayon, cheaper and better.* Rayon is historical: Japan's first maker dates from 1918 and the first commercial yarn was spun at Yonezawa in 1915 (*Hist.*). In the Realm the Columbia's electrochemistry makes its caustic soda cheap, and the Rainier Pulp and Paper Company, a San Francisco firm with its mill at Shelton on Puget Sound from 1927, makes dissolving pulp from western hemlock (*Hist.*; here a Pacifican company inside the Realm). Rayon takes the cheap stocking, lingerie and lining trade some years early; it never matches silk in the full-fashioned stocking (*Hist.*).
- *The polyamide.* Wallace Carothers (born 1896; PhD Illinois 1924, *Hist.*; background, not registered) comes to the Polytechnic in 1926, by attraction, as Pacifica's other scientists come, to the polymer laboratory beside Tacoma's acetylene rubber, instead of going to Harvard (*Hist.*: Harvard 1926, DuPont 1928). His group draws a polyamide fibre in 1928, seven years before his historical nylon of 1935. The intermediates and melt spinning take the next years, as they did historically (1935–40): a small first run of stockings sells out in the Realm in 1929, and volume comes in 1930–31 (historically May 1940). The first intermediates are made by Pacifican firms from the Columbia's residue hydrogenation and Tacoma's carbide, which turns to chemicals in 1929. The patent is licensed at the one published royalty (`CR-057`), to anyone: Japanese houses, the Union's mills and Europe's alike. Pacifica's chemists call it *amide*, for what it is, and the Realm's women buy "amides" (R-6). Under the Truth of Labels Act a cloth's label must name its fibre truly (an extension of `CR-089`'s rule, PROPOSED), so nothing is sold in the Realm as "artificial silk", and Toyo Rayon's stockings are labelled amide there; at home and in China they are Amilan, from about 1930 (*Hist.*: Toyo Rayon's nylon trade name, chosen in the early 1940s).

**A3. How the fibre hits the villages (`APPROVED`, R-1, 09:24; the grades revised after review, `PROPOSED`, R-7).** The stocking was silk's mass market, and the fibre takes it. From 1930 the Realm's women switch to amide stockings, which are sheerer and last longer; cheaper rayon takes the low end; the Union's mills license the fibre from 1930–31. The fall of the cocoon is therefore structural, not only cyclical: the Union's depression deepens it (`L1759 ("Japan's silk districts lose income")`), but the stocking trade does not come back when demand recovers, and the yen's reset of July 1932 cannot restore it. The stocking trade bought Japan's most even, best-reeled export silk (*Hist.*: Japanese filature silk won the American market on hosiery), so the fibre takes the best buyers first. Silk keeps the cloth, where Pacifican fashion still wants it: the couture, the *haori*, *chirimen*, Nishijin and the kimono trade at home, at a lower price. The export filatures of Nagano, Gunma and Yamanashi lose their best buyers, and the cocoon falls in every village that sold to them, the Tōhoku villages that send them cocoons and girls among them. The daughters are sold where the cocoon was the cash (`CR-090` `A2.6`), and the gold parity is what turns the fall into debt they cannot carry.

**A4. Japan's mills as the bridge (`APPROVED`, R-3, 09:24).**
- *Japanese mills, by Japanese decision.* The Hayama annex gives Japan the Concord's textiles (`L1654 ("Japan supplies textiles, its own nitrogen")`), and the open royalty means nobody's permission is needed. The rayon makers are Japan's own and historical (*Hist.*): Teikoku Jinken, from Yonezawa, building at Iwakuni from 1927; Mitsui's Toyo Rayon (1926); Noguchi's Asahi works, with Glanzstoff since 1922 and Bemberg's process from 1929 (Nobeoka from 1931); Ōhara Magosaburō's Kurashiki Kenshoku (1926). They license the polyamide in 1930–31. Pacifican firms sell them what the Realm is good at and the annex allows: dissolving pulp from Puget Sound, caustic soda and, in the first years, the polyamide's intermediates from the Columbia. From about 1931 Noguchi's works make the intermediates from their own ammonia and coke-oven benzene (PROPOSED), so the fibre gives Thalassia no new hold on Japan. Japan spins, weaves and knits.
- *The fight in the Diet (`PROPOSED`, R-8).* In the session of 1930–31 the Seiyūkai's farm members and the Yokohama raw-silk houses, with Katakura behind them, ask for an excise on the fibre and a ban on its sale in the home market. Noguchi and Ōhara answer that if Japan does not spin the fibre, Reading and Coventry will, and Japan will lose the stocking trade and the mills as well. Gunze, a silk reeler, crosses over and knits amide stockings at Ayabe (*Hist.*: Gunze turned to hosiery). The Diet rejects the excise and the ban and passes the silk members' price instead: a fibre mill on prefectural or cooperative power hires first from the silk prefectures' laid-off reelers.
- *The mills hire the reelers.* The spinning works stay on the coast, by the ports and the water (Iwakuni, Ōtsu, Nobeoka); the knitting and weaving mills go to the silk districts on rural hydro (`CR-088` `A5.4`), Okaya and Suwa among them, as the habutae weavers of Fukui and Ishikawa turned to rayon (*Hist.*). They take on laid-off reeling girls, whose hands already know thread. Ōhara's Kurashiki model of company housing, clinics and schooling for women operatives (*Hist.*) becomes the pattern the Home Ministry asks of the rest.
- *The dollars.* Raw silk was Japan's great earner of gold dollars. Japanese rayon and polyamide cloth and stockings, sold to the Realm under the annex, to China and to the Union's market, earn a growing part of it back. The quota is payable in gold dollars (`L1842 ("The quota is payable in gold dollars")`), and so are the last instalments to San Francisco; the fibre's exports are what keep that burden bearable before and after the reset. This is the gap the mills bridge for the Concord.
- *Japan's own fibre.* At Kyoto Imperial University, Sakurada Ichirō's group makes a polyvinyl-alcohol fibre from carbide about 1930–31, eight or nine years before its historical date of 1939 (*Hist.*), on the acetylene of Noguchi's carbide works (`L1412 ("Noguchi Shitagau, who founded Nippon Chisso in 1908")`). It is coarse, more like cotton than silk, but it is Japan's own, and the papers say so.
- *Why the mills are not the target.* The Restoration officers and Gondō say that the Concord's chemistry eats the silkworm, and the charge has force in the hill villages. It does not stick to the mills. They are Japanese-owned, they took the reelers' daughters in, and their exports pay Japan's way. Their owners, Noguchi and Ōhara in front, speak the language of national strength, not of the Concord, and the silk members wrote the hiring rule themselves. The anger in the villages goes where `CR-090` already sends it: to the gold rule, the moneylenders, the great banks and the Concord's parity, not to the mill gate.

**A5. The fleet's silk (`APPROVED`, R-4, 09:24; amount MODEL).** The common fleet's forward silk order of 1931–32 (`L1773 ("buy two years' parachute and powder-bag silk")`) is for powder bags only. Powder bags must be wholly consumed in the breech, which silk is and the new fibres are not yet (*Hist.* for silk cartridge cloth), so the order buys what the fleet needs anyway, two years early. Cartridge cloth is spun from waste silk, the filatures' floss and the pierced and double cocoons that will not reel (*Hist.*), so the order keeps Japan's spun-silk mills at work and pays something for the cocoons the filatures cannot use. The air arm moves its canopies to the amide on its own schedule, and the order does not hold it back. About $1.7 million over 1931–32 for about 700 tonnes of cartridge cloth, a war reserve of several years' peacetime use, Pacifica paying 65 per cent, about $1.1 million (MODEL). This closes `CR-090`'s OPEN amount for the silk; the nitrogen credit's stays OPEN.

## Canon tensions

**T-SILK-1. What empties the silk villages (`L1761`, `CR-090`, APPROVED; `L1842`, `CR-085`, PROPOSED).** Canon names the Union's depression as the cause.
- *Case A, canon kept:* the fibre is added beside the depression as a second cause.
- *Case B, canon amended:* the fibre is the main and lasting cause, the depression deepens it, and the reset cannot undo it.
- *Recommendation:* Case B, which is the author's direction of 09:04. No tracked fact changes.

**T-SILK-2. The Realm's own textiles under the Hayama annex (`L1654`).** The annex has Japan supply the Concord's textiles.
- *Case A:* the Realm makes the polymer and its intermediates, and Japanese mills spin, weave and knit for the Realm; Union and European mills license the fibre for their own markets.
- *Case B:* the Realm also spins and knits its own stockings.
- *Recommendation:* Case A. The annex bars Pacifican textiles only from Japan's home market, so Case A is a choice, not the annex's letter: it makes the mills Japan's and the Realm their best customer.

## Sources

- **Silk uses and the stocking trade:** three-quarters of American raw silk went to stockings (Science History Institute, *Distillations*, "Synthetic Threads"); American silk "predominately used for ladies' hosiery" (Arnold, UCSB labor history paper); a tenth of Japan's farmland in mulberry by 1929 (Old Photos of Japan, after Ma 1996).
- **Silk grades:** American hosiery mills bought the most even, top-grade Japanese filature silk, coarse and hand-reeled grades going to cloth and the home market (Federico, *An Economic History of the Silk Industry*; Ma 1996); Gunze's turn to hosiery (Gunze history).
- **Japanese rayon:** first commercial yarn at Yonezawa, 1915, and Teikoku Jinzo Kenshi, 1918 (Encyclopedia.com, "Teijin Limited"; the-shashi.com, Teijin history); Iwakuni works 1927 (FundingUniverse, Teijin); Toyo Rayon 1926 (Encyclopedia.com, "Toray Industries"; Toray, *90 Years*); Japan's rayon yarn output the world's highest in 1936 (Encyclopedia.com, "Teijin Limited"); Toyo Rayon's trade name Amilan for its nylon, chosen in the early 1940s (Toray history, his_1959_03 and *90 Years*); Vereinigte Glanzstoff's 1922 partnership with Noguchi and Asahi (Wikipedia, "Vereinigte Glanzstoff-Fabriken"); Nihon Bemberg Kenshoku (1929) and Nobeoka (1931) (Asahi Kasei history); Kurashiki Kenshoku 1926, Ōhara's housing and clinics for women operatives and the Institute for the Science of Labour (Kuraray history; the-shashi.com, Kurabo); Sakurada's polyvinyl-alcohol fibre at Kyoto Imperial University, historically 1939 (Kuraray history, 1926–1950s).
- **Pulp:** Rainier Pulp and Paper Company, incorporated 26 April 1926 with executive offices in San Francisco and its mill at Shelton, Washington, operating from July 1927, dissolving pulp from western hemlock (Thomson's *Manual of Pacific Northwest Finance*, 1929; RYAM history; Wikipedia, "Rayonier").
- **Carothers and nylon:** born 1896, PhD Illinois 1924, Harvard 1926, DuPont 1928; nylon 1935; stockings sold nationally in May 1940 (Science History Institute; ACS National Historic Chemical Landmark; Wikipedia, "Wallace Carothers"); nylon from benzene-derived adipic acid and hexamethylenediamine (Encyclopedia.com, "Carothers, Wallace").
- **Cartridge cloth:** silk powder bags were used because they were completely consumed on firing (MFA Boston, CAMEO, "Cartridge cloth"); the fleet stockpiled silk for powder bags and parachutes in 1941 (Utah Digital Newspapers).
