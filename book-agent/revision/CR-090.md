## A. The change

### A1. Meiji Constitutional Balance Under Pacifica's Shadow (1871–1912) (`APPROVED` core; particulars `PROPOSED`)

**1. The 1889 Meiji Constitution stands intact (`APPROVED`).**
Canon already establishes that in 1871 Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi read Pacifica's Articles of 1856 and refuse its confederate form (`L751 ("Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi")`), and that in 1932 Japan still operates under the **1889 Meiji Constitution**: a non-voting cabinet (`L1816 ("The officers kill Inukai on 15 May")`), an advisory Privy Council where **Hiranuma Kiichirō** (`CT-HIRANUMA-KIICHIRO`) enters formal dissents (`L1828`), the *genrō* (**Saionji Kinmochi**, `CT-SAIONJI`), active-duty service ministers, and a bicameral Imperial Diet (*Teikoku Gikai*, House of Representatives and House of Peers). Pacifica never pushes constitutional reform onto Tokyo.

Yet inside that unchanged constitutional text, **four structural divergences** shift Japan's political center of gravity away from our timeline's military-oligarchic lock:
- **Early treaty equality removes the 1880s–90s ultranationalist fuel:** Pacifica's 1870–71 equal alliance (`L749 ("1870–1871 Japanese alliance")`) and 1887 backing for European treaty revision (`L993 ("compact with Tokyo is signed")`) settle tariff autonomy and consular courts years earlier than in our timeline (`CR-088` `A2.3`). The *Jiyū Minken Undō* (Freedom and People's Rights Movement) and early Diet parties (*Jiyūtō*, *Kaishintō*, later *Seiyūkai*) argue over budgets, taxes, and railway patronage rather than fighting cabinets accused of humiliating treaty concessions.
- **State Shinto, the 1890 Imperial Rescript on Education, and *kokutai* (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 1, `PROPOSED`):** As in our timeline, the Meiji state separates civic shrine rites from sectarian preaching (1882), promulgates the **Imperial Rescript on Education** (October 1890), and establishes the Home Ministry's Shrine Bureau (1900) (*Hist.*; see `CR-088` `A4.5`). Because Japan faces neither Christian missionary extraterritoriality nor our timeline's existential diplomatic humiliations (no 1895 Triple Intervention, no 1905 Portsmouth humiliation), official *kokutai* teaching settles into **conservative civic loyalty and filial ethics** rather than paranoid spiritual mobilization. Outside Japan's borders, Tokyo's Home Ministry still chafes when Pacifican courts classify overseas shrines as voluntary churches (`CR-088` G29), but inside Japan the Rescript coexists comfortably with constitutional law and technical learning.
- **No 1905 Hibiya Riot (`L1236`):** Because Victoria advises Tokyo to refuse Roosevelt's mediation and Russia negotiates directly at Urajio — paying roughly **$300 million in indemnity** across 1906–10 (`L1236 ("Russian indemnity payments")`, `MODEL`) — Tokyo suffers **no September 1905 Hibiya Park anti-treaty riot**. The first great eruption of populist nationalist street violence that taught our timeline's Japanese politics to fear angry imperial crowds never happens.
- **The Army's structural shift after 1894 (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 3, `PROPOSED`):** Under the 1887 compact and 1907 renewal, the **Imperial Japanese Navy** is the cosmopolitan senior service integrated with Pacifica's yards, fleet, and the Kuroda house. Meanwhile, Yamagata Aritomo's Chōshū Army **never acquires colonial Governor-Generalships in Formosa** (Chinese under `CR-081`) **or Korea** (independent and guaranteed under `L1234 ("Korea remains an independent")`), which in our timeline served as autonomous Army fiefdoms and stepping-stones to the premiership. Instead, the Army's prestige and garrison politics center on the **northern/Siberian frontier** (`Enkaishū` and `Karafuto` gained in 1898–99, `CR-088` `A5.10`), nursing a quiet, persistent grievance that Pacifica stops Japan's mainland wars early (1894, `L1086 ("shortened victory")`; 1915's Twenty-One Demands cut back without Group Five; 1930's end of consular courts in China; 1931's halt in Manchuria).

### A2. Taishō Politics, 1918 Without Rice Riots, and the Narrower 1925 Security Settlement (1912–1927) (`PROPOSED`)

**1. Summer 1918: price friction without a shortage or riots (closes `CR-085` Unresolved #41 & `CR-088` hand-off 4, `PROPOSED`).**
In our timeline, soaring rice prices and physical hoarding sparked the nationwide **July–September 1918 Rice Riots (*Kome Sōdō*)**, toppling Terauchi Masatake's military cabinet and leaving elites terrified of mass disorder (*Hist.*). In canon, Hokkaido's early paddy, Manchurian bean cake, and Nichitsu's hydro-nitrogen keep Japan's 1918–20 rice crop 8% above our timeline's (`CR-085`, `L1738 ("keeps pace with its people to 1932")`). Physical supply holds, so **no famine panic or nationwide Rice Riots break out and no cabinet falls to street mobs**. Wartime freight congestion and Siberian expedition procurement still produce sharp summer 1918 price spikes and brief dockside protests in Toyama and Osaka; municipal grain releases and railway freight caps defuse them within weeks. When **Hara Takashi** becomes Japan's first commoner party prime minister in autumn 1918 (*Hist.*), his cabinet rests on Diet party strength and Saionji's constitutional counsel rather than emergency riot management.

**2. Two rival party coalitions — and their Concord paradox (`PROPOSED`; `Wakatsuki` and `Shidehara` registered under `4a`).**
By the mid-1920s, party cabinets are ordinary practice in Tokyo, rotating between two major parties whose economic doctrines create an ironic split over Pacifica:
- ***Kenseikai* / *Minseitō* (urban commerce, trading houses, Mitsubishi ties):** Led by **Wakatsuki Reijirō** (`CT-WAKATSUKI-REIJIRO`, 1866–1949; PM 1926–27 and April–December 1931, *Hist.*) and **Shidehara Kijūrō** (`CT-SHIDEHARA-KIJURO`, 1872–1951; Foreign Minister 1924–27 and 1929–31, *Hist.*). They are the natural commercial internationalists of the Concord (`L1650 ("Wakatsuki and Shidehara call it")`, `L1800 ("Japan’s national army plots")`), defending open trade, China's sovereignty, and civilian restraint over the generals. Their vulnerability is monetary orthodoxy: defending rigid gold parity with San Francisco drags rural prices down while farm debts stay fixed (`L1612 ("At gold parity Japan")`).
- ***Rikken Seiyūkai* (provincial landlords, infrastructure, domestic industry):** Splits cleanly down the middle between its pragmatic financial stewards — **Takahashi Korekiyo** (`CT-TAKAHASHI`) and **Inukai Tsuyoshi** (`CT-INUKAI-TSUYOSHI`), who accept the Concord while fighting inside it for monetary flexibility and rural relief — and its hardline nationalist floor wing led by **Mori Kaku** (`CT-MORI-KAKU`), who denounces indefinite renewal as *"the bakufu made permanent"* (`L1826 ("Mori Kaku of the Seiyūkai")`).

**3. 1925: Universal Manhood Suffrage without a Bolshevik Scare (`PROPOSED`).**
As in our timeline, the Imperial Diet abolishes tax qualifications in **1925**, enacting **Universal Manhood Suffrage** for men aged twenty-five and older (*Hist.*, expanding the electorate roughly fourfold to over twelve million voters). In our timeline, conservative terror of the newly established Soviet Union forced the **Peace Preservation Law (*Chian Ijihō*)** through the same session, empowering the *Tokkō* (Special Higher Police) to crush leftists, unions, and eventually liberal professors (*Hist.*). In canon, **Lenin and Trotsky were executed in 1920 and Russia is a federal republic under Wrangel (`CR-041`, `CR-044`) — there is no Soviet Union and no Comintern**. The Diet still passes a **narrower 1925 Public Order Statute** criminalizing violent conspiracy against the state or imperial house (the very statute later cited alongside military law against the 15 May plotters), but without our timeline's sweeping ideological thought-policing (*tenkō* purges). Mainstream labour unions, tenant-farmer associations, liberal newspapers, and university law faculties operate in the open through 1932.

### A3. The Arc of Japanese Public Opinion Toward the Concord (1887–1932) (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 2, `PROPOSED`)

Japanese public opinion toward Pacifica is never monolithic cheerleading and never uniform hostility. It moves through **four generational phases** (`L753 ("Iwakura Mission")`, `L1258 ("Julian spends a year in Tokyo")`, `L1633 ("Hirohito becomes Emperor Shōwa")`, `L1641 ("stop paying abroad")`):
1. **1871–1907 — Respectful Utility & Hard Bargaining:** Publicly seen as the one Western power that never humiliated Japan, checked in 1906–07 when Tokyo's hard bargain over Sacramento's schools proves Japan is an equal partner rather than a client (`CR-088` `A5.7`).
2. **1908–1926 — Everyday Tangibility:** Julian's 1907 marriage to Lady Chiyo, Japanese citizens prospering in Hawaiʻi and California, early AC rural hydro grids (`CR-088` `A5.4`), and **Julian's 1923 Great Kantō Earthquake relief ships** turn a diplomatic treaty into lived familiarity across Tokyo and Yokohama.
3. **Spring 1927 — The Banking Panic & the Concord Vote (`L1633`, `L1641`, `L1643`):** When London's acceptance houses pull out first and depositors queue outside Tokyo banks, the daily press (*Ōsaka Asahi*, *Tōkyō Nichinichi*, *Jiji Shimpō*) contrasts British abandonment with San Francisco's $600 million stabilization credit. When the Emperor ratifies the provisional Concord on the Privy Council's conditional advice and the Diet votes its quota (`L1643 ("Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council")`), urban opinion accepts it with sober relief rather than euphoria — reassured by the visible domestic firewalls (`L1654 ("Japan grants English no domestic")`) and the five-year first term.
4. **1930–1932 — Four Constituencies Under Strain:** By the eve of 15 May 1932, Japanese public opinion divides along four clear lines:
   - **The Urban "Pacific Generation" (pro-Concord majority):** Salaried commuters, engineers, shipyard/aircraft workers at Nagoya and Ōta, port merchants, *kissaten* patrons, and cinema audiences (`CR-088`, `CR-089`). They want the Concord stabilized and the 1937 aluminium/electrical bar lifted on schedule (`CR-085`).
   - **The Court–Navy–Finance Establishment (pragmatic stewards):** Saionji, Saitō, Takahashi, Wakatsuki, Shidehara, and the naval treaty faction. They treat the Concord as an indispensable financial and maritime shield whose terms must be bargained hard every five years.
   - **Anxious Rural Silk & Rice Villages (swing constituency, not revolutionary):** Protected from famine and colonial rice dumping (`CR-085`) and cushioned by village *Sangyō Kumiai* electric workshops (`CR-088` `A5.4`), they nonetheless feel the bite of gold-parity deflation (`L1612`) and the 1930–32 American silk slump. They sympathize with Gondō Seikyō's village lament, cheer Takahashi's July 1932 yen reset, and **refuse to rise for a military coup**.
   - **The Restoration Right & Hardline Officer Circles (vocal minority):** Army continentalists (`CT-ARAKI-SADAO`), fleet-faction holdouts remembering Kronstadt (`CR-088` `A5.9`), Seiyūkai hardliners (`CT-MORI-KAKU`), and *kokutai* absolutists (`CT-HIRANUMA-KIICHIRO`), echoed in nationalist journals and *Gen'yōsha*/*Kokuryūkai* societies (and radical pamphleteers like Kita Ikki in the background). They frame Chichibu's seat at Thalassia (`L1715 ("new bakufu")`), the 1930 surrender of China consular courts, and the 1931 Manchuria halt as a velvet subordination — a charge that gives them lethal passion on 15 May, but never a mass electoral majority.

### A4. Constitutional Orthodoxy, Press Space, and Endemic Women's Municipal Suffrage (1920s–1932) (`APPROVED` core & boundary; particulars `PROPOSED`)

**1. Minobe Tatsukichi's *"Emperor Organ Theory"* (*Tennō Kikansetsu*) holds as mainstream orthodoxy (`APPROVED`, 1a).**
In our timeline, Tokyo Imperial University jurist **Minobe Tatsukichi** (*Hist.* 1873–1948; background, not registered) taught for thirty years that under the Meiji Constitution the state is the sovereign legal person and the Emperor is its highest constitutional organ — the intellectual foundation of party government until militarists purged him in 1935 (*Hist.*). In canon, **Minobe's theory never falls**. When Restoration officers invoke "direct imperial rule" on 15 May 1932 to justify murdering Prime Minister Inukai, Emperor Shōwa himself refuses the claim — declaring by his stand (`L1822 ("stand in May is his own")`) that officers cannot bypass his lawful government in his name (`L1816`). With Saionji, Saitō, and the throne aligned behind constitutional restraint, Hiranuma Kiichirō's absolutist *kokutai* dissent in the Privy Council (`L1828`) remains a recorded minority objection rather than state dogma.

**2. Press and broadcasting (`PROPOSED`).**
Major Japanese dailies (*Ōsaka Asahi*, *Tōkyō Nichinichi*, *Jiji Shimpō*) and Japan's public radio network (JOAK, launched 1925, *Hist.*) debate the Concord's renewal, the yen parity, and the Hayama industrial bar vigorously and openly in June–July 1932 — contrasting sharply with our timeline's tightening post-Manchurian censorship. Shortwave news relays and Honolulu–Tokyo talking newsreels (`CR-078`, `CR-079`) make both publics visible to each other in real time, amplifying both mutual familiarity and mutual irritation.

**3. Endemic Japanese Women's Municipal & Prefectural Suffrage — Enacted March 1931 (`APPROVED`, 2a + author's explicit boundary).**
- **Strict canon boundary (`APPROVED`, author 27 November 2026, 15:25):** Neither **Victoria Kamāmalu** nor **Princess Kaʻiulani** ever acts as a "suffragette," adviser, or missionary for women's suffrage in Japan. Any Pacifican lecturing on Japan's franchise would strike the wrong footing, hand nationalist critics an immediate interference charge, and strain the Concord. Just as Pacifica keeps an absolute firewall on Shinto (`CR-088` `A4.5`), Japanese constitutional franchise is exclusively Japan's own affair.
- **How suffrage emerges endemically inside a modernizing Japan (`PROPOSED` mechanism):** By 1930, Japan's own internal transformation has reshaped women's daily civic weight:
  1. Near-universal girls' elementary literacy and rapid growth in girls' high schools, women's normal schools, and women's colleges (*Hist.*: Tsuda Umeko's college, Japan Women's University, Tokyo Woman's Christian University).
  2. Millions of wage-earning women in Japan's silk-reeling and textile export backbone (`CR-054`), electric-powered rural *Sangyō Kumiai* workshops (`CR-088` `A5.4`), urban department stores, telephone exchanges, clinics, and clerical offices.
  3. Indigenous Japanese civic organizing — from the New Women's Association (*Shin Fujin Kyōkai*, winning repeal of Article 5's ban on women attending political meetings in 1922, *Hist.*) to **Ichikawa Fusae**'s Women's Suffrage League (*Fusen Kakutoku Dōmei*, founded 1924, *Hist.*; background, not registered) and municipal mothers', consumer, and public-health leagues petitioning city halls over water, sanitation, and school budgets.
- **The March 1931 enactment (`PROPOSED`):** In our timeline, the Minseitō cabinet's **Municipal Women's Suffrage Bill** passed the Lower House in February 1931 before the House of Peers killed it 184–62 in March 1931, and the Manchurian Incident six months later buried reform (*Hist.*). Here, with no Bolshevik panic hardening the upper house, no Manchurian war fever, and steady rural-township civic participation, **the House of Peers passes the compromise Municipal and Prefectural Women's Suffrage Act in March 1931** (granting women aged twenty-five and older the vote and eligibility for city, town, village, and prefectural assemblies, with conservative Peers securing a phased spousal-consent review clause for married candidates that reformers immediately campaign to drop). Japanese women cast their **first legal local ballots across prefectural and municipal elections in autumn 1931 and spring 1932** — a visibly proud, wholly homegrown Japanese milestone, while **national Diet suffrage** remains an active, vigorous campaign in 1932.

### A5. The Legal & Political Reckoning of 15 May 1932: Yokosuka, Tokyo, and Stern Sentences (closes `CR-085` Unresolved #41 at `L1828` & `L2081`) (`APPROVED`, 3a)

On 15 May 1932, Restoration officers assassinate Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi and attempt to seize Japanese-crewed carriers of the Common Navy's western squadron, foiled when **Kuroda Kiyotada** (`CT-KURODA-SON`), warned by Maria Kashiwagi, holds the fleet loyal (`L1814 ("Restoration officers strike against Inukai")`). Commander Kashiwagi and the naval mutineers are arrested within the week (`L1816`).

**The Jurisdictional Question (`L1828 ("which court tries the naval officers")`, `L2081 ("which court tries the naval officers")`):**
Hardliners in Thalassia — among them Samuel Wilder King on the General Staff — ask whether mutiny aboard Common Navy vessels should be tried by a Concord court-martial. **Julian and Saitō Makoto refuse.** Acting on Saitō's governing principle (*"the army is to be mastered by Japan, as it was in May, or it has not been mastered"*, `L1822`), both capitals agree on strict **Japanese national jurisdiction**:
1. **Yokosuka Naval District Court-Martial (Japanese naval law):** Tries Commander Kashiwagi and the Japanese Common Navy officers involved in the carrier plot under the Imperial Japanese Naval Penal Code for mutiny and conspiracy. By reciprocal Concord courtesy, **a single Pacifican naval judge-advocate sits in the gallery as an observer** — with **no vote, no bench seat, and no Thalassian appellate authority**.
2. **Tokyo District Court & First Division Army Court-Martial (Japanese law):** Try Inukai's direct assassins and the army/civilian conspirators under Japanese criminal, military, and 1925 Public Order statutes.
3. **Why it matters politically:** Trying Japanese officers in Japanese courts strips **Mori Kaku** and **Hiranuma Kiichirō** of the single most explosive charge they hoped for — *"extraterritoriality restored in uniform."*
4. **Why the verdicts break our timeline's fatal precedent (`APPROVED` stern sentences; timing `PROPOSED`):** In our timeline, the 1933–34 May 15 trials degenerated into theatrical platforms for ultra-nationalist speeches; flooded by hundreds of thousands of clemency petitions, Japanese military courts handed down scandalously light prison terms (most assassins paroled within a few years), signalling to junior officers that political murder for "pure motives" carried minimal penalty (*Hist.*). In canon, **three barriers prevent that collapse**:
   - Emperor Shōwa's unmistakable public stand against officers who act in his name (`L1816`, `L1822`);
   - Saitō Makoto's cabinet and the loyal naval leadership (soon joined by Kuroda Kiyotada as Common Navy Minister on 1 August) insulating the Yokosuka bench from Restoration pressure; and
   - An intact, open national press (*Ōsaka Asahi*, *Tōkyō Nichinichi*) and energized party leadership refusing to romanticize the murder of an elected premier.

   Preliminary indictments and the first court-martial sittings open at **Yokosuka and Tokyo in summer–autumn 1932** under closed courtroom rules against political speeches, culminating in **uncommuted capital and life sentences for Inukai's direct murderers and long penal servitude with cashiering for Kashiwagi and the carrier mutineers** (final sentencing running into 1933, beyond the book's August 1932 horizon, with the firm no-clemency posture already unmistakable by July 1932).
