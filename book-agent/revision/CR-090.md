## A. The change

### A1. Meiji Constitutional Balance Under Pacifica's Shadow (1871–1912) (`APPROVED` core; particulars `PROPOSED`)

**1. The 1889 Meiji Constitution stands intact (`APPROVED`).**
Canon already establishes that in 1871 Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi read Pacifica's Articles of 1856 and refuse its confederate form (`L751 ("Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi")`), and that in 1932 Japan still operates under the **1889 Meiji Constitution**: a non-voting cabinet (`L1816 ("The officers kill Inukai on 15 May")`), an advisory Privy Council where **Hiranuma Kiichirō** (`CT-HIRANUMA-KIICHIRO`) enters formal dissents (`L1828`), the *genrō* (**Saionji Kinmochi**, `CT-SAIONJI`), active-duty service ministers, and a bicameral Imperial Diet (*Teikoku Gikai*, House of Representatives and House of Peers). Pacifica never pushes constitutional reform onto Tokyo.

Yet inside that unchanged constitutional text, **four structural divergences** shift Japan's political center of gravity away from our timeline's military-oligarchic lock:
- **Early treaty equality removes the 1880s–90s ultranationalist fuel:** Pacifica's 1870–71 equal alliance (`L749 ("1870–1871 Japanese alliance")`) and 1887 backing for European treaty revision (`L993 ("compact with Tokyo is signed")`) settle tariff autonomy and consular courts years earlier than in our timeline (`CR-088` `A2.3`). The *Jiyū Minken Undō* (Freedom and People's Rights Movement) and early Diet parties (*Jiyūtō*, *Kaishintō*, later *Seiyūkai*) argue over budgets, taxes, and railway patronage rather than fighting cabinets accused of humiliating treaty concessions.
- **State Shinto, the 1890 Imperial Rescript on Education, and the Emperor's silence on radio (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 1; microphone firewall `APPROVED`, 16:17):** As in our timeline, the Meiji state separates civic shrine rites from sectarian preaching (1882), promulgates the **Imperial Rescript on Education** (October 1890), and establishes the Home Ministry's Shrine Bureau (1900) (*Hist.*; see `CR-088` `A4.5`). Because Japan faces neither Christian missionary extraterritoriality nor our timeline's existential diplomatic humiliations (no 1895 Triple Intervention, no 1905 Portsmouth humiliation), official *kokutai* teaching settles into **conservative civic loyalty and filial ethics** rather than paranoid spiritual mobilization. When voice radio arrives a decade early (`A2`, `A4`), **Emperor Shōwa keeps the court's sacred firewall of silence on public broadcast microphones** — speaking to the public only through Imperial Rescripts read on air by the premier or grand chamberlain (mirroring Victoria Kamāmalu's own refusal of the microphone at `L1203 ("Victoria never sings again")`, and contrasting with Julian's public broadcasts and their single newspaper-noted 1928 radiotelephone call at `L1679 ("Thalassia telephones Tokyo")`).
- **No 1905 Hibiya Riot (`L1236`):** Because Victoria advises Tokyo to refuse Roosevelt's mediation and Russia negotiates directly at Urajio — paying roughly **$300 million in indemnity** across 1906–10 (`L1236 ("Russian indemnity payments")`, `MODEL`) — Tokyo suffers **no September 1905 Hibiya Park anti-treaty riot**. The first great eruption of populist nationalist street violence that taught our timeline's Japanese politics to fear angry imperial crowds never happens.
- **The Army's structural and technological split after 1894 (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 3, `APPROVED` tech upgrade):** Under the 1887 compact and 1907 renewal, the **Imperial Japanese Navy** is the cosmopolitan senior service integrated with Pacifica's yards, fleet, and the Kuroda house. Meanwhile, Yamagata Aritomo's Chōshū Army **never acquires colonial Governor-Generalships in Formosa** (Chinese under `CR-081`) **or Korea** (independent and guaranteed under `L1234 ("Korea remains an independent")`), which in our timeline served as autonomous Army fiefdoms and stepping-stones to the premiership. Instead, the Army's garrison politics center on the **northern/Siberian frontier** (`Enkaishū` and `Karafuto` gained in 1898–99, `CR-088` `A5.10`), nursing a persistent grievance that Pacifica stops Japan's mainland wars early (`L1086 ("shortened victory")`; 1915's Twenty-One Demands cut back; 1930's end of consular courts in China; 1931's halt in Manchuria). Equally decisive by the 1920s–30s is an **internal technological rift**: in a Concord equipped with all-metal aircraft (`CR-054`), electric steel, early radio-location (`L1679`), and atomic deterrence (`CR-050`), **Araki Sadao**'s (`CT-ARAKI-SADAO`) infantry-spirit (*seishin*) wing loses ground inside the officer corps to **modernizing technical-staff officers** who recognize that Japan's home army *needs* Concord aluminium, Nagoya/Ōta licensed airframes, and motorization to guard the Siberian frontier. That internal split undercuts Araki's leverage in May 1932.

### A2. Taishō Politics, Early Radio & Tabulation, 1918 Defusal, and 1925 Without a Soviet Scare (1912–1927) (`APPROVED` tech upgrades; particulars `PROPOSED`)

**1. Early kanji wire-photo (1908–10), *Teishinshō* public radio (~1915–16), and sound newsreels (1916–18) (`APPROVED` tech timeline; institutional model `PROPOSED` as recommended):**
Pacifica's accelerated media timeline (`CR-078`, `CR-079`) transforms Japanese public life a decade before our timeline:
- **Kanji wire-photo dailies (`L1201 ("Photographs go by wire")`):** Because wire-photo facsimile from 1908–10 sends kanji pages, contracts, and photographs directly as pictures — bypassing kanji's teleprinter bottleneck twenty years ahead of our timeline — *Ōsaka Asahi*, *Tōkyō Nichinichi*, and *Jiji Shimpō* grow into real-time illustrated national dailies by the 1910s, carrying same-day pictures of San Francisco, Honolulu, Siberian fronts, and Diet floor debates.
- ***Teishinshō* public-utility radio (~1915–16) shadowed by Honolulu shortwave:** Following San Francisco (1912) and Honolulu (1913–14) and household valve receivers (~1914, `L1201`), Japan does **not** wait until our timeline's 1925 JOAK. True to Meiji unitary infrastructure (`L751`, `CR-088` `A4.3`), the Ministry of Communications (*Teishinshō*) charters a **national public-utility broadcast service** from about **1915–16** in Tokyo, Osaka, and Nagoya — funded by receiver licence fees and carrying sober news bulletins, Okada's typhoon warnings, commodity/rice quotations, parliamentary summaries, and *hōgaku* music. Yet because household valve receivers and transpacific shortwave (~1920, `L1679`) let ordinary Japanese listeners twist the dial at night to catch **Honolulu's Japanese-language broadcasts (from 1916, `L1201`)**, *Pacific String* ballads, baseball scores, and Thalassia debates, **Tokyo's Home Ministry faces a permanently porous airwave border** it cannot seal without crippling its own weather and market-price network.
- **Sound newsreels (`L1681 ("Sound newsreels of the war")`) and PA loudspeakers (`L1203`):** Sound newsreels from 1916–18 and Honolulu/Tokyo talking features from 1919–20 put Kiyoshi in the Baltic, Julian's 1923 Yokohama relief ships, the 1927 banking rescue, and the 1927 atomic demonstration (`L1681`) directly before Japanese cinema crowds, while Jensen moving-coil loudspeakers shift political campaigning from local *meibōka* (rural boss) brokerage to national oratory by the early 1920s.

**2. Summer 1918: price friction defused by early rice sufficiency + tabulated logistics (closes `CR-085` Unresolved #41 & `CR-088` hand-off 4, `APPROVED` tech upgrade):**
In our timeline, soaring rice prices and physical hoarding sparked the nationwide **July–September 1918 Rice Riots (*Kome Sōdō*)**, toppling Terauchi Masatake's military cabinet (*Hist.*). In canon, Hokkaido's early paddy, Manchurian bean cake, and Nichitsu's hydro-nitrogen keep Japan's physical rice supply sufficient (`CR-085`, `L1738 ("keeps pace with its people to 1932")`). Equally critical, Japan's Agriculture & Commerce Ministry and Osaka's rice exchange already track provincial granaries and rail freight on **Hollerith/relay card tabulators**, **kanji wire-photo wires**, and **radio market bulletins**, spotting summer 1918 hoarding and wartime freight clogs in days rather than months. Brief dockside price protests in Toyama and Osaka are defused within weeks by targeted municipal grain releases and railway freight caps; **no nationwide Rice Riots break out and no cabinet falls**. When **Hara Takashi** becomes Japan's first commoner party prime minister in autumn 1918 (*Hist.*), his cabinet rests on Diet strength and Saionji's constitutional counsel rather than street disorder.

**3. September 1923 Great Kantō Earthquake: radio kills the rumor massacre (`APPROVED`, 16:17):**
When the Great Kantō Earthquake strikes on 1 September 1923, domestic radio is already an established household medium across Japan. Live wireless bulletins from Osaka, Nagoya, and intact relay transmitters outside the Tokyo fire zone rapidly publish verified casualty, water, and relief information — including the approach of Julian's Pacifican relief ships. Combined with the fact that Korea is an independent guaranteed state (`L1234`) rather than a restive annexed colony, **early radio broadcasting prevents our timeline's catastrophic September 1923 rumor-driven vigilante massacre of ethnic Koreans** (limiting disorder to isolated, quickly policed looting incidents).

**4. Two rival party coalitions — and their Concord paradox (`PROPOSED`; `Wakatsuki` and `Shidehara` registered under `4a`):**
By the mid-1920s, party cabinets are ordinary practice in Tokyo, rotating between two major parties whose economic doctrines create an ironic split over Pacifica:
- ***Kenseikai* / *Minseitō* (urban commerce, trading houses, Mitsubishi ties):** Led by **Wakatsuki Reijirō** (`CT-WAKATSUKI-REIJIRO`, 1866–1949; PM 1926–27 and April–December 1931, *Hist.*) and **Shidehara Kijūrō** (`CT-SHIDEHARA-KIJURO`, 1872–1951; Foreign Minister 1924–27 and 1929–31, *Hist.*). They are the natural commercial internationalists of the Concord (`L1650 ("Wakatsuki and Shidehara call it")`, `L1800 ("Japan’s national army plots")`), defending open trade, China's sovereignty, and civilian restraint over the generals. Their vulnerability is monetary orthodoxy: defending rigid gold parity with San Francisco drags rural prices down while farm debts stay fixed (`L1612 ("At gold parity Japan")`).
- ***Rikken Seiyūkai* (provincial landlords, infrastructure, domestic industry):** Splits cleanly down the middle between its pragmatic financial stewards — **Takahashi Korekiyo** (`CT-TAKAHASHI`) and **Inukai Tsuyoshi** (`CT-INUKAI-TSUYOSHI`), who accept the Concord while fighting inside it for monetary flexibility and rural relief — and its hardline nationalist floor wing led by **Mori Kaku** (`CT-MORI-KAKU`), who denounces indefinite renewal as *"the bakufu made permanent"* (`L1826 ("Mori Kaku of the Seiyūkai")`).

**5. 1925: Universal Manhood Suffrage without a Bolshevik Scare — policing wavelengths instead of thought (`APPROVED` tech upgrade):**
As in our timeline, the Imperial Diet abolishes tax qualifications in **1925**, enacting **Universal Manhood Suffrage** for men aged twenty-five and older (*Hist.*, expanding the electorate roughly fourfold to over twelve million voters). In our timeline, terror of the Soviet Union forced the **Peace Preservation Law (*Chian Ijihō*)** through the same session, empowering the *Tokkō* (Special Higher Police) to crush unions and liberal professors (*Hist.*). In canon, **Lenin and Trotsky were executed in 1920 and Russia is a federal republic under Wrangel (`CR-041`, `CR-044`) — there is no Soviet Union and no Comintern**. The Diet still passes a **narrower 1925 Public Order Statute** criminalizing violent conspiracy against the state or imperial house (later invoked against the 15 May plotters), without our timeline's sweeping ideological *tenkō* purges. Instead, the Home Ministry's sharpest 1920s–30s regulatory battles are over **unlicensed shortwave transmitters, broadcast spectrum rules, and sound-newsreel censorship** — fighting a porous Pacific media ecosystem while labour unions, tenant associations, and university law faculties remain legally open through 1932.

### A3. The Arc of Japanese Public Opinion, Tabulated Finance, and the "Machine-State" Critique (1887–1932) (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 2, `APPROVED` tech upgrades)

Japanese public opinion toward Pacifica moves through **four generational phases** (`L753 ("Iwakura Mission")`, `L1258 ("Julian spends a year in Tokyo")`, `L1633 ("Hirohito becomes Emperor Shōwa")`, `L1641 ("stop paying abroad")`):
1. **1871–1907 — Respectful Utility & Hard Bargaining:** Seen as the one Western power that never humiliated Japan, proved equal in 1906–07 when Tokyo bargains hard over Sacramento's schools (`CR-088` `A5.7`).
2. **1908–1926 — Everyday Tangibility:** Julian's 1907 marriage to Lady Chiyo, kanji wire-photo dailies, radio broadcasts, Honolulu Japanese talkies, early AC rural hydro (`CR-088` `A5.4`), and **Julian's 1923 earthquake relief** turn a diplomatic treaty into lived familiarity.
3. **Spring 1927 — The Tabulated Bank Rescue & Radio Reassurance (`L1633`, `L1641`, `L1643`):** When London acceptance houses pull out first and depositors queue outside Tokyo banks, **Takahashi's three-week moratorium** audits overlapping collateral across hundreds of Japanese banks using **punched-card and relay tabulators** while **his calm voice on *Teishinshō* public radio** explains the domestic rebuild and San Francisco's $600 million stabilization credit — breaking depositor panic far faster than print extras alone. When the Emperor ratifies the provisional Concord on the Privy Council's conditional advice and the Diet votes its quota (`L1643 ("Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council")`), urban opinion accepts it with sober relief, reassured by Japan's domestic firewalls (`L1654 ("Japan grants English no domestic")`).
4. **1930–1932 — Four Constituencies & the "Calculating-Machine State" Backlash:** By 1931, San Francisco's Reserve Bank computes its gold cover **daily** on a stored-program Realm machine (`L1691 ("Japan buys the Tokyo machine")`) — making the gold-parity ceiling on Japan's rural relief lending instantaneous and merciless (`L1832 ("would drain Japan's earmarked gold")`) — even as **Japan buys its own stored-program machine in 1931** for Okada's typhoon tracks (while Tokyo laboratories already experiment with ferrite cores, `L1691`). On the eve of 15 May 1932, Japanese opinion splits into four clear constituencies:
   - **The Urban "Pacific Generation" (pro-Concord majority):** Salaried commuters, engineers, shipyard/aircraft workers at Nagoya and Ōta, port merchants, radio listeners, and cinema audiences (`CR-088`, `CR-089`). They back renewal and the scheduled 1937 lift of the Hayama aluminium bar (`CR-085`).
   - **The Court–Navy–Finance Establishment (pragmatic stewards):** Saionji, Saitō, Takahashi, Wakatsuki, Shidehara, and the naval treaty faction.
   - **Connected Rural Villages (anxious swing constituency, not revolutionary):** Protected from famine and colonial rice dumping (`CR-085`) and wired into regional AC hydro, radio price bulletins, and village *Sangyō Kumiai* electric workshops (`CR-088` `A5.4`, `L1685 ("Gondō Seikyō preaches")`), rural cooperatives lobby the Diet directly. Squeezed by gold-parity deflation (`L1612`) and the American silk slump, they sympathize with Gondō's lament, cheer Takahashi's radio-explained July 1932 yen reset, and **refuse to rise for a military coup**.
   - **The Restoration Right & the "Calculating-Machine State" Critique (vocal minority):** Army continentalists (`CT-ARAKI-SADAO`), fleet-faction holdouts remembering Kronstadt (`CR-088` `A5.9`), Seiyūkai hardliners (`CT-MORI-KAKU`), and *kokutai* absolutists (`CT-HIRANUMA-KIICHIRO`), echoed in *Gen'yōsha*/*Kokuryūkai* journals (and Kita Ikki in the background). Just as Honolulu debates whether machines possess *mana* (`L1691`), **Japanese traditionalists attack "San Francisco's calculating-machine order"** — charging that punched-card tax rolls, San Francisco's daily gold-cover machine, and the upcoming 1937 Polytechnic/Statistics Bureau quota audit (`CR-085`) reduce a living imperial nation and its villages to holes in an American card, turning Chichibu's Thalassia seat (`L1715 ("new bakufu")`) into a technocratic *bakufu*. That distinctively Pacifican critique gives the 15 May conspirators lethal passion — without ever winning an electoral majority.

### A4. Constitutional Orthodoxy, Press Space, and Endemic Women's Municipal Suffrage (1920s–1932) (`APPROVED` core, boundary, & tech upgrades)

**1. Minobe Tatsukichi's *"Emperor Organ Theory"* (*Tennō Kikansetsu*) holds as mainstream orthodoxy (`APPROVED`, 1a).**
In our timeline, Tokyo Imperial University jurist **Minobe Tatsukichi** (*Hist.* 1873–1948; background, not registered) taught for thirty years that under the Meiji Constitution the state is the sovereign legal person and the Emperor is its highest constitutional organ — the foundation of party government until militarists purged him in 1935 (*Hist.*). In canon, **Minobe's theory never falls**. When Restoration officers invoke "direct imperial rule" on 15 May 1932 to justify murdering Prime Minister Inukai, Emperor Shōwa himself refuses the claim — declaring through his stand (`L1822 ("stand in May is his own")`) that officers cannot bypass his lawful government in his name (`L1816`). With Saionji, Saitō, and the throne aligned behind constitutional restraint, Hiranuma Kiichirō's absolutist *kokutai* dissent in the Privy Council (`L1828`) remains a recorded minority objection.

**2. Broadcasting, press, and television's horizon (`APPROVED` tech alignment):**
Because *Teishinshō* public radio has run since ~1915–16 and kanji wire-photo dailies since 1908–10, the June–July 1932 renewal debate plays out openly across national radio summaries, illustrated dailies, and sound newsreels. **Public television** (`L1810 ("public television service")`: San Francisco 1930, Honolulu November 1931, Los Angeles 1932) is **not yet** a public broadcast service in Japan in 1932 — Tokyo has only experimental laboratory sets alongside its ferrite-core work (`L1691`) — though Japanese delegates and correspondents watch live television charts and newsreels in Honolulu during the July 1932 Thalassia Conference.

**3. Endemic Japanese Women's Municipal & Prefectural Suffrage — Enacted March 1931 (`APPROVED`, 2a + author's explicit boundary + tech upgrade):**
- **Strict canon boundary (`APPROVED`, author 27 November 2026, 15:25):** Neither **Victoria Kamāmalu** nor **Princess Kaʻiulani** ever acts as a "suffragette," adviser, or missionary for women's suffrage in Japan. Any Pacifican lecturing on Japan's franchise would strike the wrong footing, hand nationalist critics an interference charge, and strain the Concord. Just as Pacifica keeps an absolute firewall on Shinto (`CR-088` `A4.5`), Japanese constitutional franchise is exclusively Japan's own affair.
- **How suffrage emerges endemically inside a technologically accelerated Japan (`APPROVED` tech upgrade; legislative particulars `PROPOSED`):** By 1930, Japan's own internal modernization has transformed women's civic and economic standing far faster than in our timeline:
  1. Near-universal girls' elementary literacy and rapid growth in girls' high schools, women's normal schools, and women's colleges (*Hist.*).
  2. Millions of wage-earning women not only in silk-reeling and textiles (`CR-054`), but across Japan's accelerated **telephone exchanges, Hollerith card-punching and statistical bureaus, kanji wire-photo newsrooms, radio studios, banking/clerical offices, and electrified rural *Sangyō Kumiai* cooperative bookkeeping** — forming a visibly indispensable Japanese white-collar and technical female workforce by 1925–30.
  3. **Home radio** carrying consumer-price bulletins, municipal sanitation debates, and parliamentary summaries directly into households (bypassing male-only political lecture halls), reinforcing indigenous Japanese organizing — *Shin Fujin Kyōkai* (winning repeal of Article 5's ban on women attending political meetings in 1922, *Hist.*), **Ichikawa Fusae**'s *Fusen Kakutoku Dōmei* (founded 1924, *Hist.*; background, not registered), and municipal mothers' and consumer-budget leagues.
- **The March 1931 enactment (`PROPOSED`):** In our timeline, the Minseitō cabinet's **Municipal Women's Suffrage Bill** passed the Lower House in February 1931 before the House of Peers killed it 184–62 in March 1931, and the Manchurian Incident buried reform six months later (*Hist.*). Here, with no Bolshevik panic hardening the Peers, no Manchurian war fever, and a deeply visible female clerical/technical/cooperative citizenry, **the House of Peers passes the compromise Municipal and Prefectural Women's Suffrage Act in March 1931** (granting women twenty-five and older the vote and eligibility for city, town, village, and prefectural assemblies, with conservative Peers securing a phased spousal-consent review clause for married candidates that reformers immediately campaign to drop). Japanese women cast their **first legal local ballots in autumn 1931 and spring 1932**, while **national Diet suffrage** remains an active Japanese campaign in 1932.

### A5. The Legal & Political Reckoning of 15 May 1932: Yokosuka, Sealed Magnetic Wire, and Stern Sentences (closes `CR-085` Unresolved #41 at `L1828` & `L2081`) (`APPROVED`, 3a + tech upgrade)

On 15 May 1932, Restoration officers assassinate Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi and attempt to seize Japanese-crewed carriers of the Common Navy's western squadron, foiled when **Kuroda Kiyotada** (`CT-KURODA-SON`), warned by Maria Kashiwagi, holds the fleet loyal (`L1814 ("Restoration officers strike against Inukai")`). Crucially, because the plotters fail to capture Tokyo's *Teishinshō* broadcast transmitter, **Takahashi and Saitō put verified statements live on national radio within hours** (coordinating quietly with Thalassia over the scrambled radiotelephone, `L1679`), denying the coup the rumour fog it counted on. Commander Kashiwagi and the naval mutineers are arrested within the week (`L1816`).

**The Jurisdictional & Media Rule (`L1828 ("which court tries the naval officers")`, `L2081 ("which court tries the naval officers")`):**
Hardliners in Thalassia — among them Samuel Wilder King on the General Staff — ask whether mutiny aboard Common Navy vessels should be tried by a Concord court-martial. **Julian and Saitō Makoto refuse.** Acting on Saitō's governing principle (*"the army is to be mastered by Japan, as it was in May, or it has not been mastered"*, `L1822`), both capitals agree on strict **Japanese national jurisdiction**:
1. **Yokosuka Naval District Court-Martial (Japanese naval law):** Tries Commander Kashiwagi and the Japanese Common Navy officers involved in the carrier plot under the Imperial Japanese Naval Penal Code for mutiny and conspiracy. By reciprocal Concord courtesy, **a single Pacifican naval judge-advocate sits in the gallery as an observer** — with **no vote, no bench seat, and no Thalassian appellate authority**.
2. **Tokyo District Court & First Division Army Court-Martial (Japanese law):** Try Inukai's direct assassins and the army/civilian conspirators under Japanese criminal, military, and 1925 Public Order statutes.
3. **Zero Broadcast Theatre — Sealed Magnetic Wire Only (`APPROVED` tech upgrade):** In our timeline, the 1933–34 May 15 trials degenerated into media-amplified platforms for patriotic speeches, flooding the courts with clemency petitions and yielding scandalously light sentences (*Hist.*). In canon — where radio and sound newsreels reach every town — Saitō's cabinet and the Yokosuka bench **strictly bar radio microphones and sound-newsreel cameras from the courtrooms**, recording testimony solely on **magnetic wire for the sealed judicial record** (`L1203`) and publishing only dry daily bench summaries.
4. **Why the verdicts hold (`APPROVED` stern sentences; timing `PROPOSED`):** Stripped of both an extraterritoriality rally cry and a broadcast martyrdom stage — and facing Emperor Shōwa's unmistakable stand (`L1816`, `L1822`), loyal naval leadership, and an intact open press mourning a murdered premier whose voice every household knew from radio — the Yokosuka and Tokyo courts proceed from summer–autumn 1932 toward **uncommuted capital and life sentences for Inukai's direct murderers and long penal servitude with cashiering for Kashiwagi and the carrier mutineers** (final sentencing running into 1933, beyond the book's August 1932 horizon, with the firm no-clemency posture unmistakable by July 1932).
