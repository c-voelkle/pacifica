## A. The change

### A1. Meiji Constitutional Balance Under Pacifica's Shadow (1871–1912) (`APPROVED` core; particulars `PROPOSED`)

**1. The 1889 Meiji Constitution stands intact (`APPROVED`, D-1).**
Canon already establishes that in 1871 Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi read Pacifica's Articles of 1856 and refuse its confederate form (`L751 ("Kido Takayoshi and Itō Hirobumi")`), and that in 1932 Japan still operates under the **1889 Meiji Constitution**: a non-voting cabinet (`L1816 ("The officers kill Inukai on 15 May")`), an advisory Privy Council where **Hiranuma Kiichirō** (`CT-HIRANUMA-KIICHIRO`) enters formal dissents (`L1828`), the *genrō* (**Saionji Kinmochi**, `CT-SAIONJI`), active-duty service ministers, and a bicameral Imperial Diet (*Teikoku Gikai*, House of Representatives and House of Peers). Pacifica never pushes constitutional reform onto Tokyo.

Inside that unchanged constitutional text, **four structural divergences** shift Japan's political center of gravity away from our timeline's military-oligarchic lock:
- **Softened treaty-revision politics (`PROPOSED`, D-10; aligned with `CR-088` `A1.3`):** Pacifica's 1870–71 equal alliance ends mixed panels on its fixed date without demanding interior landownership (`L749 ("1870–1871 Japanese alliance")`), and Pacifica backs Japan's European treaty revision from 1887 (`L993 ("compact with Tokyo is signed")`). As `CR-088` `A1.3` notes, the Atlantic powers still make mixed residence (*naichi zakkyo*) their price in the revisions of 1894–99, so the Japanese nationalist debate over foreign courts and residence is **softened rather than erased** — depriving Meiji ultranationalists of the single existential grievance that poisoned the 1880s Diet while keeping *kokusui hozon* watchfulness alive. Early Diet parties (*Jiyūtō*, *Kaishintō*, later *Seiyūkai*) therefore spend far more of their strength bargaining over budgets, land taxes, and railway appropriations.
- **State Shinto, the 1890 Imperial Rescript on Education, and Emperor Shōwa's silence on radio (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 1; microphone firewall `APPROVED`, D-6; civic moderation `PROPOSED`, D-10):** As in our timeline, the Meiji state separates civic shrine rites from sectarian preaching (1882), promulgates the **Imperial Rescript on Education** (October 1890), and establishes the Home Ministry's Shrine Bureau (1900) (*Hist.*; see `CR-088` `A4.5`). Because Japan faces neither Christian missionary extraterritoriality nor our timeline's existential humiliations (no 1895 Triple Intervention, no 1905 Portsmouth outrage), domestic *kokutai* teaching settles into **conservative civic loyalty and filial ethics** rather than paranoid spiritual mobilization. Transpacific friction still survives at the border (`CR-088` G29, `L1194 ("Japanese faiths under the Realm's floors")`): Tokyo's Home Ministry denounces Japanese Pacificans who decline shrine rites as apostates because Realm courts classify overseas shrines as voluntary churches and bar the Emperor's portrait and Rescript from Realm schools. When voice radio arrives a decade early (`A2`, `A4`), **Emperor Shōwa keeps the court's sacred firewall of silence on public broadcast microphones** — speaking to the public only through Imperial Rescripts read on air by the premier or grand chamberlain (mirroring Victoria Kamāmalu's refusal of the microphone at `L1203 ("Victoria never sings again")`, and matching canon's care at `L1679 ("Thalassia telephones Tokyo")` and `L1696 ("decides a second station")` in naming *"the Emperor's household"* rather than Hirohito's public voice on air).
- **No 1905 Hibiya Riot (`PROPOSED`, D-10; grounded in `L1236`):** Because Victoria advises Tokyo to refuse Roosevelt's mediation and Russia negotiates directly at Urajio — paying roughly **$300 million in indemnity** across 1906–10 (`L1236 ("Russian indemnity payments")`, `MODEL`) — Tokyo suffers **no September 1905 Hibiya Park anti-treaty riot**. The first great eruption of populist nationalist street violence that taught our timeline's Japanese politics to fear angry imperial crowds never happens.
- **The Army's post-1894 northern-frontier shift and internal technological rift (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 3; tech split `APPROVED` D-8, historical particulars `PROPOSED` D-11):** Under the 1887 compact and 1907 renewal, the **Imperial Japanese Navy** is the cosmopolitan senior service integrated with Pacifica's yards, fleet, and the Kuroda house. Meanwhile, Yamagata Aritomo's Chōshū Army **never acquires colonial Governor-Generalships in Formosa** (Chinese under `CR-081`) **or Korea** (independent and guaranteed under `L1234 ("Korea remains an independent")`), which in our timeline served as autonomous Army fiefdoms and stepping-stones to the premiership. Instead, Army garrison politics center on the **northern/Siberian frontier** (`Enkaishū` and `Karafuto` gained in 1898–99, `CR-088` `A5.10`), nursing a durable resentment that Pacifica cuts Japan's mainland wars short (`L1086 ("shortened victory")`; 1915's Demands cut back; 1930's end of consular courts in China; 1931's halt in Manchuria). By the 1920s–30s, accelerated Pacific technology opens a **cold internal rift inside the officer corps**: against **Araki Sadao**'s (`CT-ARAKI-SADAO`) infantry-spirit (*seishin*) wing stand **modernizing technical-staff officers** — no sentimental friends of Pacifica, but cold-eyed nationalists who calculate that Japan's home army *needs* Concord aluminium, electric steel, licensed Nagoya/Ōta all-metal airframes (`L1650`), and modern motorization to face federal Russia on the Siberian frontier. They back restraint in 1931–32 strictly on industrial arithmetic while resenting the domestic aluminium bar (`CR-088` G20) and preparing to press for harder industrial concessions at the **1937 review**.

### A2. Early Broadcasts & Tabulation, 1918 Inflation, 1923 Quake, Party Politics, and 1925 Public Order (1912–1931)

**1. Kanji wire-photo (1908–10), *Teishinshō* public-utility radio (~1915–16), and sound newsreels (1916–18) (`APPROVED`, D-8 & D-9):**
Pacifica's accelerated media timeline (`CR-078`, `CR-079`) reshapes Japanese public life a decade before our timeline:
- **Kanji wire-photo dailies (`L1201 ("Photographs go by wire")`):** Because wire-photo facsimile from 1908–10 transmits kanji pages, contracts, and photographs directly as pictures — bypassing kanji's teleprinter bottleneck twenty years early — *Ōsaka Asahi*, *Tōkyō Nichinichi*, and *Jiji Shimpō* grow into real-time illustrated national dailies by the 1910s, carrying same-day pictures of San Francisco, Honolulu, Siberian fronts, and Diet floor debates.
- ***Teishinshō* public-utility radio (~1915–16) and the porous shortwave border (`APPROVED`, D-9):** Following San Francisco (1912), Honolulu (1913–14), and household valve receivers (~1914, `L1199 ("valve receiver is a household good")`), Japan does **not** wait until our timeline's 1925 JOAK. True to Meiji unitary infrastructure (`L751`, `CR-088` `A4.3`), the Ministry of Communications (*Teishinshō*) charters a **national public-utility broadcast service** from about **1915–16** in Tokyo, Osaka, and Nagoya — funded by receiver licence fees and carrying sober news bulletins, Okada's typhoon warnings, commodity/rice quotations, parliamentary summaries, and *hōgaku* music. **Domestic media losers:** Japan's great national dailies (*Ōsaka Asahi*, *Tōkyō Nichinichi*, *Jiji Shimpō*) fight fiercely for private radio franchises before *Teishinshō* enforces consolidation, resenting the state news monopoly (mirroring Hearst's editors at `L1201`) and answering with aggressive wire-photo picture extras. **The porous airwave border:** When Honolulu begins Japanese-language air-school and cultural broadcasts in **1916** (`L1201`), coastal listening posts, shipping offices, and newspaper wire-desks pick them up first; once **transpacific shortwave** arrives about **1920** (`L1679`), ordinary Japanese households with licensed valve sets can twist the dial at night to catch Honolulu's *Pacific String* ballads, film reviews, baseball scores, and Thalassia Senate debates — confronting Tokyo's Home Ministry with an un-censorable airwave border it cannot shut down without crippling its own weather and cooperative market-price network.
- **Sound newsreels (`L1681 ("Sound newsreels of the war")`) and PA loudspeakers (`L1203`):** Sound newsreels from 1916–18 and Honolulu/Tokyo talking features from 1919–20 put Kiyoshi in the Baltic, Julian's 1923 Yokohama relief ships, the 1927 banking rescue, and the 1927 atomic demonstration (`L1681`) before Japanese cinema crowds, while Jensen moving-coil loudspeakers shift political campaigning from local *meibōka* (rural boss) brokerage to national oratory by the early 1920s.

**2. Summer 1918: Siberian requisitioning, wartime inflation, and Hara's constitutional accession (closes `CR-085` Unresolved #41 & `CR-088` hand-off 4; tech tools `APPROVED` D-8, political settlement `PROPOSED` D-12):**
In our timeline, soaring rice prices and physical hoarding sparked the nationwide **July–September 1918 Rice Riots (*Kome Sōdō*)**, toppling Field Marshal Terauchi Masatake's non-party cabinet (*Hist.*). In canon, wartime monetary/export inflation and quartermaster buying for **Japan's five-division Siberian mobilization (`L1448 ("Between 26 May and 10 June 1918")`)** still inflict real price pain on urban wage-earners and clerks, sparking genuine summer 1918 dockside protests in Toyama and Osaka and tense strikes in Kobe and Osaka shipyards. Two things keep those protests from exploding into a nationwide insurrection: (1) Hokkaido's early paddy, Manchurian bean cake, and Nichitsu nitrogen keep Japan's physical rice supply sufficient (`CR-085`, `L1738 ("keeps pace with its people to 1932")`); and (2) Japan's Agriculture & Commerce Ministry and Osaka's rice exchange track provincial granaries and rail freight on **Hollerith/relay card tabulators** (`L984 ("punched cards tabulate")`), kanji wire-photo wires, and *Teishinshō* radio market bulletins, enabling targeted municipal grain releases and railway freight caps within days (infuriating speculative rice dealers and provincial landlords). **No nationwide Rice Riots break out and no cabinet falls to street mobs.**

Instead, in autumn 1918, Field Marshal Terauchi steps down from **failing health** (*Hist.*: Terauchi died in November 1919) and because the **Seiyūkai refuses to carry the five-division Siberian campaign's supplementary budget in the coming Diet session** (the Diet does not sit until December), allowing Saionji to recommend **Hara Takashi**, leader of the largest party in the House, as Japan's first commoner party prime minister (*Hist.*) through ordinary constitutional leverage rather than street arson.

**3. September 1923 Great Kantō Earthquake: battery wireless, independent Korea, and post-quake grit (`APPROVED`, D-7; operational detail `PROPOSED`, D-12):**
When the Great Kantō Earthquake strikes on 1 September 1923 (`L1593 ("Tokyo fire of September 1923")`), Tokyo's and Yokohama's electric grids collapse immediately and firestorms gut nearly half the capital. In our timeline, the Home Ministry used the Funabashi naval wireless station to broadcast false rumours of a colonial Korean uprising nationwide — driven by post-1910 annexation paranoia after the 1919 March First Movement — unleashing vigilante massacres of ethnic Koreans (*Hist.*). In canon, two facts break that chain: **Korea is an independent guaranteed state** with an active legation in Tokyo (`L1234`), removing colonial *futei Senjin* security mania; and *Teishinshō*'s intact relay transmitters in Osaka and Nagoya feed **battery-powered valve receivers and Jensen PA loudspeakers at surviving ward offices, railway depots, and relief ships entering Tokyo Bay**, broadcasting verified water, shelter, and Julian's relief-ship bulletins instead of police panic. The catastrophe is never bloodless: over a hundred thousand die in quake and fire, 24–48 hours of terror and martial-law friction still produce localized vigilante scares (shut down by troops rather than licensed by them), and survivors direct ferocious political anger at Tokyo officials who rejected Imamura Akitsune's 1906 seismic warning (`L1254 ("earthquake of April 1906")`, `L1593`).

**The state's own killing (`APPROVED` loss, author 27 November 2026, 17:29; facts *Hist.*; Pacifica's response `APPROVED`, D-19):** The troops who put down the vigilantes are not the whole army. On 16 September 1923 a *Kempeitai* squad under Captain **Amakasu Masahiko** (*Hist.*; background, not registered) arrests the anarchist **Ōsugi Sakae**, the feminist writer **Itō Noe** and Ōsugi's six-year-old nephew **Tachibana Munekazu**; all three are killed in custody and thrown into a well (*Hist.*). Amakasu is court-martialled and sentenced to ten years, reduced to seven and a half in August 1924, and released under an amnesty in October 1926 (*Hist.*). Itō's murder stays in the women's movement's memory into 1931 (`A4.3`). The boy was born at Portland, Oregon, in 1917 (*Hist.*) — Pacifican soil in canon — and Julian is at Yokohama with relief that month (`L1591 ("brings relief and reconstruction credit to Yokohama")`). The Realm's legation asks for the trial and gets it; Julian says nothing in public, and the amnesty of 1926 passes without a Pacifican note (D-19). The friendship has a price, and it is paid on both sides.

**4. Party cabinets, Tanaka's 1927–29 fall, and the Minseitō government of 1929–31 under Hamaguchi and Wakatsuki (`APPROVED` chronology, D-13; reason for the 1931 hand-over and coalition framing `PROPOSED`, D-13):**
By the 1920s, rotating party cabinets govern Tokyo inside a sharp structural split over Pacifica:
- ***Rikken Seiyūkai* (provincial landlords, domestic industry, infrastructure):** Splits between pragmatic Concord stewards — **Takahashi Korekiyo** (`CT-TAKAHASHI`) and **Inukai Tsuyoshi** (`CT-INUKAI-TSUYOSHI`), who accept the Concord while fighting inside it for monetary relief — and its hardline nationalist wing. Under **Tanaka Giichi's 1927–29 Seiyūkai cabinet** (`L1587 ("Tanaka's cabinet sends troops")`), that hardline wing holds office, sends troops to Shandong outside Concord command, fails to punish Zhang Zuolin's assassins, and **falls in July 1929** (`L1587`) — proving to Japanese voters that unilateral Army adventurism ruins cabinets. After Tanaka's fall, **Mori Kaku** (`CT-MORI-KAKU`) carries the Seiyūkai floor's nationalist banner, later denouncing indefinite renewal as *"the bakufu made permanent"* (`L1826 ("Mori Kaku of the Seiyūkai")`).
- ***Kenseikai* / *Minseitō* (urban commerce, trading houses, Mitsubishi ties):** **Wakatsuki Reijirō** (`CT-WAKATSUKI-REIJIRO`, 1866–1949) is Kenseikai prime minister from January 1926 and falls in the bank panic of April 1927 (*Hist.*); **Hamaguchi Osachi** (*Hist.* 1870–1931; background, not registered), the Minseitō's president from its founding in 1927, succeeds Tanaka in **July 1929**. No 1930 London Naval Treaty exists in canon, so nobody shoots Hamaguchi; instead, **ill and worn down by the winter's fights over gold-parity deflation, he resigns in April 1931 and dies that August** (`PROPOSED`, D-13; the date is our timeline's, the cause is not; his last winter is in `A2.6`), and the party recalls **Wakatsuki** as prime minister from **April to December 1931**. **Shidehara Kijūrō** (`CT-SHIDEHARA-KIJURO`, 1872–1951) is Foreign Minister in 1924–27 and in both Minseitō cabinets of 1929–31. They are the commercial internationalists of the Concord (`L1650 ("Wakatsuki and Shidehara call it")`, `L1800 ("Japan’s national army plots")`), restraining the generals in 1931 while trapped politically by gold-parity deflation (`L1612 ("At gold parity Japan")`).

**5. 1925: Universal Manhood Suffrage without a Soviet scare — policing wavelengths instead of thought (`APPROVED` tech shift D-8; legislative settlement `PROPOSED` D-14):**
As in our timeline, the Imperial Diet abolishes tax qualifications in **1925**, enacting **Universal Manhood Suffrage** for men twenty-five and older (*Hist.*, expanding the electorate roughly fourfold from ~3.3 million to ~12.5 million). Japan already possessed pre-1917 domestic security machinery — the **1900 Public Order and Police Law (*Chian Keisatsuhō*)** curbing strikes and the **1911 *Tokkō* (Special Higher Police)** founded after the indigenous anarchist High Treason Incident (*Hist.*) — and the Privy Council still demands a conservative statutory counterweight in 1925 as its domestic price for quadrupling the franchise. Because **Lenin and Trotsky were executed in 1920 and Russia is a federal republic under Wrangel (`CR-041`) — with no Soviet Union and no Comintern** — the Diet passes a **narrower 1925 Public Order Statute** targeting violent conspiracy against the state or imperial house, and **never enacts our timeline's 1928 death-penalty escalation or sweeping ideological *tenkō* purges**. Moderate socialist parties (including figures like `CT-ABE-ISOO`), legal labour unions, and university law faculties operate in the open through 1932 — though the Home Ministry still harasses radical tenant leagues under ordinary police powers and fights an exasperating, losing regulatory battle over **unlicensed shortwave transmitters, Honolulu broadcasts, and sound-newsreel censorship**.

**6. The villages under gold, 1927–1932 (losses `APPROVED` in direction, author 27 November 2026, 17:29; mechanism and texture `PROPOSED`, D-18):**
This answers the author's question of 17:29, *"What exactly does canon's dedication to sound money do to Japan's villagers?"*, from canon's own rules.
- **The rule (canon).** Since 1927 gold parity and the gold cover are agreed matters of the Concord (`L1647 ("Customs, gold parity")`), so Tokyo cannot float or devalue the yen alone. The Reserve Bank watches its cover and never a price index, and Pacifica's prices fall year after year as its inputs get cheaper; at the same parity Japan's prices must follow them down while its farmers' debts do not (`L1612 ("At gold parity Japan")`). In San Francisco falling prices are the dividend of productivity, paid with money wages that hold. In a Nagano hill village they are a debt that grows every year without a sen being borrowed.
- **What the village owes in fixed yen.** The land tax and the village levies, the school rate and the teacher's salary, cash rents, the landlord's and the moneylender's advances at interest — and, new to this timeline, the cooperative's own notes. In the 1920s the *Sangyō Kumiai* borrowed to sign bulk power contracts and to put small electric reeling motors and pumps into family workshops (`CR-088` `A5.4`). The modernity that cushions the villages was bought on credit, in yen at the prices of the mid-1920s. Debt stays shallower than in our timeline, where the average Nagano farm household owed 868 yen in 1930 (*Hist.*), but it is fixed while every price the village sells at falls (`L1685 ("keep tenant debt far shallower")`).
- **What the village sells.** Rice holds best: no colonial rice breaks the price and the crop keeps pace (`L1738`). It still falls with every other price, though by less than our timeline's fall of more than two-fifths, from 28 to 16 yen a koku between 1929 and 1931 (*Hist.*). Cocoons are the cash. When the Union's depression takes the buyers of raw silk (`L1755 ("silk districts lose income")`, `L1832`), the price falls by half or more, as it did historically (*Hist.*); San Francisco's couture houses (`L1685`) soften it only at the top of the market. The Suwa and Okaya filatures lay off their girls, and the girls come home without the wages that paid the household's interest.
- **Pacifica looks at it (`PROPOSED`, D-18; author's direction of 17:47).** Thalassia does not pretend the villages are fine. The Office's film unit shoots the Nagano and Tōhoku silk villages in the winter of 1930–31, and the press estate distributes the film unaltered in the Realm (`L1203`), so Pacifican audiences see the idle reeling rooms and the notices about daughters. The cabinet's papers on Japan from 1931 carry the cocoon price and the village figures beside the gold cover. Nobody in Thalassia calls the parity painless; the ministers defend it as the smaller harm, and they say so in those words.
- **The money that is not printed, and why (canon refusal; argument `PROPOSED`).** In the winter of 1931–32 Takahashi asks the common Finance minister for fresh dollars for Japan's villages, and the Reserve Bank's board refuses to issue beyond its cover (`L1765 ("refuses to issue beyond its cover")`). The refusal is not indifference, and Thalassia does not send it as a cable. The common Finance minister borrows Pavel Kashevarov (`CT-PAVEL-KASHEVAROV`), head of the Reserve Bank's Russian department (`L1832`), and sends him to Tokyo in January 1932 to give the answer in person and argue it, and the argument is the one Pacifica makes about its own farmers:
  - *Borrowed dollars are a new debt on the same deflation.* A loan in gold dollars to villages whose prices are falling adds to what they owe at the worst rate there is. What lifts a village's fixed yen debts is a lower yen, which raises every price the village sells at.
  - *A bank that prints for one plea cannot refuse the next.* Kashevarov watched Omsk print Siberia's money away in 1918 (`L1765`). The same board is refusing the Realm's own county farms that winter, and they are foreclosed (`L1765`); it cannot print for Nagano and not for Fresno, and printing for both ends the cover.
  - *The cover is Japan's own savings.* Japan's earmarked gold and half-covered balances sit in San Francisco (`L1647`), and the cover's credit stopped the run of 1927 (`L1633`–`L1643`). With Britain off gold since September 1931 (`L1755`), a dollar nobody trusts would leave no lender anywhere for the next 1927.
  Takahashi, who knows debt better than anyone in Tokyo, takes the first argument and turns it on Thalassia: if a lower yen is the real relief, give Japan an adjustable yen. That is his request from then on (`L1804 ("asking for an adjustable yen")`), and it is the one Julian grants.
- **What Pacifica gives before the reset (`PROPOSED`, D-18; amounts OPEN).** Help that needs no new money starts at once:
  - *Nitrogen and seed in kind.* The Concord refuses a partner neither seed nor nitrogen (`L1800 ("does not refuse seed, nitrogen or grain")`), and the slump has half idled the Columbia's ammonia plants (`L1765`). From the spring of 1931 the Realm delivers ammonium sulfate and seed rice to the *Sangyō Kumiai* on a ten-year credit repayable in yen, so no gold leaves Japan and none is issued. Hamaguchi, who will not ask for a looser parity, accepts this; it is one of his last acts.
  - *The fleet's silk.* The common fleet and the Realm's air arm buy two years' parachute and powder-bag silk ahead in 1931–32, from Japanese reelers, out of the common budget. Pacifica pays its 65 per cent of it. It does not bring back the Union's buyers, but it keeps some filatures running and some girls at their reels.
- **Why the reset waits for July, and what Julian spends on it (canon, `L1822`, `L1830`, `L1832`).** The parity is an agreed matter (`L1647`), and moving it in the winter that Britain fell off gold would read in every market as Japan's flight; at Hayama in June it reads as a treaty. Julian has to carry it against his own side: Giannini and his allies, who watched the Realm's farms foreclose under the same refusal, object to the yen's reset (`L1822 ("object to the yen's reset")`), and he concedes it because rigid deflation on gold is handing recruits to the Restoration officers (`L1832`). The yen is reset by about a fifth about 28–29 July 1932 (`L1830 ("reset by a fifth")`). Even then the Bank of Japan's relief underwriting has a ceiling in earmarked gold, and the quota, payable in gold dollars, costs the Diet about a quarter more in yen, so relief is fought against naval taxes (`L1832`).
- **The cost the CR does not hide.** In our timeline Japan left gold on 13 December 1931, the day Takahashi became Inukai's Finance Minister, and the Bank of Japan underwrote an emergency rural relief programme of more than two billion yen in 1932–34 (*Hist.*). Here the villages carry the parity seven months longer and get a smaller relief after it, softened by the nitrogen and the silk orders. What Pacifica's leaders can say for it is that the yen, the banks and the Concord are all still standing in August 1932. The Restoration officers never mention that the same rule foreclosed San Francisco's own farmers.
- **On the page:**
  - *The ruin read aloud.* Every evening the *Teishinshō* market bulletin (`A2.1`) reads the Yokohama raw-silk and provincial cocoon quotations in the same calm voice that reads the typhoon warnings. In the hill villages the cooperative hall's loudspeaker carries the number to men who already know what it means.
  - *The idle motor.* The electric reeling motor in the back room, bought on the cooperative's note, stands still while its instalments run. Some cooperatives cut the night current to keep up the power contract, and villages sit in the dark under their own lines.
  - *Teachers unpaid, children without lunch.* In Tōhoku and the Nagano hills village treasuries fall months behind on teachers' salaries, and children come to school without lunch boxes (*kesshoku jidō*), as in our timeline (*Hist.*) — in fewer villages and for fewer months, because the rice is there and the money is not.
  - **The daughters (`APPROVED` loss, 17:29).** In the silk villages of Tōhoku, Nagano and Gunma, families sell daughters into the licensed quarters of Tokyo and Osaka in 1931–32 to pay the interest. It happens in fewer villages than in our timeline and almost never in the rice plains, because the trade follows the cocoon, not the rice: historically the number of a prefecture's girls in Tokyo's licensed quarters fell when its cocoon output rose and did not track its rice (*Hist.*, An 2017). Village offices and police stations post notices telling parents not to sell their daughters (*Hist.* for the form). Pacifica protected Japan's rice, and that does not protect its girls; only the silk price would, and nobody in either capital can raise it.
  - *The officers' letters.* Young officers read their conscripts' letters about sisters sold. Gondō's lament gets faces, and the officers of 15 May get a grievance that is more than ideology (`L1612`, `L1685`). The countryside still does not rise for them (`A3`).
- **Hamaguchi's last winter (D-13, `PROPOSED`).** Hamaguchi holds the parity as creed and as treaty. Through the Diet session of 1930–31 he refuses to ask Thalassia to move it and refuses relief bonds that the gold ceiling would not allow; against his doctors' advice he answers the Seiyūkai's farm members in person through February and March, resigns in April and dies in August. The rural members say the Lion ate the villages; his own party says he kept Japan's word.

### A3. The Arc of Japanese Public Opinion, Tabulated Finance, and the "Machine-State" Critique (1887–1932) (fulfills `CR-088` hand-off 2; tech upgrades `APPROVED` D-8, arc framing `PROPOSED` D-15)

Japanese public opinion toward Pacifica moves through **four generational phases** (`L753 ("Iwakura Mission")`, `L1258 ("Julian spends a year in Tokyo")`, `L1633 ("Hirohito becomes Emperor Shōwa")`, `L1641 ("stop paying abroad")`):
1. **1871–1907 — Respectful Utility & Hard Bargaining:** Seen as the one Western power that never humiliated Japan, proved equal in 1906–07 when Tokyo bargains hard over Sacramento's schools (`CR-088` `A5.7`).
2. **1908–1926 — Everyday Tangibility:** Julian's 1907 marriage to Lady Chiyo, kanji wire-photo dailies, *Teishinshō* radio and ~1920+ Honolulu shortwave, early AC rural hydro (`CR-088` `A5.4`), and **Julian's 1923 earthquake relief** turn a diplomatic treaty into lived familiarity.
3. **Spring 1927 — The Tabulated Bank Rescue & Radio Reassurance (`L1633`, `L1641`, `L1643`):** When London acceptance houses pull out first and depositors queue outside Tokyo banks, **Takahashi's three-week moratorium** audits overlapping collateral across hundreds of Japanese banks on **punched-card and relay tabulators** (`L986 ("circuit algebra in 1886")`) while **his calm voice on *Teishinshō* public radio** explains the domestic rebuild and San Francisco's $600 million stabilization credit — breaking depositor panic far faster than print extras alone. When the Emperor ratifies the provisional Concord on the Privy Council's conditional advice and the Diet votes its quota (`L1643 ("Emperor ratifies on the Privy Council")`), urban opinion accepts it with sober relief, reassured by Japan's domestic firewalls (`L1654 ("Japan grants English no domestic")`).
4. **1930–1932 — Four Constituencies & the "Calculating-Machine State" Backlash:** By 1931, San Francisco's Reserve Bank computes its gold cover **daily** on a stored-program Realm machine (`L1691 ("Japan buys the Tokyo machine")`) — making the gold-parity ceiling on Japan's rural relief lending instantaneous and merciless (`L1832 ("would drain Japan's earmarked gold")`) — even as **Japan buys its own stored-program machine in 1931** for Okada's typhoon tracks (while Tokyo laboratories experiment with ferrite cores, `L1691`). On the eve of 15 May 1932, Japanese opinion splits into four clear constituencies:
   - **The Urban "Pacific Generation" (pro-Concord majority):** Salaried commuters, engineers, shipyard/aircraft workers at Nagoya and Ōta, port merchants, radio listeners, and cinema audiences (`CR-088`, `CR-089`). They back renewal and the scheduled 1937 lift of the Hayama aluminium bar (`CR-085`).
   - **The Court–Navy–Finance Establishment (pragmatic stewards):** Saionji, Saitō, Takahashi, Wakatsuki, Shidehara, and the naval treaty faction.
   - **Connected Rural Villages (anxious swing constituency, not revolutionary):** Protected from famine and colonial rice dumping (`CR-085`) and wired into regional AC hydro, radio price bulletins, and village *Sangyō Kumiai* electric workshops (`CR-088` `A5.4`, `L1685 ("Gondō Seikyō preaches")`), rural cooperatives lobby the Diet directly. Squeezed by gold-parity deflation (`L1612`) and the American silk slump (`A2.6`), selling daughters in the silk villages, they sympathize with Gondō's lament, cheer Takahashi's radio-explained July 1932 yen reset, and **refuse to rise for a military coup**.
   - **The Restoration Right & the "Calculating-Machine State" Critique (vocal minority):** Army continentalists (`CT-ARAKI-SADAO`), fleet-faction holdouts remembering Kronstadt (`CR-088` `A5.9`), Seiyūkai hardliners (`CT-MORI-KAKU`), and *kokutai* absolutists (`CT-HIRANUMA-KIICHIRO`), echoed in *Gen'yōsha*/*Kokuryūkai* journals (and Kita Ikki in the background). Just as Honolulu debates whether machines possess *mana* (`L1691`), **Japanese traditionalists attack "San Francisco's calculating-machine order"** — charging that punched-card tax rolls, San Francisco's daily gold-cover machine, and the upcoming 1937 Polytechnic/Statistics Bureau quota audit (`CR-085`) reduce a living imperial nation and its villages to **holes in a San Francisco card**, turning Chichibu's Thalassia seat (`L1715 ("new bakufu")`) into a technocratic *bakufu*. That distinctively Pacifican critique gives the 15 May conspirators lethal passion — without ever winning an electoral majority.

### A4. Constitutional Orthodoxy, Press Space, and Endemic Women's Municipal Suffrage (1920s–1932) (`APPROVED` core, boundary, & tech upgrades)

**1. Minobe Tatsukichi's *"Emperor Organ Theory"* (*Tennō Kikansetsu*) holds as mainstream orthodoxy (`APPROVED`, D-1).**
In our timeline, Tokyo Imperial University jurist **Minobe Tatsukichi** (*Hist.* 1873–1948; background, not registered) taught for thirty years that under the Meiji Constitution the state is the sovereign legal person and the Emperor is its highest constitutional organ — the foundation of party government until militarists purged him in 1935 (*Hist.*). In canon, **Minobe's theory never falls**. When Restoration officers invoke "direct imperial rule" on 15 May 1932 to justify murdering Prime Minister Inukai, Emperor Shōwa himself refuses the claim — making it known through his stand (`L1822 ("stand in May is his own")`) that officers cannot bypass his lawful government in his name (`L1816`). With Saionji, Saitō, and the throne aligned behind constitutional restraint, Hiranuma Kiichirō's absolutist *kokutai* dissent in the Privy Council (`L1828`) remains a recorded minority objection.

**2. Broadcasting, press, and television's horizon (`APPROVED`, D-8 & D-9):**
Because *Teishinshō* public radio has run since ~1915–16 and kanji wire-photo dailies since 1908–10, the June–July 1932 renewal debate plays out openly across national radio summaries, illustrated dailies, and sound newsreels. **Public television** (`L1810 ("public television service")`: San Francisco 1930, Honolulu November 1931, Los Angeles 1932) is **not yet** a public broadcast service in Japan in 1932 — Tokyo has only experimental laboratory sets alongside its ferrite-core work (`L1691`) — though Japanese delegates and correspondents watch live television charts and newsreels in Honolulu while the delegations vote at Thalassia in July 1932 (about 9–11 July, `L1826`).

**3. Endemic Japanese Women's Municipal Suffrage — Enacted March 1931 (`APPROVED`, D-2 + author's explicit boundary + D-8 tech upgrade; the Peers vote APPROVED 17:47, D-16; legislative particulars `PROPOSED`, D-16):**
- **Strict canon boundary (`APPROVED`, author 27 November 2026, 15:25):** Neither **Victoria Kamāmalu** nor **Princess Kaʻiulani** ever acts as a "suffragette," adviser, or missionary for women's suffrage in Japan. Any Pacifican lecturing on Japan's franchise would strike the wrong footing, hand nationalist critics an interference charge, and strain the Concord. Just as Pacifica keeps an absolute firewall on Shinto (`CR-088` `A4.5`), Japanese constitutional franchise is exclusively Japan's own affair.
- **How suffrage emerges endemically inside a technologically accelerated Japan:** By 1930, Japan's own internal modernization has transformed women's civic and economic standing far faster than in our timeline:
  1. Near-universal girls' elementary literacy and rapid growth in girls' high schools, women's normal schools, and women's colleges (*Hist.*).
  2. Millions of wage-earning women not only in silk-reeling and textiles (`CR-054`), but across Japan's accelerated **telephone exchanges, Hollerith card-punching and statistical bureaus, kanji wire-photo newsrooms, radio studios, banking/clerical offices, and electrified rural *Sangyō Kumiai* cooperative bookkeeping** — forming a visibly indispensable Japanese white-collar and technical female workforce by 1925–30.
  3. **Home radio** carrying consumer-price bulletins, municipal sanitation debates, and parliamentary summaries directly into households (bypassing male-only political lecture halls), reinforcing indigenous Japanese organizing — *Shin Fujin Kyōkai* (winning repeal of Article 5's ban on women attending political meetings in 1922, *Hist.*), **Ichikawa Fusae**'s *Fusen Kakutoku Dōmei* (founded 1924, *Hist.*; background, not registered), and municipal mothers' and consumer-budget leagues.
- **The March 1931 enactment (`PROPOSED`, D-16):** In our timeline, the Minseitō cabinet's **Municipal Women's Suffrage Bill** — championed by Home Minister **Adachi Kenzō** (*Hist.*; background, not registered) and already incorporating a husband's-consent clause for married women running for local office — passed the Lower House in February 1931 before the House of Peers killed it 184–62 in March 1931 over the 1898 Civil Code's *ie* household system, and the Manchurian Incident buried reform six months later (*Hist.*). Here, under **Hamaguchi's Minseitō cabinet** with Adachi Kenzō at the Home Ministry, the bill keeps its historical scope — cities, towns and villages, not the prefectures — and the Peers are brought round from inside the conservative camp (author's decision, 27 November 2026, 17:47). **The Patriotic Women's Association (*Aikoku Fujinkai*)**, the great conservative women's body founded in 1901 under imperial patronage and led by aristocratic ladies (*Hist.*), comes out in the winter of 1930–31 for the municipal vote as the household's voice on schools, sanitation and prices. That answers the Peers' objection from the *ie* system in the system's own terms: the vote is the mistress of the house speaking for it in the ward and the village, not a woman leaving it. Many peers find their own wives on the other side of the question. The Peers' leaders accept it as a safe, local measure that may also *head off* national suffrage, and **the House of Peers narrowly passes the Municipal Women's Suffrage Act in March 1931**, with the cabinet's spousal-consent compromise intact. Pacifica takes no part. Japanese women cast their **first legal ballots in the municipal elections of spring 1932**, while prefectural and **national Diet suffrage** stay an active campaign. **Real friction remains:** Ichikawa Fusae's league celebrates the ballot while immediately campaigning to strike the insulting spousal-consent clause, and traditionalist Peers and Restorationists attack the Act alongside the *moga* (`CR-088` `A5.2`) as "Honolulu rot."

### A5. The Legal & Political Reckoning of 15 May 1932: Yokosuka, Sealed Magnetic Wire, and Stern Sentences (closes `CR-085` Unresolved #41 at `L1828` & `L2081`) (`APPROVED` D-3 & D-8; legal bridge & specific sentences `PROPOSED` D-17)

On 15 May 1932, Restoration conspirators — young naval officers alongside Army cadets and civilian rightists — assassinate Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi (`CR-085`, `L1814 ("Restoration officers strike against Inukai")`), while Commander Kashiwagi and sympathetic fleet-faction officers attempt to seize Japanese-crewed carriers of the Common Navy's western squadron, foiled when **Kuroda Kiyotada** (`CT-KURODA-SON`), warned by Maria Kashiwagi, holds the fleet loyal (`L1814`). Because the plotters fail to capture Tokyo's *Teishinshō* broadcast transmitter, **Takahashi, acting prime minister from that evening (`L1816`), puts verified statements live on national radio within hours** (coordinating quietly with Thalassia over the scrambled radiotelephone, `L1679`), denying the coup the rumour fog it counted on. Arrests follow within the week (`L1816`).

**The Legal Bridge & Three-Court Split (`L1828 ("which court tries the naval officers")`, `L2081 ("which court tries the naval officers")`):**
Hardliners in Thalassia — among them Samuel Wilder King on the General Staff — ask whether mutiny aboard Common Navy vessels should be tried by a Concord court-martial or referred to the **Common Tribunal** (`L1654`, *"decides competences"*). **Julian and Saitō Makoto, prime minister from 26 May, refuse.** Acting on Saitō's governing principle (*"the army is to be mastered by Japan, as it was in May, or it has not been mastered"*, `L1822`), both capitals rest on the **Concord's domestic-law firewall (`L1654`)**: although Japan has had no separate navy outside the Concord since 1927 (`L1822`), under the reserved-squadron and Japanese home-station regime still in force on 15 May (until consolidated on 1 August 1932), Japanese officers in those units hold Japanese imperial commissions and remain subject to Japan's domestic **Naval Penal Code (*Kaigun Keihō*)** by virtue of the commission, **wherever they serve** — so Kashiwagi's carriers fall under it whether they lay in Japanese waters or off Oʻahu (`L1814`). Jurisdiction attaches on the day of the offence, so the consolidation of 1 August does not move the trials. The court-martial sits at Yokosuka, a station of the common Navy that Japanese officers command (`L1822`), under Japanese law. Both crowns decline to invoke the Common Tribunal, affirming that Concord institutions hold no domestic criminal court in Japan (`PROPOSED`, D-17):
1. **Yokosuka Naval District Court-Martial (Japanese Naval Penal Code):** Tries **all implicated naval officers** — both Commander Kashiwagi's carrier mutineers and the young naval officers who raided Inukai's residence — under Japanese naval law (which already carries capital punishment for rebellion and mutiny). By reciprocal Concord courtesy, **a single Pacifican naval judge-advocate sits in the gallery as an observer** — with **no vote, no bench seat, and no Thalassian appellate authority**.
2. **First Division Army Court-Martial (Japanese Army Penal Code):** Tries the implicated Army officers and cadets (`CR-085`).
3. **Tokyo District Court (Japanese Criminal Code & 1925 Public Order Statute):** Tries the civilian Restorationist conspirators.
4. **Zero Broadcast Theatre — Sealed Magnetic Wire Only (`APPROVED`, D-8):** In our timeline, the 1933–34 May 15 trials degenerated into media-amplified platforms for patriotic speeches, flooding courts with clemency petitions and yielding light sentences (*Hist.*). Here — where radio and sound newsreels reach every town — Saitō's cabinet and the Yokosuka bench **strictly bar radio microphones and sound-newsreel cameras from the courtrooms**, recording testimony solely on **magnetic wire for the sealed judicial record** (`L1203`) and publishing only dry daily bench summaries.
5. **Stern verdicts at real political cost (`APPROVED` stern direction D-3; specific sentences & backlash `PROPOSED` D-17):** The ban does not buy silence: Japan still has an open print press, nationalist pamphlets, and officer messes. **Tens of thousands of clemency petitions still reach Yokosuka and the Justice Ministry**, and Mori Kaku's and Hiranuma's circles denounce `"closed-microphone star-chamber justice to please Thalassia."` Saitō's cabinet and the bench hold firm at real domestic political cost — backed by Emperor Shōwa's unmistakable stand (`L1816`, `L1822`), loyal naval leadership, and a public mourning a murdered premier whose voice every household knew from radio. Proceedings open at **Yokosuka and Tokyo in summer–autumn 1932** and move toward **uncommuted capital and life sentences for Inukai's direct murderers and long penal servitude with cashiering for Kashiwagi and the carrier mutineers** (final sentencing running into 1933, beyond the book's August 1932 horizon, with the firm no-clemency posture unmistakable by July 1932).
