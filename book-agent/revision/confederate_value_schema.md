# Confederate character values for Pacifica

## Working premise

A character's political convictions, personal obligations, and immediate needs operate together. Their relative importance changes with circumstances.

Give each Confederate character three separate answers:

- **Enlistment:** What made joining seem necessary, honorable, or attractive?
- **Persistence:** What keeps him serving after the original expectations have failed?
- **Breaking point:** What could justify disobedience, desertion, surrender, or a change of allegiance in his own eyes?

Preserve the Confederacy's commitment to slavery alongside the variety of individual motivations. A soldier's concern with food, family, or incompetent leadership does not establish his opposition to slavery. Equally, commitment to a slaveholding society does not exhaust his personality. The political anchor is the [Mississippi declaration of secession](https://home.nps.gov/articles/ms-secession.htm) and the [Confederate Constitution](https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/civil-war/preview/breaking-apart/).

The examples below establish possible reactions and patterns of reasoning, not their prevalence. The character applications and scene tests are authorial guidance for the novel, not new historical claims or events already established in the manuscript.

## Core values

| Value | Meaning for the character | Pressure that reveals its limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home and family | Protecting particular people, property, livelihoods, and familiar ways of life. | Serving far away while the household becomes exposed or destitute. |
| Political independence and consent | Preserving his state or community's ability to govern itself. | Richmond's demands, Unionist neighbors, or Pacifica's protection under permanent supervision. |
| Honor and standing | Keeping faith, displaying courage, and receiving recognition as a legitimate soldier or equal partner. | Humiliation, accusations of cowardice, broken promises, or surrender terms that treat him as a criminal. |
| Inherited republican legitimacy | Defending what he understands as the constitutional inheritance of the founding generation. | Emergency government and military power overriding the liberties supposedly being defended. |
| Social order and racial mastery | Preserving a hierarchy he considers legitimate, necessary, or beneficial. | Emancipation, independent Black action, and demands to extend consent beyond his own political community. |
| Reciprocal obligation | Superiors deserve loyalty when they protect, provide, and discharge their responsibilities. | Neglect, favoritism, corruption, avoidable losses, and abandonment of dependents. |
| Comradeship and professional duty | Remaining dependable to men who have shared danger with him. | Orders that expose them needlessly, competing family obligations, or dissolution of the unit. |
| Economic independence | Retaining control of property, trade, livelihood, and the household's future. | Inflation, requisition, interrupted markets, or dependence on a stronger ally. |
| Religious duty | Meeting obligations faithfully and seeking forgiveness, consolation, and meaning. | Loss, misconduct by his own side, unanswered prayers, and uncertainty about providence. |

The constitutional and social framework draws on S, H, P, C, and M in the source register. Those works have different evidentiary status; their interpretations must not become unqualified narrator fact.

## Military experience and competing obligations

### Obedience and deserving leadership

In his January 5, 1863 letter to his mother, James H. Hamner praises Breckinridge's men's courage, describes his voluntary assistance during the fighting, and condemns the retreat. He calls Bragg the army's laughingstock and anticipates desertion among Tennessee troops, adding that he cannot blame them. His claims about enemy movements record his contemporary understanding, not a verified account. [HM]

Separate loyalty to comrades, confidence in a commander, and allegiance to the government. A fictional captain might refuse to sacrifice a company to protect a general's reputation; another officer might regard the refusal as the beginning of military collapse. Both can invoke duty.

### Ordinary acts and comradeship

Jackman's December 31, 1862-January 6, 1863 entries move between the spectacle of battle, narrowly escaped death, cold, tobacco, meals, his brother, letters home, and camp absurdity. [J]

Record the concrete habits through which loyalty is earned. A fictional soldier might trust someone who saves a place by the fire, shares a blanket, carries a message, or remembers where his brother was taken. A Pacifican pay offer could divide a mess if accepting separately would scatter its survivors.

### Necessary hardship and its costs

Peticolas's March 26, 1862 entry describes an urgent march to reinforce Pyron. He emphasizes endurance despite cold, sore feet, and a longer journey than promised. He also describes finding shelter and using women's clothing left in a house for bedding. Scurry's March 30 report combines a claim of battlefield victory with admission that the destroyed supply train prevented continued operations. [T, N]

Ask what makes hardship seem necessary and whose property or comfort absorbs its cost. A fictional officer can believe his men proved their courage against Thorne while recognizing that ammunition, transport, and food have made resistance untenable.

### Standards applied to one's own side

Reproduced passages concerning Morgan's June 1864 Kentucky raid show Guerrant condemning robbery and plunder, crediting enemy steadiness, and criticizing Morgan's delay in bringing assistance. [G]

Give characters specific standards for Confederate conduct. A fictional officer might object because pillage violates property, disgraces the service, injures neighbors, or destroys support for the cause. Those objections arise within his convictions; they do not require a modern political outlook.

### Material fairness and conscience

Watkins's memoir praises leadership that feeds and preserves its men while accusing commissaries of enriching themselves. His account of stealing a destitute family's hog includes remorse and attempted restitution. These are retrospective stories shaped by memory and self-presentation. [W, chapter XI]

Add each character's standard of fair treatment and his method of making amends. Two fictional soldiers might both forage illegally, then respond differently when they learn whose food they took. Their conduct after discovery reveals more than a declaration of honor.

## Daily attention

For each viewpoint character, identify:

- **The person he watches:** a brother, friend, captain, rival, dependent, or enemy.
- **The shortage he feels:** food, sleep, shoes, ammunition, money, information, or affection.
- **The unfairness he notices:** unequal danger, privileges, broken promises, stolen rations, or neglect.
- **The responsibility he postpones:** writing home, paying a debt, confessing wrongdoing, or deciding whether to leave.
- **The fact he cannot yet know:** where his family is, whether an absent friend survived, or whether a retreat was necessary.

This drafting tool is inferred from the selected accounts. It keeps strategic knowledge from flowing automatically into every soldier's mind.

## Different forms of commitment

| Character pattern | What sustains him | What might divide his loyalties |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Convinced separatist | Political independence and constitutional legitimacy. | Richmond or Pacifica coercing the community whose consent he values. |
| Reluctant former Unionist | State, kin, locality, and unwillingness to fight neighbors. | Recognition that those neighbors are themselves divided. |
| Proprietor or paternalist | Property, racial authority, household continuity, and claimed responsibility for dependents. | Dependents asserting purposes he has not authorized. |
| Company loyalist | Familiar men and obligations accumulated through shared danger. | Survival requiring the company to disperse. |
| Professional critic of command | Competent service, military honor, and preservation of fighting strength. | A superior demanding an action he considers wasteful or disgraceful. |
| Disappointed volunteer | Pride, habit, affection, unfinished obligations, and reluctance to abandon previous sacrifices. | Persistence adding suffering without serving his original purpose. |
| Soldier considering departure | Continuing attachment to some obligations despite rejecting others. | Choosing between comrades and people who need him at home. |

These patterns overlap. Disappointment need not produce enlightenment; hardship need not destroy conviction; surrender need not extinguish loyalty.

## Applications to the existing cast

These applications implement CR-012. The canonical dossiers are CT-DAVIS, CT-BENJAMIN, CT-ST-CLAIR and CT-ARMISTEAD. Earlier meetings, assistance, injury dates, transfer terms and overseas appearances remain material for reconciliation with the revised chronology. St. Clair's original 1847 Knights of the Golden Circle affiliation specifically requires reassessment. The following scene tests do not settle those incidents.

### Jefferson Davis

**Dominant values:** sovereignty, legitimate political standing, and public responsibility.

Keep his objection to becoming Thorne's dependent. Give that objection substance: accepting relief might extinguish the independence his government exists to defend. He can understand Pacifica's material superiority while refusing its claim that power confers legitimate jurisdiction. His investment in slavery remains part of the social order he seeks to preserve.

Add the burden of deciding how much suffering sovereignty can justify. A disagreement with Benjamin is stronger if Davis understands the material danger and Benjamin understands what a concession permanently surrenders. His anger may sharpen a coherent argument; it should not replace the argument.

**Scene test:** Benjamin presents terms that could feed an army but permanently transfer a port, railway, or customs revenue to Pacifica. Davis identifies what would remain sovereign after acceptance, and must face the names and needs of those who will pay for refusal.

**Source analogues:** S and C for the argument against coercion; HM for the gap between courage and confidence in command; T and N for distinguishing military courage from strategic feasibility. These are comparative supports, not testimony about Davis's private motives.

### Judah Benjamin

**Dominant values:** institutional survival, enforceable agreements, and responsibility for the consequences of policy.

Give him a positive purpose beyond predicting ruin: a government able to protect civilians, feed soldiers, recover prisoners, and negotiate another day. He understands that principles require surviving institutions; he also knows each concession can reduce what those institutions govern.

Give each compromise a named beneficiary and a precise cost. His controlled legal voice distinguishes temporary arrangements from irreversible transfers of jurisdiction. He can recognize Davis's concern without sharing Davis's judgment about the permissible price of independence. Do not make his Jewish identity an explanation for financial calculation or moral detachment.

**Scene test:** He identifies a concession that preserves immediate life while binding a successor government, and must explain why he believes the obligation is acceptable.

**Source analogues:** S for professional and political standing; HM for morale under disputed command; N for the material limits on military action. The profile is authorial interpretation of the novel's Benjamin.

### Major Julian St. Clair

**Dominant values:** pledged obligation, solidarity, and recognized standing.

His defining grievance is the conversion of a political undertaking into a settled financial account. In the original sketch, assistance represented a relationship, while Pacifica treated repayment with interest as extinguishing obligation. The revised story retains this conflict as a characterization direction; the assistance and bargain require chronological reconciliation. His wound and service can give that grievance bodily stakes without making him incapable of recognizing Pacifica's strength.

Establish what he himself sacrifices to honor a promise. His concern for honor should govern treatment of an inferior as well as exchanges with equals. Preserve his stake in the slaveholding order and its exclusions; personal generosity does not settle that contradiction.

**Scene test:** Better treatment is available if he abandons his escort, wounded subordinate, or named dependent. His decision establishes whether reciprocity is a practiced obligation or only a demand on others.

**Source analogues:** S for professional honor and costly allegiance; G for standards applied to one's own side; W for conscience demonstrated through conduct. These analogues establish possibilities, not a historical identification.

### Colonel Armistead

**Dominant values:** comradeship, professional competence, and continuity after defeat.

His move from Confederate service into Pacifica's Atlantic Auxiliary needs a bridge. Possible motives include preserving survivors as a unit, receiving arrears, securing provisions, supporting households, and continuing the profession he knows. These are prospective characterization choices, not additional manuscript events.

Keep his practical Virginia voice and battlefield observation, but give them a moral object: men whose lives he has been responsible for. His later imperial career is a sequence of decisions. Each new campaign asks whether he still fulfills the obligation that made accepting Pacifican service defensible.

**Scene test:** Pacifica orders him to treat a local insurgency in a manner he once condemned when used against his own country. Does he refuse, restrain implementation, rationalize compliance, or discover that his original accommodation has become permanent service?

**Source analogues:** J for personal networks and limited attention; HM for loyalty despite criticism of command; G for objections to misconduct; S for professional continuity. Western accounts are comparative evidence for this Virginia officer, not evidence that all theaters or units were identical. Armistead is the novel's character; this profile makes no identification with historical Lewis Armistead.

## Western and trans Mississippi settings

**Tennessee and the Mississippi Valley:** make confidence in commanders, access to family, food, requisition, and civilian losses recurrent concerns. A withdrawal can appear necessary at headquarters and intolerable to someone leaving his own district.

**Missouri and Arkansas:** distinguish regular service, partisan allegiance, local defense, and predation through actions. Identify who authorizes a seizure, who benefits, and whether promised protection survives disagreement.

**Texas and New Mexico:** make water, animals, ammunition, routes, local inhabitants, and trustworthy information part of military judgment. Characters encounter Pacifica's power through concrete constraints before comprehending its continental ambitions.

The University of Tennessee and Western Border collections are repositories for selecting further named documents. No single viewpoint belongs to an entire collection.

### Fictional Tennessee sergeant

He enlisted to defend his state and maintain his standing among neighbors. He now remains chiefly because his company depends on him. He distrusts the general but trusts his captain, who shares hardship and explains orders.

His family's circumstances are worsening. Abandoning the company seems shameful, yet he thinks a government unable to protect soldiers' households has incurred an unpaid obligation. He accepts his society's racial hierarchy; resentment of privileged officers has not made him an egalitarian.

Thorne offers provisions and safe passage for disarmament. The sergeant first asks whether the whole company may leave together. Political implications emerge through that immediate obligation. This invented composite is informed especially by HM's distinction between soldiers' courage and commanders' judgment.

## Source discipline and alternate history

Keep separate notes for what a writer witnessed, what he heard or believed, what he later remembered, and what the novelist infers. Absence of a subject from one letter is not evidence that the writer rejected it. Educated survivors and preserved correspondence cannot stand for every soldier.

Transfer plausible habits of thought and human pressures into the alternate timeline. Historical events after the divergence are comparative evidence, not automatic events in Pacifica. Keep character convictions distinct from the Chronicler's retrospective evaluations and from established historical fact.

The canonical [character bible](character_bible.md) and the [registry's](character_registry.json) `confederate_value_profile` fields implement these distinctions. Source identifiers below point to analogues, not proof of fictional scenes or private motivations assigned to historical figures.

## Source register

### Original constitutional and social references

- **S - Raphael Semmes, Memoirs of Service Afloat.** Participant memoir written after the war; chapters VI-VII, XXXI-XXXII, XXXIX, and LVII support political self-description, professional standing, recruitment incentives, and competing military and household duties. Treat its judgments of opponents as the author's claims. Original supplied reference: `Semmes _ Memoirs of Service Afloat.pdf`.
- **H - Hilary A. Herbert, The Abolition Crusade and Its Consequences (1912).** Later participant interpretation; especially chapter VIII on honor, constitutional attachment, paternalism, and racial caste. Its picture of Southern society is not representative testimony from every soldier. Original supplied reference: `Herbert _ The Abolition Crusade and its Consequences.pdf`.
- **P - Ulrich B. Phillips, Economic and Political Essays in the Ante-Bellum South (1909).** A later survey, not a collection of the original essays. Printed pp. 183-191 discuss different paternalist, economic, and constitutional arguments. Original supplied reference: `Phillips _ Economic and Political Essays of the Antebellum South.pdf`.
- **C - Mary Scrugham, Force or Consent as the Basis of American Government.** Later argument on consent and coercion; the pamphlet includes W. H. Townsend's rebuttals. Preserve their disagreement rather than treating the opening argument as a settled finding. Original supplied reference: `Scrugham _ Force or Consent as the Basis of American Government.pdf`.
- **M - Edgar Lee Masters, Lincoln the Man (1931).** Later interpretation of consolidation and republican liberty, especially chapters XIII and XVIII. It does not directly record wartime Confederate soldiers' thoughts. Original supplied reference: `Masters _ Lincoln the Man.pdf`.

## CR-015 continuity for applying these character tests

The historical source passages above are unchanged. Fictional applications now follow the 1865 peace, 1870–1871 failed Union war, customs/debt association and abolition course in CR-015. Davis leaves office in 1868, Johnston serves to 1874 and Gordon follows; Benjamin's later senate/cabinet route remains proposed. Armistead's Auxiliary service is recruitment from an independent partner, with Philippine service possible after 1876. No extinguished Confederacy or 1860s Philippine conquest is required.

The fugitive agreement, free-womb reform, customs-secured compensation and later Gulf secession create new tests of responsibility: which households an officer protects, whose freedom a legal bargain excludes and whether a debt survives the political authority that incurred it. These are scene opportunities, not approved incidents. Alabama stays rump Confederate after 1900; Louisiana, Texas, Arkansas and Sequoyah form the Gulf Republic. Loyalty to a regiment, Richmond and a home state can therefore diverge without reducing every soldier to a fixed faction.


## Later CR-016 continuity

The managed 1887 succession replaces civil war. Kaiulani adopts Julian while his mother lives and raises him as successor; exact instruments remain proposed. The Polytechnic commander is unrelated to the Vances, provisionally Rafael Soberanes. CR-018 identifies Kiyoshi as Fleet-General Kuroda through the approved 1886 adoption/marriage, makes Julian direct successor in 1915 and fixes the surviving stakeholder empire. Japan controls and completes the eastern railway link through Chinese-owned Manchuria. Earlier childhood, founding and wartime value tests remain intact. Vance’s proposed Altai / confession / death arc stays OPEN-018-18; CR-019 gives the triumph a labor and creditor-order cost.
