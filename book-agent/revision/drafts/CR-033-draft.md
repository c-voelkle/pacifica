# CR-033 (draft) — Settlement, Native recovery and the shape of Pacifica's population

**Status:** PROPOSED draft, 27 October 2026; third revision (land finance, titles and the compact form, approved by the author), after two revisions each followed by one fresh-context review. Not adopted: no storyline, register, character or workbook file is changed by this draft. CR-033 is the next free number (CR-032 is used by the separate alternative timelines).
**Basis:** author decisions of 27 October 2026, made while reviewing the population model (`population/README.md`; outputs in `population/outputs/`).

## Author decisions

> Let's divide up that stream. I think most of it would be coming from the Americas and Europe then Japan, then China (Japan would canonically have much higher rates of emigration to Pacifica in this timeline, China also, but not as high as Japan), with Filipino immigration mostly after 1914.

> 1: disease without mass killing. 2: yes, [the sheet's 1846 Californians] include native population. 3: if it can be realistically explained through believable counterfactuals in the alternate timeline, including such as have not yet been made explicit in the canon, I like the 1.2m figure better for 1861. 4: I think a predominance of Japanese immigration also in absolute terms makes more sense for the picture shown in the storyline. It is explained not just by redirected historical emigration flows but also by emigration flows increased by changed circumstances in the alternate timeline.

> I approve the candidate explanations in the population README. I think the 1865 figure should rise. I think the population should continue to rise generously after 1915 too — that's when Pacifica becomes a really attractive place to live. I think the native population should also be modeled as growing after the initial dip. This is explained by fair treatment and generally superior quality of life compared to the historical timeline.

> Now Pacifica is also not in the business of "charity" and it is not in its character to fund another nation out of the goodness of its moral principles. There always has to be a reason why it ends up benefiting Pacifica.

On the proposal that followed (the Republic as sole buyer of Native land, treaties and annuities, no public money for militias, a lasting health gap), the author asked for the draft to be revised accordingly.

> I approve of these decisions.

That approval, given on 27 October 2026, covers the follow-up proposal: how the Republic pays for Native land, how titles become secure, and the legal form of the treaties (A17–A23). Because those decisions rest on the Republic as sole buyer and on the 1851–52 treaties, A5 and A6 are now treated as approved direction too. A7, A8 and A8a stay PROPOSED.

The README explanations the author approved:
- The light foreign-miner licences keep Sonoran, Chilean, Peruvian and Chinese miners.
- Assisted passage for women and families turns sojourners into settlers.
- Secure land titles and a stable republic.
- A Pacific-facing republic draws arrivals from Australia and Chile directly.
- Americans keep moving west to an independent but open California.

## Proposed storyline text

Each insertion is a new paragraph, shown with the existing passage it follows or precedes, quoted exactly. Comparisons with our timeline stay in these notes and in `historical_background.md`, not in the story.

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…Exact incorporation, patent and recruitment measures remain **[OPEN]**." (A4–A9, A8a, A17–A20, A22)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** In 1851 volunteer companies want the state to pay for expeditions against the Native villages. On Elias's figures Vallejo's government refuses: every expedition would be paid from the Republic's own treasury, and a militia that makes its own war is a private army. Commissioners sign treaties with the Native nations in 1851–52 instead, and Elias keeps his promise to the Miwok guide: his people sign first, and their homeland becomes the pattern. A homeland stays the nation's own; it can't be sold, and it can be leased only through the Republic, which keeps a fee. Beyond the homelands only the Republic may buy Native land. It pays in annuities of goods from a Land Fund kept outside the revenues pledged to London, and sells to settlers on instalments that carry the annuities, with the margin paying for assisted passage. Each sale is entered in a public register, kept from 1852 by the Superintendency, and the entry is the title. Militiamen who serve under the Republic's command are paid in land warrants, good only on land the Republic has bought. Licence commissioners with mounted police keep order in the diggings and bring killers to trial. Squatters and volunteer captains call it robbery. Prices, annuity terms and boundaries remain **[OPEN]**.

### §6, after the paragraph ending "…Kamehameha III dies in December 1854 with the American annexation treaty unsigned, and Alexander succeeds as Kamehameha IV." (A4)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Alexander and Lot, who rode with Elias into the foothills, have watched how the Republic treats California's own peoples. Its treaties weigh with chiefs who fear what American settlers would do to theirs.

### End of §8, before the heading "# PART II — The struggle for North America (1861–1867)" (A1–A3, A8, A21)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** The gold rush has settled rather than passed through. The light licences keep the Sonoran, Chilean, Peruvian and Chinese miners whom the American miners wanted taxed out. Assisted passage brings women and families. Holders of the old grants who come forward get a surveyed patent within months and pay its cost in land, which many Californio grantees call a tithe on their own property; squatters buy their improved plots from the Land Fund on instalments. More miners buy land and stay. Pacific shipping brings Australians and Chileans as well as Americans moving west. By 1861 the Confederation counts about 1.2 million people **[MODEL]**. Measles, smallpox and malaria meanwhile sweep the Native villages of California. From 1861 men of the treaty nations scout and guard the Sierra routes and the telegraph.

### §25, after the paragraph ending "…The depression, Coxey's Army and Pullman in 1893–1894 give the weekly further material." (A10, A23)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Protected in their homelands and paid for their work, Native Californians, reduced by disease to about two-thirds of their number at the gold discoveries, begin to grow again in the later 1890s. The Office's county tables show them poorer and sicker than their neighbours. The bureau's own tables thus sit uneasily beside its comparisons with Wounded Knee. The treaties hold, but long leases at low rents, annuity claims signed over to merchants for debts, and court rulings on boundaries and water keep moving land out of Native hands, and the Realm earns a fee on every lease. By 1932 they number about 130,000 **[MODEL]**.

### §26, after the paragraph ending "…family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans." (A11–A13)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** With Korea independent, Manchuria Chinese and Japan's eastern holdings drawing few settlers, Japanese emigrants have been choosing Pacifica in growing numbers; in the 1900s their arrivals overtake the Chinese. With Japanese families admitted under the compact, the lead widens, and by 1932 Japanese-origin Pacificans outnumber Chinese-origin ones, about 4.6 against 4.3 million **[MODEL]**. Filipino migration stays small until the Philippine crown joins Julian's, and grows after it.

### §33, before the paragraph beginning "Shared infrastructure, technical education and investment also lift the partners." (A14–A15)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Pacifica itself keeps growing fast. Its public health ranks with the best in the world, families stay large and immigrants keep coming. By 1932 Pacifica, Hawaii included, counts about 30 million people **[MODEL]**, though selective entry flatters the bureau's health and examination tables.

## Impact notes

- **Dependency:** the §5 promise of a Miwok homeland (PROPOSED — CR-028) fits A5–A6, but they don't require it. The §5 paragraph on squatters shedding blood at Sacramento in 1850 stays. The new paragraph follows it in time and carries the same conflict forward.
- **Unresolved-items list:** items 4 (obligations to Indigenous polities), 6 (private land titles, Indigenous rights) and 7 (Indigenous claims) are narrowed by CR-033. The §5 anchor's "recruitment measures remain **[OPEN]**" stays: the margin funds assisted passage, but its measures and amounts stay open. - **1860 Pyramid Lake (§8, existing canon):** Elias recommends negotiation but agrees to Hays's relief force of about 500 volunteers to keep support for the naval programme. Under A7 this is a campaign the Republic commands, so it's allowed. It is also the one time Elias pays for volunteers against his own rule, which sharpens the existing "decisive warning" rather than contradicting it.
- **What the compact prefigures (A22):** the same tools return later in canon:
  - the lease that alienates nothing (Puʻuloa 1874, §17; Midway, §14; the Thalassia ground, §17);
  - the compact only the other party can undo (Sulu 1878, §15);
  - revenue collected for another polity against a fee (the Formosan convention of 1895, §25);
  - the land board selling on instalments, which "becomes a landlord too" (the friar estates, §15);
  - the loan serviced from the asset's own gain, with published accounts (the Philippine Redemption Loan, §15);
  - and the land-title register as a condition of membership (the Gulf compact, §25).

  California in 1851 is where Elias first puts them together. The parallels belong in the notes, not the prose.
- **Who pays (A17–A19):** the London pledge on royalties and customs (§5) is why the Land Fund is separate. The annuities in goods recirculate money to Pacifican merchants. The land warrants line up the miners' interest with the treaties. The margin gives A1's assisted passage a source of money.
- **Bear River, 1863 (§10, existing canon):** "a Pacifican atrocity … under a local commander" stays. A4 rules out a policy of mass killing in California, not every massacre. Bear River is the kind of local crime A7's commanders and courts are meant to prevent, and it happens in territory joined that same year, before the compact system reaches it. Union critics' later use of it (§25) still lands.
- **Why the Republic benefits (A4–A8):**
  - treaties and annuities are cheaper than war for a state that pays its own bills;
  - recorded purchases give clean title, which London will lend against;
  - the land margin is public revenue (§5 already counts land among the Republic's revenues);
  - a monopoly of force against private armies, in line with §5's "public government cannot coexist with private courts";
  - credibility with the Hawaiian partners;
  - labour, and wartime scouts.

  The reasons belong in the notes, not the prose. Only the Hawaiian watchers appear on-page.
