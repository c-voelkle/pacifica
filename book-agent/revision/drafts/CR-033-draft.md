# CR-033 (draft) — Settlement, Native recovery and the shape of Pacifica's population

**Status:** PROPOSED draft, 27 October 2026; second revision (Native policy as Pacifican self-interest), each revision followed by one fresh-context review. Not adopted: no storyline, register, character or workbook file is changed by this draft. CR-033 is the next free number (CR-032 is used by the separate alternative timelines).
**Basis:** author decisions of 27 October 2026, made while reviewing the population model (`population/README.md`; outputs in `population/outputs/`).

## Author decisions

> Let's divide up that stream. I think most of it would be coming from the Americas and Europe then Japan, then China (Japan would canonically have much higher rates of emigration to Pacifica in this timeline, China also, but not as high as Japan), with Filipino immigration mostly after 1914.

> 1: disease without mass killing. 2: yes, [the sheet's 1846 Californians] include native population. 3: if it can be realistically explained through believable counterfactuals in the alternate timeline, including such as have not yet been made explicit in the canon, I like the 1.2m figure better for 1861. 4: I think a predominance of Japanese immigration also in absolute terms makes more sense for the picture shown in the storyline. It is explained not just by redirected historical emigration flows but also by emigration flows increased by changed circumstances in the alternate timeline.

> I approve the candidate explanations in the population README. I think the 1865 figure should rise. I think the population should continue to rise generously after 1915 too — that's when Pacifica becomes a really attractive place to live. I think the native population should also be modeled as growing after the initial dip. This is explained by fair treatment and generally superior quality of life compared to the historical timeline.

> Now Pacifica is also not in the business of "charity" and it is not in its character to fund another nation out of the goodness of its moral principles. There always has to be a reason why it ends up benefiting Pacifica.

On the proposal that followed (the Republic as sole buyer of Native land, treaties and annuities, no public money for militias, a lasting health gap), the author asked for the draft to be revised accordingly. Those elements are therefore PROPOSED below, not approved canon.

The README explanations the author approved:
- The light foreign-miner licences keep Sonoran, Chilean, Peruvian and Chinese miners.
- Assisted passage for women and families turns sojourners into settlers.
- Secure land titles and a stable republic.
- A Pacific-facing republic draws arrivals from Australia and Chile directly.
- Americans keep moving west to an independent but open California.

## Proposed storyline text

Each insertion is a new paragraph, shown with the existing passage it follows or precedes, quoted exactly. Comparisons with our timeline stay in these notes and in `historical_background.md`, not in the story.

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…Exact incorporation, patent and recruitment measures remain **[OPEN]**." (A4–A9, A8a)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Volunteer companies want the state to pay for expeditions against the Native villages. On Elias's figures Vallejo's government refuses: every expedition would be paid from the Republic's own treasury, its credit sits in London, and a militia that makes its own war is a private army. Instead, commissioners sign treaties with the Native nations in 1851–52 that reserve their homelands, and the Republic ratifies them. The Miwok guide's people sign first; the homeland that was his price becomes the pattern. Only the Republic may buy Native land. It pays annuities in goods from Pacifican merchants and resells or leases the land to settlers, railways and companies at a margin; each acre that changes hands carries a title a London bank will lend against. Native workers sign on for wages in the diggings and on the ranchos. Licence commissioners with mounted police keep order in the diggings and bring killers to trial. Squatters and volunteer captains call it robbery. Prices, boundaries and the legal form of the homelands remain **[OPEN]**.

### §6, after the paragraph ending "…Kamehameha III dies in December 1854 with the American annexation treaty unsigned, and Alexander succeeds as Kamehameha IV." (A4)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Alexander and Lot, who rode with Elias into the foothills, have watched how the Republic treats California's own peoples. Its treaties weigh with chiefs who fear what American settlers would do to theirs.

### End of §8, before the heading "# PART II — The struggle for North America (1861–1867)" (A1–A3, A8)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** The gold rush has settled rather than passed through. The light licences keep the Sonoran, Chilean, Peruvian and Chinese miners whom the American miners wanted taxed out. Assisted passage brings women and families. As titles under the old grants are confirmed, by means still **[OPEN]**, more miners buy land and stay. Pacific shipping brings Australians and Chileans as well as Americans moving west. By 1861 the Confederation counts about 1.2 million people **[MODEL]**. Measles, smallpox and malaria meanwhile sweep the Native villages of California. When war comes, men of the treaty nations scout and guard the Sierra routes and the telegraph.

### §25, after the paragraph ending "…These routes sustain the industrial economy while leaving a class of workers outside citizenship." (A10)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Protected in their homelands and paid for their work, Native Californians, reduced by disease to about two-thirds of their number at the gold discoveries, begin to grow again in the later 1890s. The Office's county tables show them poorer and sicker than their neighbours. The treaties hold, but markets, debt and the courts keep moving land out of Native hands, and the Republic profits from every sale. By 1932 they number about 130,000 **[MODEL]**.

### §26, after the paragraph ending "…family immigration and civic equality for Japanese Pacificans." (A11–A13)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** With Korea independent, Manchuria Chinese and Japan's eastern holdings drawing few settlers, Japanese emigrants have been choosing Pacifica in growing numbers; in the 1900s their arrivals overtake the Chinese. With Japanese families admitted under the compact, the lead widens, and by 1932 Japanese-origin Pacificans outnumber Chinese-origin ones, about 3.9 against 3.6 million **[MODEL]**. Filipino migration stays small until the Philippine crown joins Julian's, and grows after it.

### §33, before the paragraph beginning "Shared infrastructure, technical education and investment also lift the partners." (A14–A15)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Pacifica itself keeps growing fast. Its public health ranks with the best in the world, families stay large and immigrants keep coming. By 1932 Pacifica, Hawaii included, counts about 30 million people **[MODEL]**, though selective entry flatters the bureau's health and examination tables.

## Impact notes

- **Storyline:** six new paragraphs (§5, §6, end of §8, §25, §26, §33), each with a hidden CR-033 coverage comment. No existing story sentence changes, and each insertion sits in the section whose dates it covers. The §5 licence compromise, the 1888 selective-entry law (§25) and the Hapa passages (§15, §26) are unchanged and consistent.
- **Dependency:** the §5 promise of a Miwok homeland (PROPOSED — CR-028) fits A5–A6, but they don't require it. The §5 paragraph on squatters shedding blood at Sacramento in 1850 stays. The new paragraph follows it in time and carries the same conflict forward.
- **Unresolved-items list:** items 4 (obligations to Indigenous polities), 6 (Indigenous rights) and 7 (Indigenous claims) are narrowed by CR-033. On adoption, add "narrowed by CR-033: treaties and sole-buyer purchase; terms OPEN" to each, as earlier CRs did.
- **1860 Pyramid Lake (§8, existing canon):** Elias recommends negotiation but agrees to Hays's relief force of about 500 volunteers to keep support for the naval programme. Under A7 this is a campaign the Republic commands, so it's allowed. It is also the one time Elias pays for volunteers against his own rule, which sharpens the existing "decisive warning" rather than contradicting it.
- **Why the Republic benefits (A4–A8):**
  - treaties and annuities are cheaper than war for a state that pays its own bills;
  - recorded purchases give clean title, which London will lend against;
  - the land margin is public revenue (§5 already counts land among the Republic's revenues);
  - a monopoly of force against private armies, in line with §5's "public government cannot coexist with private courts";
  - credibility with the Hawaiian partners;
  - labour, and wartime scouts.

  The reasons belong in the notes, not the prose. Only the Hawaiian watchers appear on-page.
- **Historical background:** on adoption, add the divergences to `historical_background.md`, with our-timeline anchors and sources:
  - our timeline's punitive 1850 Foreign Miners' Tax and the departure of Latin American miners;
  - the Native Californian decline (Madley 2016);
  - Burnett's 1851 message and California's state-funded, federally reimbursed militia campaigns;
  - the 1850 Act for the Government and Protection of Indians (bound labour);
  - the eighteen treaties of 1851–52, rejected by the US Senate in 1852;
  - Crown pre-emption and the Native Land Court in New Zealand, and Canada's mounted police;
  - the Māori life-expectancy benchmark (Te Ara);
  - Japanese emigration and colonial settlement (MOFA 1985; Discover Nikkei);
  - the New Zealand and Canadian benchmarks.
- **Assumptions:** DEM-01 in `war_base_assumptions.md` already reads about 1.2m (1861) and 1.7m (1865).
- **Character documents:** none affected.
- **Costing (not yet done):** Chryseis should net the policy against the workbook: annuities, survey, commissioners and police against the land-sale margin, avoided war debt and better credit. The draft asserts that it pays; the calculation should confirm it before adoption.
- **Financial workbook:** unchanged. It differs from the model on Native Hawaiians and Native Californians (the sheet grows both with everyone), on the origin of its talent stream, and on growth after 1915. These belong in the workbook change list, not this CR.

- Whether the Native Californian turn in the later 1890s should hang on a named event, such as a public-health law reaching the homelands. Canon gives none, and the model's dates are MODEL.
- Whether to name the treaty commissioners, or the instrument that makes the Republic sole buyer.
- Whether the population figures (1861, 1932, Japanese and Chinese) belong in the storyline or only in these notes.
- The model's 1895 total, about 6.8m, sits just above the CR-015 range of 4–6.5m. Raise the range, or accept the overshoot as a consequence of A1–A2.
