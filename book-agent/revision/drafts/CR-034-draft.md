# CR-034 (draft) — From the secret find to a destination, and the Polytechnic's first decade

**Status:** PROPOSED draft, 27 October 2026; first revision. Not adopted: no storyline, register, character or workbook file is changed by this draft.
**Basis:** author decisions and brainstorm of 27 October 2026. Builds on CR-028 (the October 1847 find and the Monterey silence) and draft CR-033 (settlement, titles, Native policy, about 1.2 million people in 1861).

## Author decisions

> Next, we should add a more concrete step-by-step timeline of the gold discovery and the establishment of Pacifica's prestige and desirability as a destination.

> Yes let's keep it in October 1847.

> Found the Polytechnic's practical nucleus during the 1848–1850 gold rush, combining an assay service, analytical laboratory and technical instruction. Gold revenues fund its development … Recruitment should proceed through paid commissions, credible appointments and demonstrated working conditions. Researchers remain because they acquire students, colleagues, collections and productive programmes. Elias earns their confidence by honouring scientific independence—including unfavourable findings.

The author's brainstorm also names the first recruits and their approach:
- **Genth** about 1850–51, through commissioned analyses after his return to Philadelphia in August 1850.
- **Zeuner** about 1853–54, through Weisbach, before his historical Zürich appointment of 1855.

It names the strongest early achievements:
- difficult gold ores, possibly with Deetken and chlorination;
- water flow and machinery performance;
- steam-engine design validated in working installations;
- Hallidie's wire rope;
- Ericsson's arrival about 1855, strengthening an institution already running.

> I would like the prestige discovery to be moved even earlier, so that Pacifica can be established as a prestigious and serious player before the war breaks out. 1858 or so would be good. Zeuner can become a recruiter based in Freiberg after 1871.

## Proposed storyline text

Each change is shown with the existing passage it follows or replaces, quoted exactly.

### §4, replace "Elias pledges royalties from a find that only five people in the world know of" with "Elias pledges royalties from a find that only seven people in the world know of" (A3)

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…then large numbers of Americans arrive as foreigners subject to Californian law." (A4)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The opening is managed. The decree of October 1848 opens the gravels to anyone who registers a claim and pays the royalty, and the first newcomers are already at work. In December President Polk tells Congress that there is gold in the Republic of California. By then San Francisco's harbour master is registering every ship and every passenger, and the tens of thousands who come in 1849 land with a paper naming the law they will live under. Hundreds of sailors desert the American squadron for the diggings. The treaty of 1848 obliges the Republic to return them, and its sheriffs find few; it is the first quarrel under the convention.

### §5, after the paragraph ending "…separate from public gold, public debt and the nation's annual mineral production." (A6–A9)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The assay office is older than the mint. It began in the secret winter as the room at Carquinez where Elias tested the Mokelumne samples, with two assistants sworn to the Republic's silence, and its first rule was the government's own: silence, but no lies. With the decree it moves to San Francisco as the State Assay Office, because a royalty assessed at the mint is only as good as the assay behind it. From 1849 no one may sign an assay for the Republic without passing its examination, the first the state sets. In 1850 the office finds the coins of a private mint favoured by the merchants underweight, and Elias publishes the result over their protests. Frederick Genth, back in Philadelphia that summer from a failed mine in North Carolina, analyses Californian ores on commission. In 1851 he accepts the Republic's offer: a laboratory, assistants, a salary that does not depend on clients, the right to publish and time for his own chemistry.

### §8, after the paragraph ending "…banking arrangements and the scale of the difference from historical eastern access." (A5)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The rush becomes a settlement in the years when it could have ended. From 1852 the Superintendency pays the passage of women and of whole families; the same year twenty thousand Chinese land at San Francisco. When gold is found in Victoria, Australian diggers sail home, but many stay, because a claim, a town lot or a farm in California now comes with a registered title. By 1853 the easy gold is gone. Water companies turn hydraulic monitors on the old river gravels, quartz companies sink shafts at Grass Valley, and the men who remain become wage-earners, mechanics and shareholders. In February 1855 the failure of Page, Bacon's parent house in St. Louis brings down San Francisco's leading bank and two hundred firms with it. The mint's bullion and the Superintendency's credit carry the solvent houses through, and the Republic's money keeps its value. Emigrant guides in New York, Hamburg and Canton begin to say so.

### §8, after the paragraph ending "…**[OPEN]** Facilities, budgets, suppliers, armament, dimensions and construction schedule." (A8, A10–A13, A15)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** Since 1852 the laboratory has been a school beside the Carquinez foundries. In 1853 Genth reports that the Superintendency's own quartz mill loses most of the gold locked in sulphides, and Elias prints the report. Gustav Zeuner fought on the Dresden barricades in 1849 and may never teach at a Saxon university. He comes in 1854 on the word of his teacher Julius Weisbach to build the engineering course: mathematics first, then the testing floor and the works. He measures the water the hydraulic companies buy by the miner's inch and the debris they wash into the rivers, and he tests the engines Ericsson builds at the foundries. Andrew Hallidie, who came as a boy in 1852, brings his wire bridges and the first wire rope for the quartz mills to its testing machine. In 1858 the school publishes two results that travel. One is Zeuner's method for slide-valve gear, worked out on Ericsson's engines. The other is Genth's analysis of quartz from Carson Hill: he identifies a gold telluride that ordinary milling cannot touch, and shows that roasting and chlorine recover it. Freiberg reviews both. In 1859 the first assay of the Washoe ore is made at Nevada City by an assayer the Republic licensed, and San Francisco's money moves before Washington's officials arrive. By 1860 the school calls itself the Polytechnic, students apply from Europe and Japan, and engineers who have never seen the Pacific know where Carquinez is. **[OPEN]** Contracts, salaries, buildings and the order of later appointments.

### §7, after the paragraph ending "…This restraint preserves the independence of the two powers even as their fortunes become increasingly interconnected." (A13)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** The *Kanrin Maru*'s engineers spend their days at Carquinez, among machines whose drawings they have studied, and leave asking for places for Japanese students.

### §19, after the paragraph ending "…turn the examination state's learning into firms, equipment and careers." (A10)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-034]** Its first chairs stayed for what they had built. When Saxony's amnesty of 1862 would have let Zeuner teach at home, he kept his students, his testing floor and his programme. He goes back only in 1871, to direct the Mining Academy at Freiberg, and from Freiberg he sends Pacifica its engineers.
