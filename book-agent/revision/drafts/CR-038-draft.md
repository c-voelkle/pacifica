# CR-038 (draft) — The machinery's own safeguards: retirement, a common college, inspectors, hard numbers, bonded scholarships, official pay and county halls and a trades congress (1887–1890s)

**Status:** PROPOSED draft, 28 October 2026; third revision, adding three borrowings from Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew (F6–F8), a chartered trades congress (F9) and the background to the 1890 guest-worker programme (F10–F11). Not adopted: no storyline, register, character or workbook file is changed by this draft.
**Basis:** author decision of 28 October 2026, after a comparison with the Chinese Communist Party's personnel system under Deng Xiaoping and his successors. Builds on the 1887 charters (§24), the Office of Public Measures (§25) and draft CR-037 (no cliques).

## Decisions

| ID | Decision | Label |
|---|---|---|
| F1 | **Retirement at 68.** Apart from the Truce's named holders, who keep their offices for life (§22), every estate head and senior officer appointed under the charters of 1887–88 retires in the year they turn 68, so each generation leaves on schedule and no clique sits for life. The Crown, the Regent and the Senate's life seats are outside the rule, and the Office keeps its twelve-year heads (§24) under the same age limit. Soberanes, a Truce holder, retires by his own choice in 1915, the year he turns 68 (born 1847). This is the Deng-era fix (the informal rule that officials of 67 may stay and those of 68 go) made into law. | APPROVED (author) |
| F2 | **A common college.** From 1889 the old Council offices at Monterey, which the government left for Thalassia in 1885 and which stand beside the Court of the Realm, house the Realm College. Rising officials of the Polytechnic, the Office, the navy, the chartered champions and the counties spend a year there together, mixed by origin and language. Their loyalty runs to the Realm and to their year rather than to an estate or a community. The model is the Communist Party's Central Party School. The college is also where the "Carquinez men" learn to recognise one another, a clique of the examined. | APPROVED (author); name, date and course PROPOSED |
| F3 | **Inspectors.** The Office sends inspectors, unannounced, to check the counties' returns on the ground. They report to the Regent and publish. They check the estates only through the returns the estates themselves publish, because the Crown does not touch the estates' appointments or charters (§24). The model is the Party's inspection tours. | APPROVED (author); mechanics PROPOSED |
| F4 | **Numbers that are hard to fake.** Every county return is checked against figures that are hard to falsify: freight tonnage, coal and power used, savings deposits, burials and vaccination records. The lesson comes from Chinese growth targets, which officials gamed. It is Victoria's honest numbers with an instrument. | APPROVED (author) |
| F6 | **Bonded scholarships.** From 1888 a state scholarship to the Polytechnic or a League college binds its holder to five years in the service of the Realm, an estate, a chartered champion or a county, or repayment. The ablest of every origin start their careers inside the state's institutions rather than against them. The model is Singapore's Public Service Commission scholarships. | APPROVED (author); length of bond PROPOSED |
| F7 | **Benchmarked official pay.** From 1888 the pay of senior officials is set each year against what the chartered champions pay their managers, and published. An official earns less than a manager but enough that corruption does not pay; the CR-038 inspectors and the CR-037 audits are the other half of the bargain. The model is Singapore's ministerial pay. | APPROVED (author); formula PROPOSED |
| F8 | **Chartered county halls.** From the 1890s each county may charter a hall, open to every resident, that offers what hometown associations, clan houses and congregations offer their own: sick and burial funds, interpreters, night classes, notices of work and help with papers. Measures grants pay for halls that meet their standards. The hall does not replace those bodies; it competes with them, so that no one needs a clan to get by. The model is Singapore's People's Association. It answers CR-037's problem of hidden ties. | APPROVED (author); funding and governance PROPOSED |
| F9 | **A chartered trades congress (1895).** After the Bread March of 1894, Victoria wants Pacifican workers to have the lawful channel the Union's marchers lacked, and a congress of the trades is chartered by law. It keeps sick, accident and burial funds; it keeps the register of tested tradesmen, whose tests the Polytechnic sets (including the guest workers' test for residence); and it publishes, with the employers' associations and the Office as arbiter, a reference table of wages and conditions that contracts may cite. **Cautions:** (1) It never controls hiring, training or pay: no closed shop, hiring hall or gang of its own, so contractors keep recruitment, training, payment and control of the work (CR-031-R1). (2) It is chartered by law, not affiliated to the Crown, so that no wage dispute becomes a dispute with the Crown. (3) Independent unions stay legal; the congress wins members by its benefits (CR-031-R1 bans no association or collective bargaining). (4) It stays in the background like the guest permits: CR-020 took the strike and labour-charter arc out of the story. (5) Guest workers may join. | APPROVED (author); date and details PROPOSED |
| F10 | **Background: Hawaii's inspected contracts.** Historically the first Chinese contract labourers reached Hawaii's sugar plantations in 1852, under the 1850 Masters and Servants Act's penal contracts, in which a worker who quit could be jailed; Japan's government-sponsored contract emigration followed in 1885–94. In Pacifica, after joining the Confederation in 1856, the kingdom's legislature replaces penal contracts with inspected ones, against the planters' resistance: inspected housing, protected wages, paid passage home and the right to change employer after a contract. Plantation labour becomes a free-labour market in line with CR-031-R1. | APPROVED as background (author); year and legislative route OPEN |
| F11 | **Background: contract crews for the great works.** In the 1860s and 1870s the Sierra railway and the interior links hire fixed-term crews, many of them Chinese, on contracts with return passage, inside the years of the open door. Canon's Chinese construction crews keep their say over working conditions. These contracts give the Realm its first experience of running temporary labour fairly. | APPROVED as background (author); details PROPOSED |
| F12 | **The general programme stays in 1890.** Before 1888 the door is open, and those who might come as guests come as settlers, which serves Pacifica better: settlers become citizens, taxpayers, soldiers and examinees. The 1890 programme copies the Hawaiian contract (F10) and the works crews' contracts (F11) after two years of labour shortage following the 1888 restriction. | APPROVED (author) |
| F5 | **What is not copied.** No term limit is lifted for anyone; anti-corruption is never used as a purge; and there is no assimilation by force. These stay temptations for Julian, whose test is OPEN (CR-036). | PROPOSED |

## Proposed storyline text

Each paragraph is shown with the existing passage it follows, quoted exactly. Where CR-037 inserts after the same passage, CR-038's paragraphs follow it.

### §25, after the paragraph ending "…Julian later uses commissions and ordinances more freely, giving county oligarchies reason to fight through their senators." (F1–F4)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-038]** Victoria and Soberanes make the machinery guard itself. Apart from the Truce's named holders, every estate head and senior officer appointed under the charters retires in the year they turn sixty-eight, so that no clique grows old in office. From 1889 the old Council offices at Monterey, beside the Court, house the Realm College, where rising officers of the Polytechnic, the Office, the navy, the champions and the counties spend a year sharing rooms with strangers of other origins and languages. The Office's inspectors arrive in a county without warning. They check its returns against what is hard to fake, freight tonnage, coal and power, savings deposits and burials, and they publish what they find.

### §25, directly after the CR-038 paragraph above (F6–F8)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-038]** The Regent also pays for loyalty in advance. From 1888 a state scholarship binds its holder to five years in the Realm's, an estate's or a county's service, and the clever children of every county and origin start their careers inside the machine. Each year the Senate sets senior officials' pay against what the champions pay their managers and prints the two side by side, so that an honest official is well paid and a corrupt one has much to lose. In the 1890s, under a Realm enabling law, the counties begin to charter halls open to every resident, with sick and burial funds, interpreters, night classes and notices of work. A newcomer no longer needs a clan house or a congregation to find a job or bury a child.

### §25, after the paragraph ending "…These routes sustain the industrial economy while leaving a class of workers outside citizenship." (F10–F12)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-038]** Victoria's draughtsmen take the guest contract from Hawaii's plantations, where the kingdom abolished penal labour contracts over the planters' protests after joining the Confederation, and from the railway and interior works, which have hired their crews on fixed terms since the 1860s. Inspected housing, protected wages, the passage home and a free choice of employer at the end of the term become the Realm's law. **[OPEN]** The year of Hawaii's reform.

### §25, after the paragraph ending "…The affair damages silver populism and revanchism while exposing the exclusions of Victoria's settlement." (F9)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-038]** Victoria wants Pacifica's own workers to have the lawful channel the marchers lacked. In 1895 a trades congress is chartered by law. It keeps sick and burial funds and the register of tradesmen who have passed the Polytechnic's trade tests, guest workers among them, and with the employers it publishes a reference table of wages that contracts may cite. It hires no one and trains no one; the contractors keep command of their work.

## Impact notes

- **Storyline:** four new paragraphs in §25, each with a hidden CR-038 coverage comment. No existing sentence changes.
- **Review:** one fresh-context review; applied: the Truce holders' life offices and the Senate's life seats exempted from retirement; the Office's twelve-year heads kept; the Court still at Monterey; the congress no longer trains or sets pay and the Polytechnic sets the trade tests; the Bread March link given a motive; the Hawaiian reform dated after 1856 with its year OPEN; paragraphs given actors and placed after the county standards, the guest-worker programme and the Bread March; the Senate sets official pay; halls under a Realm enabling law.
- **Existing canon kept:**
  - §24: the Crown does not touch the estates' charters or appointments. The retirement rule is in the 1887 charters themselves, and the inspectors check the estates only through their published returns.
  - §25: Victoria's honest numbers and the county standards. The inspectors give the standards teeth.
  - §28: Soberanes retires in 1915.
  - §17: the Seat of Government Act moves the offices from Monterey in 1885; the Court of the Realm stays there (§21).
  - §22: the Truce's named holders keep their offices for life.
- **Dependencies:** CR-037 (no cliques) sets the rules; CR-038 gives them machinery. Adopt them together or CR-037 first.
- **Character documents:** Soberanes (F1) and Victoria (F3, F4) gain a line each on adoption.
- **Historical background:** on adoption, add the Chinese Communist Party's retirement norms, Central Party School, central inspection tours and the problem of gamed growth figures, with sources (see the sources below).
- **Commitments ledger:** on adoption, add the retirement condition to the Polytechnic charter entry (and the other chartered estates) in `commitments/data/ledger.toml`; no new commitment.
- **Labour canon:** CR-031-R1 (contractors keep recruitment and control; no ban on association) and CR-020 (no strike or labour-charter arc) are kept by F9's cautions.
- **Guest workers:** canon's 1890 programme (§25) is unchanged; F10–F12 give it a past. The Hawaiian and works contracts fall inside the workbook's existing arrival streams, so the workbook needs no change.
- **Workbook:** none now. Scholarships, official pay and county halls fit inside existing public-administration and measures-grant spending; a later costing could check that.
