## Japan — 1858 treaty, 1860 naval visit

**Binding fictional chronology:** `Shimoda agreement 1855 → Treaty of Edo 1858 → Kanrin Maru visit March 1860 → guaranteed transition at Edo in 1868 and equal alliance in 1870–1871`.

Historical context:
- Abe Masahiro died in 1857, Shimazu Nariakira in 1858, and the new shogunal leadership made treaty negotiations more politically perilous.
- The 1858 date is the author's explicit correction and overrides conflicting tentative dates in the original `rise_to_power.md` table and earlier planning text.
- The *Kanrin Maru* historically crossed to San Francisco in 1860 with Nakahama Manjirō as interpreter. It is **not** the occasion for a new Treaty of Edo in the fictional timeline; it illustrates and deepens the treaty signed two years earlier.

## Founding date, fiscal and constitutional accuracy

- **31 May 1846 was Pentecost Sunday** in the Gregorian calendar; a fictional Santa Barbara declaration on that date is symbolically coherent, although the real council did not create this republic.
- **31 July 1856**, the fifteenth anniversary of Hawaiian Sovereignty Restoration Day, is the approved founding declaration of the Confederation; the two member legislatures ratify in September and November.
- **November 1858** is Elias and Victoria’s marriage after the Confederation and Edo negotiations (CR-027).
- Elias remains Californian **First Minister and Revenue Superintendent** while becoming confederal Chancellor after the 1856 ratification. He acquires the temporary title **Protector** only after Article XIV's first invocation in 1861.
- **Washoe 1860** is a coalition victory: Victoria persuades Hawaiian institutions to withhold state assent. **1864–1865** supplies the working dissolution/election and enabling settlement, adopted through amendment majorities and member ratifications. Lot retains the Kuhina Nui; confederal guarantees concern state institutions rather than a personal countersignature.
- Model values in the uploaded document (population `1.2m`, annual revenue `$24m`, wartime army `180k`, fleet `90` ships) are not historical reference facts. The feasibility audit and planned war_base_assumptions.md remain pending; these figures have not been validated.

## High-impact unresolved mechanics

1. **1846**: Why Sloat extends his hesitation beyond the historical July 7 occupation, what Frémont's irregulars do, how Pico and Castro are reconciled, and why the crisis ends without American annexation.
2. **Debt**: Which lender committee receives a California debt assumption, who authorizes Mexico's recognition, and how early payments are funded before the rush.
3. **Revenue**: Claims register, exact royalty assessment, rights on grants and tribal lands, bullion assay, borrowing, and treasury legitimacy.
4. **Hawaii:** CR-027 resolves Victoria’s identity, retained Kuhina Nui, 1866 survival, 1872 accession and the lease/ordinances distinction. The exact 1874–1876 instruments, judgment wording, Kiyoshi disclosure and scene-level factions retain their OPEN/PROPOSED labels.
5. **Family chronology**: CR-022 and CR-027 settle earlier relationships: Silas is born in 1851 to Abigail Merritt and Kiyoshi in 1859 to a Satsuma mother. They precede the lost child’s later-1870s death; that is no longer an unresolved chronological choice. Victoria’s discovery of Kiyoshi and the lost child’s precise dates remain open.
6. **Japan**: How the Californian treaty supersedes the historical Perry mission, how Japanese domain and court politics remain plausible, and how currency reforms interact with treaty terms.
7. **Institutional dates**: The 1852 revenue office should not be described as the sovereign or first Providentor's presidency; later titles depend on the American wars.

## CR-037 — keeping institutions open (28 October 2026)

- **Anonymous and recopied scripts.** From the Song dynasty, China's examinations covered candidates' names and had clerks recopy scripts so that examiners could not recognise a hand; Pacifica adopts both from the late 1870s.
- **The law of avoidance.** Qing officials could not serve in their home provinces or examine their kin; the Chinese Communist Party still rotates officials away from home ([Christian Science Monitor, 1990](https://www.csmonitor.com/1990/1114/ochin.html)). Canon already applies the principle to Commissioners (1887) and the Philippine Gobernadores.
- **The passed-over test.** The two-thirds threshold is the examination state's counterpart of the "four-fifths" rule that U.S. federal agencies adopted in 1978 for adverse impact in hiring; it is a measure of exclusion, not a quota.
- **Divergence.** No historical state combined these rules with published appointment tables; the 1890 audit and the county rule are fictional.

## CR-039 — loose ends in the commitments (28 October 2026)

- **Japanese place names.** From the transfer onward, canon uses the names Japan itself used. Urajio (浦塩, short for 浦塩斯徳 Urajiosutoku) was the established Japanese name for Vladivostok before 1945. Karafuto (樺太) was Japan's name for Sakhalin and for its southern territory from 1905 to 1945. Enkaishū (沿海州, "coastal province") was the Japanese name for the Russian Primorye; this timeline gives it to the province Japan takes in 1899. The Razdolnaya is named only at the transfer. Further names for places that join Japan follow the same rule when they enter the story.
- **Treaties ended by war.** In nineteenth-century practice war between the parties ended most political and commercial treaties between them, and the peace treaty said which ones revived. The 1865 peace's ending of the 1848 and 1860 conventions follows that practice; the storehouse sealed in 1861 is consistent with it.
- **Querétaro's pledge.** Mexico's interest in 1847 was to keep California from the United States; the narrowed pledge (never to join the United States or cede territory to a foreign power) is the fictional convention's, not a historical text.
- **Nanking and Nanjing.** The Treaty of Nanking (1842) fixed China's tariff by treaty, and the foreign-run Inspectorate followed from 1854. Historically the Nationalist government recovered tariff autonomy in 1928–1930. The fictional Treaty of Nanjing of 1928 gives that recovery to a treaty of guarantee with the Concord.
- **The Gulf and the canal.** Canon's ranges for the Gulf Republic (1899–1900) and the canal's opening (around 1900–1902) meet only in 1900. Historically Colombia's Thousand Days' War ran from 1899 to 1902.

A. Mikhailov, Yarmosh; Bukeikhanov, Yermekov, Tokhtamyshev) sat 29–30 July and 2–3 August 1918 and agreed a draft on mixed institutions under joint commissars in Akmolinsk, Semipalatinsk and Turgai, Alash courts and kibitka tax, a Kirghiz army organised on the Siberian army's plan under common command, land status quo; Alash withdrew mutual recognition; Yarmosh dissented that the political relationship was undefined (Sbornik, docs 41–43). The Provisional Siberian Government recognised only national-cultural autonomy (July 1918 "Fundamental regulations"; Ministry of Native Affairs under Shatilov); Sapozhnikov at Ufa rejected separatism; Bukeikhanov's Ufa speech of 12 September 1918 for a Russia "federated, democratic and unified"; plans for a federal union of autonomous Siberia, Alash, Bashkiria and Turkestan were proposed (D. Amanzholova, "The movement for Siberian regional and national autonomy in the early 1920s", Sibirica 3:1, 2003, pp. 89–91). Alash militia 13,500 planned, about 9,500 in September 1919 ([Alash militia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alash_militia)); Bukeikhanov's February 1919 proposal to Omsk for Alash cavalry on the Cossack model was refused; the Turkestan Front RVS amnesty of 4 November 1919; Western Alash's assembly of 10 December and its capture of the Iletsk Corps headquarters at Kyzyl-Kuga on 27 December 1919; the Kirghiz MRC's liquidation order of 9 March 1920; Kazakh ASSR 26 August 1920 (Amanzholova 1997, "Defeat", pp. 49–83). **Divergences:** the tri-partite treaty (signed in the storyline at Omsk on 4 August and Samara on 6 August 1918, after the commission's sittings), the restored recognition clause, the Emba concession (signed by the Western Alash Orda, whose ground is the Uralsk oblast) and the Tomsk vote of 1921 are fictional; the storyline's Japanese line-guard battalions as far as Omsk and the Altai branch are fictional (historically Japan's troops stayed east of Baikal); the historical Alash–Siberia quarrel over north-eastern Kazakhstan continued between the Siberian and Kirghiz revolutionary committees in 1920 (ibid., p. 75).
- **Attendance at Tomsk.** Whether Alash delegates sat at the October and December 1917 Siberian congresses beyond Bukeikhanov's reported speech remains VERIFY (M. V. Shilovsky's work on Alash Orda and Siberian regionalism; S. Sabol, Russian Colonization and the Genesis of Kazak National Consciousness, 2003, ch. 8).

## CR-042 — Huludao (30 October 2026)

Winter ice conditions: **VERIFY**; canon does not call the harbour ice-free.

Pacifica's trust is the alternate timeline's version of the attempt to give Manchuria an outlet no foreign power holds.
- **The line to the gulf.** The Peking–Mukden line (Imperial Railways of North China) was financed by a British loan of 1898 through the British and Chinese Corporation (**VERIFY** amount and terms). **Divergence:** the Mukden–Jinzhou–Huludao railway is built in 1908–1910 by a British and Pacifican syndicate, Britain senior (CR-042 H6).

- **The Harbin–Mukden line.** Historically the southern branch of the Chinese Eastern Railway, built by Russia in 1898–1903. **Divergence:** a Chinese line through Changchun to Mukden by 1912 (CR-042 H4); builder and finance PROPOSED.


## CR-043 — the peace of 1919 (31 October 2026)

- **Special mediation.** The 1907 Hague Convention for the Pacific Settlement of International Disputes treats good offices and mediation in Articles 2–8. Article 8 recommends "special mediation": each side chooses a power, the two mediating powers deal directly, the parties stop direct communication on the dispute for the mandate (thirty days unless stipulated otherwise), and "in case of a definite rupture of pacific relations, these Powers are charged with the joint task of taking advantage of any opportunity to restore peace" (wording verified by the author, 30 October 2026; [Convention text, Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/pacific.asp)). **Divergence:** first used to end a general war, with Pacifica a belligerent at sea and the Union neutral (CR-043 P2).
- **William Jennings Bryan.** Secretary of State 1913–1915. He signed some thirty "cooling-off" conciliation treaties in 1913–14; in August 1914 he advised Wilson against loans to belligerents, money being "the worst of all contrabands"; he resigned on 9 June 1915 rather than sign the second Lusitania note ([Office of the Historian](https://history.state.gov/departmenthistory/people/bryan-william-jennings)). Died 26 July 1925. **Divergence:** the Union's mediator at The Hague.
- **The end of the war.** The Salonika offensive broke the Bulgarian front in September 1918 and Bulgaria signed an armistice at Salonika on 29 September; the Ottoman armistice was signed at Mudros on 30 October 1918 aboard HMS *Agamemnon*, negotiated by Britain alone. **Divergence:** the 11 November armistice is signed on the line Germany then holds, inside France and Belgium (CR-043 P5).
- **The Peace Palace and Carnegie.** The Peace Palace at The Hague opened in 1913, built with Andrew Carnegie's gift; Carnegie died on 11 August 1919.
- **Strasbourg, November 1918.** A soldiers' and workers' council took power in Strasbourg on 10–11 November 1918 and a Republic of Alsace-Lorraine was proclaimed; it ended when French troops entered the city on 22 November ([Alsace-Lorraine Soviet Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alsace-Lorraine_Soviet_Republic); **VERIFY** the dates against a printed source). **Divergence:** the republic survives because the armistice line keeps the French army out, and becomes neutral in union with Switzerland (CR-043 P7).
- **Austria-Hungary.** Emperor Karl's manifesto of 16 October 1918 proposed turning the Austrian half of the monarchy into a federation of national states. The Fundamental Articles of 1871 (the Hohenwart ministry) would have given the lands of the Bohemian crown a status like Hungary's; they were withdrawn in October 1871. **Divergence:** the peace binds Karl to his manifesto, and the Bohemian lands become a federal kingdom (CR-043 P8, detail PROPOSED).
- **The Czechoslovak army in France.** A French decree of December 1917 created an autonomous Czechoslovak army under French command, of which the Legion in Russia was formally part (**VERIFY** the decree's date, 16 or 19 December 1917); France recognised the Czechoslovak National Council as the supreme organ of the nation in June 1918 (**VERIFY** the day). **Divergence:** France pays the Legion in francs from the Entente credit Pacifica raised (CR-043 P9), and withdraws its recognition under the Bohemian settlement.
- **Kocher.** Theodor Kocher of Bern, Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1909 for his work on the thyroid; by the end of his career he had performed some five thousand thyroid operations at a mortality near half a per cent (**VERIFY** the figures). Staged operations (ligation of the thyroid arteries before partial resection) were the practice of the period for toxic goitre.
- **Kaʻiulani.** Princess Kaʻiulani (1875–1899), schooled in England from 1889; caught a chill riding in the rain on the island of Hawaiʻi in late 1898 or January 1899 and died on 6 March 1899; the illness is recorded as inflammatory rheumatism, and exophthalmic goitre is also reported. **Divergence:** Kocher's operation in 1897 and her survival (CR-043 P1).
- **Germany's crown.** Wilhelm II's grandson Wilhelm, eldest son of Crown Prince Wilhelm, was born on 4 July 1906. **Divergence:** Wilhelm II abdicates for him under a regency (CR-043 P12; the regent OPEN).

## CR-044 — Omsk, the hostages and the Russian war's loose ends (31 October 2026)

- **The Omsk coup, 17–18 November 1918.** On the night of 17 November, at a city banquet in honour of General Janin, officers demanded "God Save the Tsar"; the Directory ordered the singers arrested, and Colonel Volkov arrested the Directory's left wing before he could be arrested himself ([Kolchak Coup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolchak_Coup)). Janin reached Omsk only in December 1918 (**VERIFY** the date). Ivan Mikhailov, the finance minister, was the coup's political organiser and patron, not present at the arrests (Budberg, Syromyatnikov via Ilyin, Parfenov via Vologodsky; Gins dissents); see the project dossier on Mikhailov and M. M. Stelmak and D. I. Petin, *Istoricheskii vestnik* XLII (2022), [PDF](https://historicalreporter.ru/upload/iblock/698/2qnwfc4sw5xa6n8jjl4qixrj371b50aq.pdf). **Divergence:** the coup fails by morning (CR-041 N2).
- **The British at Omsk.** Major-General Alfred Knox headed the British military mission; Colonel John Ward MP commanded the 25th Middlesex, whose memoir is *With the "Die-Hards" in Siberia* (1920). How far the British were party to the coup is debated: Lt-Col. Syromyatnikov's letter of April 1919 says Colonel Neilson and Captain Steveni assured him of Allied neutrality if the coup was bloodless; R. G. Gagkuev judges this evidence of contact and neutrality, not of British planning, and finds the "British conspiracy" version largely sourced from the French mission ([Gagkuev, *Istoricheskii vestnik* XLVII, 2024](https://www.historicalreporter.ru/upload/iblock/686/m6xfk0pcnun8vch65sv8mn9n4qs70ata.pdf)). **Divergence:** Knox's foreknowledge of several days, Ward's battalion standing by, and Volkov's papers documenting both are PROPOSED canon (CR-044 C3).
- **Cromie.** Captain Francis Cromie, British naval attaché, was killed resisting Cheka men at the British embassy in Petrograd on 31 August 1918. His burial place is **VERIFY** (often given as the Smolensky Lutheran cemetery). **Divergence:** the Pacifican advisers are shot in the same week, and the Pacifican and British dead are buried together after Petrograd falls (CR-044 B2).
- **Stalin and Krasnaya Gorka.** In June 1919 Stalin directed the recapture of the Krasnaya Gorka fort, mutinous for the Whites, from the sea, and reported to Lenin that he deplored the naval science that called it impossible. **Divergence:** he goes south to Georgia when Moscow falls; the Democratic Republic of Georgia (1918–1921, Menshevik-led) does not give him up (CR-044 B6).
- **Dzerzhinsky.** Felix Dzerzhinsky founded and led the Cheka from December 1917. **Divergence:** he is the commissar on Kronstadt in May 1920 and dies by Kiyoshi's cut (CR-044 B6).
- **The Dowager Empress and the British crown.** Maria Feodorovna was the sister of Queen Alexandra; in 1917 George V withdrew the offer of asylum to Nicholas II's family. **Divergence:** Victoria meets the British royal party at the Romanov burial (CR-044 B4).
- **Lenin's birthday.** Lenin was born on 22 April 1870 (New Style). **Divergence:** taken at the Kotelnich bridge on his fiftieth birthday (CR-044 B1).
- **Patton.** George S. Patton was born at San Gabriel, California, on 11 November 1885; grandson of Col. George S. Patton of the 22nd Virginia, mortally wounded at Third Winchester (1864), and of Benjamin Davis Wilson, mayor of Los Angeles in 1851; fifth in the modern pentathlon at the 1912 Olympics; designer of the M1913 cavalry sabre; in May 1916 he led a raid in three cars at San Miguelito that killed Julio Cárdenas. **Divergence:** a Pacifican cavalry officer who commands *Pele* at Kotelnich (CR-044 C5); the 1916 raid OPEN.
- **The Gannenmono.** The first group of Japanese emigrants reached Hawaiʻi in 1868, the first year of Meiji. **Divergence:** Nagai Shōzō, an invented grandson, is a Polytechnic engineer (CR-044 C5).
- **Pele.** The Hawaiian goddess of fire and volcanoes, who in the chants travels from Kahiki across the sea to Hawaiʻi. **Divergence:** the name of the Pacifican armoured train (CR-044 A1, superseding *Māui* in CR-041 N10).

## CR-045 — Omsk in depth: Mikhailov, the paymaster and Maria (31 October 2026)

- **Ivan Adrianovich Mikhailov.** Finance minister of the Provisional Siberian Government from 1918 at twenty-six; called Vanka-Kain, Ivan Intriganovich and the Siberian Machiavelli by his enemies; the political organiser and patron of the 18 November coup, absent from the arrests and late to the morning Council. In Harbin he headed the Chinese Eastern Railway's economic bureau and worked on *Zarya*, *Kharbinskoe vremya* and the *Manchurian Economic Review*; deported to the USSR in 1945 and shot on 30 August 1946. Birth date **VERIFY**. Sources: the diaries of Vologodsky, Ilyin and Budberg; Serebrennikov, *Velikii otkhod* and his memoirs, ch. III; Krol, *Za tri goda*; Gins, *Sibir', soyuzniki i Kolchak* (istmat.org/node/25476); Stelmak & Petin; Zhuravlev 2011. Full dossier in the project's `Mikhailov-dossier.md`. **Divergence:** he sells the coup to Pacifica on 17 November 1918 and is shot at Harbin by Volkov in January 1928 (CR-045).
- **The September crisis of 1918.** A closed session of the Siberian Regional Duma on 18 September resolved to bring A. E. Novoselov into the government; on the night of 20–21 September Colonel V. I. Volkov arrested Krutovsky, Shatilov, Yakushev and Novoselov; Novoselov, who would not sign, was shot by his escort in the Zagorodnaya grove on 22–23 September; on 24 September Czech counter-intelligence (Colonel Zaichek) tried to arrest Mikhailov, who hid in Annenkov's barracks; Argunov's investigation found nothing to charge him with, and Volkov swore he had no accomplices (Serebrennikov, pp. 396–401; Gins; Zhuravlev 2011). **Divergence:** the Czech search is called off on Pacifican orders (CR-045 M3).
- **After the night.** Volkov, Krasilnikov and Katanaev were acquitted by an emergency court on 21 November 1918; Volkov was promoted major-general and sent to Chita. His later fate **VERIFY**. **Divergence:** the coup fails, and Volkov is cashiered (CR-045).
- **Grishin-Almazov and Maria.** General A. N. Grishin-Almazov, first commander of the Siberian Army, was dismissed in September 1918 and went south to Denikin; on 5 May 1919, crossing the Caspian with Denikin's letters to Kolchak aboard the *Leila*, he was taken by the Red destroyer *Karl Liebknecht* and shot himself. Maria Alexandrovna Grishina-Almazova (1891–1976), a former Khabarovsk café singer, kept a monarchist salon at Omsk and was Mikhailov's mistress; her diary of 17 January 1919 records a fortune-teller's prophecy; she refused Mikhailov's proposal at Irkutsk on 3 January 1920 on her way to Japan, was imprisoned by the Political Centre and the Cheka for about eleven months, married Mikhailov at Harbin in 1921 (son Georgy, born 25 October 1921, later at Waseda), and died in San Francisco in 1976 (Petin, *Noveishaya istoriya Rossii* 9:2, 2019; *Radio Svoboda*). **Divergence:** the steamer that takes the *Leila* is out of Astrakhan; she refuses Mikhailov, goes to Japan and marries Commander Kashiwagi (CR-045 G2–G5).
- **The Alaskan Kashevarovs.** A. F. Kashevarov (1809–1870), a Creole navigator of the Russian-American Company, surveyed the Arctic coast of Alaska in 1838 and rose to general's rank in Russian service (**VERIFY** dates and rank); the Kashevaroff family remained at Kodiak and Sitka. **Divergence:** the paymaster Pavel Kashevarov is invented (CR-045 M1).
- **Corrections.** London gave the Omsk government no formal recognition after November 1918; the Allies' conditional support came in May–June 1919. The Provisional Siberian Government's farewell charter of 3 November 1918 cites its declaration of independence of 4 July 1918.

## CR-046 — The Petrograd account (31 October 2026)

- **Rasputin's murder.** Rasputin was killed at the Yusupov palace on the Moika on the night of 16–17 December 1916 (Old Style). Andrew Cook (*To Kill Rasputin*, 2005) argued that Oswald Rayner of the British intelligence station fired the last shot; Douglas Smith (*Rasputin*, 2016) found no trace of British involvement in the service's files, and Samuel Hoare, head of British intelligence in Russia 1916–17, held that Russian rightists tried to frame the British ([Oswald Rayner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oswald_Rayner)). **Divergence:** a British payment for "the December affair" (CR-046 P1).
- **The palace-coup plot.** Guchkov, with General Krymov and others, planned in the winter of 1916–17 to force Nicholas II to abdicate in March 1917; the February Revolution overtook them. British fear of a separate peace and the embassy's contacts with the Duma opposition are documented; British money in the plot is invented. **Divergence:** CR-046 P1.
- **The banks.** The Bolsheviks nationalised the banks in December 1917 and seized their records.
- **Copenhagen, 1920.** On 12 February 1920 James O'Grady and Maxim Litvinov signed the Anglo-Soviet agreement for the exchange of prisoners at Copenhagen ([UK treaty record](https://api.parliament.uk/uk-treaties/treaties/6792); *Historical Journal*, "Lloyd George and the Copenhagen Conference of 1919–1920"). **Divergence:** gold for the Cheka's British file (CR-046 P1).
- **Steveni.** Leo Steveni (1893–1972) joined the British military mission in Petrograd in 1915 and was Knox's liaison officer at the White Army's Omsk headquarters from November 1918 to November 1919 ([King's College London, Liddell Hart Centre](https://archives.kingscollections.org/index.php/steveni-leo-1893-1972-colonel)).
- **Hoare and Churchill.** Hoare was Secretary of State for Air 1922–24 and 1924–29; Churchill was Secretary of State for War 1919–21 and Chancellor of the Exchequer 1924–29.
- **Kamo.** Simon Ter-Petrosian, "Kamo" (1882–1922), led the Tiflis bank robbery of 1907, was arrested in Berlin later that year and died in Tiflis on 14 July 1922 when a lorry struck his bicycle. **Divergence:** Stalin's courier to Ankara in 1921 (CR-046 P6).
- **1927 and 1928.** The Metropolitan Police raided the Soviet trade organisation Arcos in London on 12 May 1927, and Britain broke relations with Moscow that month; Japanese officers of the Kwantung Army killed Zhang Zuolin on 4 June 1928. **Divergence:** the raid on Pacifica's Moorgate agency (CR-046 P7); Stalin killed in Tiflis in 1921 (P6).

## CR-047 — The three districts (31 October 2026)

- **The three districts.** Kars, Ardahan and Batum (the Ottoman *elviye-i selâse*) passed to Russia in 1878. Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) returned them subject to a vote, which the Ottomans held under occupation in July 1918; more than 100,000 Armenians were expelled from the Kars province. The National Pact of 1920 claimed the districts on the 1918 vote and accepted a new plebiscite if needed.
- **1919–1921.** The South-West Caucasus Republic governed Kars until Armenian and British forces took it in April 1919; Georgia held Ardahan; Britain held Batum until July 1920. Karabekir took Kars on 30 October 1920. The treaties of Alexandropol (3 December 1920), Moscow (16 March 1921) and Kars (13 October 1921) gave Kars, Ardahan and Surmalu (Iğdır and Ararat, formerly of the Erivan province) to Turkey, and Batum to Georgia with autonomy for its Ajarian Muslims and free transit for Turkish goods.
- **The people.** 1897 census, Kars oblast (290,654), by mother tongue: Kars okrug 134,142 (Armenian 34.8%, Karapapakh 16.4%, Russian 12.6%, Greek 11.0%, Turkish 7.9%, Kurdish 6.8%); Kağızman 59,230 (Armenian 36.6%, Kurdish 29.9%, Greek 12.2%, Turkish 8.7%); Olti 31,519 (Turkish 62.6%, Kurdish 11.1%, Armenian 9.9%); Ardahan 65,763 (Turkish 42.7%, Kurdish 19.1%, Karapapakh 12.0%, Greek 11.9%) ([Kars oblast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kars_oblast) and the okrug articles). The 1916 *Kavkazskii kalendar'* figures by nationality show the Armenian share rising in Kars and Kağızman; their "Roma" category looks like a misreading in the secondary tables. The Upper Silesia plebiscite of 20 March 1921 let natives who had moved away vote.
- **Divergence:** no Turkish–Armenian war in 1920; a Pacifican-supervised vote by district on the 1914 rolls; Kars and Kağızman to Armenia, Olti and Ardahan to Turkey; Ararat Armenian; Batum a Georgian free port without a garrison (CR-047).

## CR-048 — the rump Confederacy and the long emergency (31 October 2026)

Census figures for the five states are recalled from the Census Bureau's compilation (Gibson & Jung, *Historical Census Statistics on Population Totals by Race, 1790 to 1990*, Working Paper 56, 2002) and remain **VERIFY**; Georgia's totals for 1910–1930 were spot-checked. The population model's closure and departures are in `population/neighbours.py` and `population/outputs/neighbours.md`.

- **The five states in 1900.** South Carolina 1,340,316 (58.4% Black), Georgia 2,216,331 (46.7%), Alabama 1,828,697 (45.2%), Mississippi 1,551,270 (58.5%), Florida 528,542 (43.7%): together 7,465,156, 50.7% Black. **Divergence:** about 7.1 million and 48% Black after a generation of departures under the Freedom Act; about 9.15 million and 29% Black in 1932 against 10.97 million and 39% historically (MODEL, CR-048 R1).
- **Cotton.** The boll weevil crossed the Mississippi about 1907, reached Alabama about 1909, Georgia in 1915 and South Carolina in 1917; cotton prices collapsed in 1920. Alabama leased convicts to the Birmingham mines until 1928. Mississippi's Parchman farm in Sunflower County opened in 1901.
- **Dividing a state's debt.** Gran Colombia's debt was split by population in 1834 (New Granada 50%, Venezuela 28.5%, Ecuador 21.5%); the treaties of Saint-Germain (1919) and Lausanne (1923) divided Austrian and Ottoman debt by the revenue each territory had yielded. **Divergence:** the Protocol of Richmond of 1898 uses the security and the beneficiary as its rules (CR-048 R2).
- **Readmission.** Congress readmitted Virginia in January 1870 on fundamental conditions, among them that its constitution never deprive any citizen or class of citizens of the vote except for crime. Section 4 of the Fourteenth Amendment repudiated Confederate debt and compensation for emancipation. North Carolina's suffrage amendment of 1900 and Virginia's convention of 1901–1902 disfranchised most Black voters. George Henry White, the last Black congressman of the period, left North Carolina's Second District in 1901. White supremacists overthrew Wilmington's elected government on 10 November 1898. **Divergence:** Virginia readmitted in 1898 and North Carolina in 1899 on such conditions, with ten years of federal supervisors in North Carolina; White holds his seat until 1910 (CR-048 R9).
- **Harpers Ferry.** John Brown raided the arsenal on 16 October 1859 with money from six Northern backers; Virginia hanged him on 2 December 1859. **Divergence:** the Parchman raid of 16 October 1910 under the invented Josiah Haynes (CR-048 R14).
- **Universities and oaths.** Columbia dismissed Cattell and Dana over the war in 1917. Howard University has received an annual federal appropriation since 1879. **Divergence:** the Parchman Act of 1911 attaches oaths to the emulation programme's grants (CR-048 R10).
- **Du Bois and Washington.** Du Bois taught at Atlanta University from 1897 to 1910 and directed the Atlanta Studies; his data charts were shown at the Paris Exposition of 1900; he founded the Niagara Movement at Fort Erie in 1905, helped found the NAACP in 1909 and edited *The Crisis* from November 1910. Booker T. Washington spoke at Atlanta in 1895 and led Tuskegee until his death in 1915; Carnegie gave Tuskegee $600,000 in 1903; Rosenwald's school programme began in 1912. Franz Boas gave Atlanta University's commencement address in 1906.
- **Ernest Everett Just.** Born in Charleston on 14 August 1883; at Howard from 1907; first Spingarn Medal 1915; doctorate at Chicago 1916; worked summers at Woods Hole and, denied a research post in the United States, in Naples, Berlin and Paris from 1929. **Divergence:** Carquinez fellow from 1920 and director of the Moku o Loʻe station from 1922 (CR-048 R11). The station sits on the islet that became the Hawaii Institute of Marine Biology in our history.
- **Violence.** Atlanta (22–24 September 1906), stoked by Hoke Smith's campaign; Black residents of Brownsville armed and defended their district. Springfield, Illinois (August 1908); the second Klan refounded on Stone Mountain (1915); *The Birth of a Nation* (1915); East St. Louis (July 1917); Chicago (July 1919); Tulsa (31 May–1 June 1921); the Greenville levee camp in the 1927 flood. The Stone Mountain carving began in 1923.
- **Marcus Garvey.** Born at St. Ann's Bay, Jamaica, in 1887; timekeeper for United Fruit in Costa Rica and editor in Colón, 1910–1911; in London 1912–1914; founded the UNIA in 1914 and came to Harlem in 1916; the *Negro World* from 1918, the Black Star Line from 1919, the Madison Square Garden convention of August 1920, the Liberia programme of 1920–1924. He met Edward Young Clarke, the Klan's acting Imperial Wizard, in Atlanta in June 1922. Convicted of mail fraud in June 1923, imprisoned in the Atlanta federal penitentiary from 1925, his sentence commuted in November 1927, deported to Jamaica in December 1927. British colonies banned the *Negro World*; British and American officials exchanged intelligence on him. ### CR-049 atomic branch, reworked (approved 2 November 2026)

Anchors for requirements 1–5 of the rework.

- **Joachimsthal.** Adolf Patera's process for uranium compounds from pitchblende went into production at a new works at Joachimsthal in 1853, making uranium yellows for glass; its radium-rich residues were what the Curies later worked ([Adolf Patera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Patera); [ORAU, Jáchymov](https://orau.org/health-physics-museum/articles/jachymov-cradle-of-the-atomic-age.html)). **Divergence:** Carquinez buys the residues from the 1860s.
- **Becquerel and the Curies.** Radioactivity 1896; polonium and radium 1898; Pierre Curie and Albert Laborde showed in 1903 that radium salts give off heat continuously ([Pierre Curie, Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/pierre-curie-lecture.pdf)). Owens (1899) traced erratic thorium readings to air currents carrying an active gas; Rutherford and Soddy's transformation theory followed in 1902 ([Rutherford on the thorium emanation](https://web.chemteam.info/Chem-History/Rutherford-half-life.html)). **Divergence:** 1861, 1868–1869, 1867–1870 and 1877 (CR-049 S1).
- **Cathode rays and the electron.** Plücker deflected the discharge glow with a magnet in 1858; the Sprengel pump dates from 1865; Hertz failed to deflect cathode rays electrically in 1883; J. J. Thomson measured their charge-to-mass ratio in 1897. Eugen Goldstein (born Gleiwitz, 1850, to a Jewish family; a student of Helmholtz) named cathode rays in 1876, worked at the Berlin Observatory from 1878 to 1890 and then at Potsdam, and found canal rays in 1886 ([Eugen Goldstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugen_Goldstein)). **Divergence:** Goldstein at Carquinez from 1876; the electron 1879 (CR-049 S1).
- **Helium.** Found in the solar spectrum in 1868. William Francis Hillebrand (born Honolulu, 12 December 1853; doctorate at Heidelberg 1875; Leadville assay office 1879; Geological Survey laboratory at Denver from 1880) took a gas from uraninite for nitrogen; Ramsay found helium in such minerals in 1895 ([W. F. Hillebrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Francis_Hillebrand)). **Divergence:** Hillebrand Pacifican by birth; helium identified in 1881 with Morley.
- **Scintillation and the nucleus.** Théodore Sidot prepared phosphorescent zinc sulfide in 1866 ([Théodore Sidot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%A9odore_Sidot)); Crookes's spinthariscope (1903) showed single alpha flashes on it ([ORAU, spinthariscopes](https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/spinthariscopes/index.html)); Geiger and Marsden counted scattered alpha particles by eye in 1909; Rutherford's nuclear atom followed in 1911. **Divergence:** 1883 (CR-049 S1).
- **Isotopes.** Soddy's chemically inseparable radioelements and Thomson's positive-ray masses (1913); Richards's atomic weight of lead from uranium ores (1914). **Divergence:** 1884–1886.
- **Valves.** Edison observed current crossing the vacuum from a hot lamp filament in 1880–1883; Fleming's valve dates from 1904 and de Forest's triode from 1906–1907. **Divergence:** Pacifican valve amplifiers c. 1895, from naval wireless (CR-049 S2).
- **Atomic weights and counting.** Morley's determination of the oxygen–hydrogen ratio was published in 1895; Rutherford and Geiger's gas-multiplying counter for single alpha particles dates from 1908. **Divergence:** Morley's ratio 1884–1885; a counter at Ralston College 1885–1887.
- **The neutron.** Bothe and Becker (1930) and Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot (1932) produced a penetrating radiation from beryllium under polonium alpha particles and took it for gamma rays; Chadwick showed in 1932, from proton and nitrogen recoils, that it was a neutral particle of about the proton's mass ([APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2007/05/may-1932-chadwick-discovery-neutron)). Nagaoka Hantarō (born 1865) graduated in physics at Tokyo in 1887, studied in Berlin, Munich and Vienna from 1893 and proposed his Saturnian atom in 1904 ([Hantaro Nagaoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hantaro_Nagaoka)). **Divergence:** neutron 1899, confirmed 1901; Nagaoka in Pacifica from 1887.
- **Fission.** Hahn and Strassmann found barium among the products of neutron-irradiated uranium in December 1938; Meitner and Frisch interpreted it as splitting, and Frisch measured the large ionisation pulses, in early 1939; neutron multiplication was measured later in 1939; the first controlled pile went critical at Chicago on 2 December 1942. **Divergence (re-paced):** fission 1908, neutron multiplication 1909, a research installation c. 1913 (CR-049 S2).
- **Western Slope uranium.** Tom Talbert found yellow uranium-vanadium ore on Roc Creek in 1881; carnotite was found in western Montrose County in 1897–1898 (named by Friedel and Cumenge in 1899); ten tons of ore of about 20% uranium oxide were shipped from the Copper Prince claim in 1898; Standard Chemical began the Colorado radium boom at Paradox Valley in 1910 ([Timeline of mining in Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_mining_in_Colorado)). Under the 1865 line the Western Slope is Pacifican and Leadville is Union (master storyline). **Divergence:** Western Slope ore assayed 1881–1882 and mined for uranium from 1893.
- **Graphite and uranium metal.** Acheson's electric-furnace graphite dates from 1896; Moissan made uranium metal in the electric furnace in the 1890s. **Divergence:** Tesla's furnaces in the 1890s.

## CR-050 — the sealed envelope and the walled college (3 November 2026)

Historical anchors for the secrecy settlement, the light isotope, the halls and the knock-ons. Checked by an independent reviewer in a fresh context on 3 November 2026; the divergences are in the storyline and CR-050.

Arthur Jeffrey Dempster (born Toronto 1886; Toronto M.A. 1910; Göttingen, Munich and Würzburg from about 1911; to the United States in 1914; Chicago Ph.D. 1916; mass spectrometer 1918) ([AIP](https://history.aip.org/phn/11506018.html)). **Divergence:** Meitner's conjecture 1911–1912, Dempster's companion c. 1915, proof 1916–1917.

Costs through 1945: Hanford about $390 million, Y-12 about $478 million, K-25 about $512 million ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/the-costs-of-the-manhattan-project/)).

Acheson graphite (1896) carried neutron-absorbing boron; purer graphite and purified uranium were obtained in months in 1942.
- **Hahn and Meitner.** Hahn and Meitner worked together in Berlin from 1907; Hahn served in chemical warfare in the First World War; Meitner's beta-spectra work ran through the 1910s and 1920s; Hahn received the 1944 chemistry prize alone (Rhodes). **Divergence:** Meitner in Pacifica from about 1910; Hahn rediscovers fission in 1920.

- **Radiation and health.** Härting and Hesse described the Schneeberg miners' disease as a malignant lung tumour in 1879 (they classed it as lymphosarcoma; it was later confirmed as lung cancer) ([National Academies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK218116/)); the link to radon was proposed in the 1920s–1930s; the International X-ray and Radium Protection Committee was founded in 1928; Boltwood's radiometric dating 1907; Hevesy's tracers 1913; Hess's cosmic rays 1912. ## CR-071 — the states dissolved and the 124 counties (integrated 18 November 2026)

Approved by the author in parts between 15 and 18 November 2026 and integrated on 18 November 2026, local labels retained, after CR-072 and CR-073 (K-1). The decision record is [the outline](CR-071-outline.md) and its parts: [D](CR-071-D-meiji.md), [E](CR-071-E-preparation.md), [F–G](CR-071-FG-triggers-act.md), [the addendum](CR-071-G-addendum.md), [H](CR-071-H-resistance.md) and [I](CR-071-I-map.md). - **The model of the method: Meiji (Part D).** *History:* the return of the domain registers (1869); the abolition of the domains and creation of the prefectures (August 1871), with the new government assuming the domains' debts and paper; the Iwakura Mission (1871–73); the commutation of the samurai stipends (1876); the Satsuma rebellion (1877). *Divergence:* Elias watches the abolition as a co-financier, and part of Japan's loan of 1873 is placed in San Francisco. The comparison of frictions (H5) is the CR's reasoning, not the Chronicler's.

- **The anti-Chinese movement (H6, H-17).** *History:* the Workingmen's Party of California (1877–79) and Denis Kearney (1847–1907); Frank Roney (1841–1925), Fenian moulder and trades organiser; the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882); the Rock Springs massacre (2 September 1885) and the Hells Canyon massacre (May 1887), whose killers were acquitted in 1888; the expulsions of 1885–86 at Eureka, Truckee and Nicolaus (to verify). *People v. Hall* (1854) barred Chinese testimony against whites. *Divergence:* Rock Springs and Hells Canyon do not happen. The drivers are mostly absent: no penal or credit-ticket labour, family migration, one court for all, naturalization by English and not by race, and no crash of 1875. The Wallowa is the Nez Perce's country. In their place is the Feather River case of 1886, tried before the Confederal Court. Pacifican nativism is a grievance of status and ethos, the author's comparison being Malaysia's (H-18).
- **The eight-hour day (H7.2).** *History:* California's eight-hour law of 1868 for public works (unenforced) and the federal law of 1868 for federal workers; the eight-hour movement of the Knights of Labor (1880s).
- **The Pacific Companies (CR-071 H6.5, H7.8; CR-074).** *History:* the Declaration of Paris (16 April 1856) abolished privateering among its parties, could not be accepted in part and has no withdrawal clause; the United States refused to sign on Marcy's terms (reply of 28 July 1856) and offered to accede in 1861 (Seward). Hawaii acceded on 26 March 1855 to the neutral principles of the convention of 1854 between the United States and Russia (Wyllie's declaration), not to the Declaration. Russia's Volunteer Fleet (Dobroflot) was founded by subscription in April 1878. Frederick Townsend Ward (1831–1862): Salem; San Francisco 1850; Shanghai 1851; Walker's Sonora filibuster 1853, deserted; Mexico; to California on a mule and first mate of the *Westward Ho!* early in 1854; a French lieutenancy in the Crimea; the *Antelope* in China in 1857; New York early in 1859; Shanghai late in 1859 with his brother Harry. The Foreign Arms Corps of June 1860 for the taotai Wu Xu and the banker Yang Fang; the first Songjiang assault failed; about eighty Filipino "Manilamen" under Vincente Macanaya joined by mid-July; Songjiang fell with 62 killed and 100 wounded of about 250. Ward married Yang Fang's daughter Changmei in mid-March 1862, sent Harry to buy steamers in March 1862 (Harry sold them to the Union Army and never returned), was shot at Cixi on 21 September 1862, dictated his will to Lieutenant Bogle (Wu owed 110,000 taels, Yang 30,000; 50,000 to his wife; the rest to his brother and sister; Hope and Burlingame executors) and died at Ningpo on the 22nd. Burgevine struck Yang Fang on 4 January 1863 (the "Takee Incident"); Gordon took command in late March 1863. At Suzhou Gao Yongkuan's party killed Tan Shaoguang on 4 December 1863 and surrendered on the 5th; the leaders were killed on the 6th; the consuls condemned it on 16 December; Gordon withdrew to Kunshan for nearly two months and returned. Peking let foreigners run the Customs but would not put its army under them. Sources: Caleb Carr, *The Devil Soldier* (1992); Richard J. Smith, *Mercenaries and Mandarins* (1978), both supplied by the author. Other anchors: Pumpelly and Blake's survey of Ezo (1862); the Kaitakushi (from 1869; Kuroda deputy 1870, director 1874) and the tondenhei (1874); the French at Keelung (August 1884; the port held October 1884 – June 1885); Dye's instructors in Korea (April 1888); Crabb shot at Caborca (April 1857); Camilo Ynitia (1803–1856), headman of the Coast Miwok of Olompali, grantee of Rancho Olompali (1843), sold most of it in 1852; the Battle of Olompali (24 June 1846; de la Torre with about fifty men and twenty irregulars); the US bounty-land warrants (1847–55) and their speculators; the Swiss capitulations until the federal ban of 1859; North Borneo: the British company's constabulary (1882), indentured Chinese labour on the tobacco estates, Mat Salleh of the Lingkabo and Sugut (1894–1900), his village at Jambongan burned by the company on 29 August 1895, Gaya burned on 9 July 1897, Ranau (January 1898), Cowie's Palatan peace (April 1898), its breach at Tambunan and Mat Salleh's death there (31 January 1900; some sources give 1 February); the rising ends in 1903 or 1905, by different sources. *Divergence:* Ward a Pacifican from 1859 with Qing rank by letter; the company under the consul's licence leaves the service after Suzhou; letters of marque in 1878 and 1914; accession at The Hague in 1919; the Companies Act of 1888; the burning at Jambongan refused (August 1895); the Palatan peace kept. To verify: whether the Chinese wrecked the Keelung works in 1884; the British North Borneo constabulary's founding year.
- **Honolulu, 1889 (H4).** *History:* Robert W. Wilcox (1855–1903), schooled at Turin, led the rising of 30 July 1889 against the Bayonet government; seven of his men were killed, and a Hawaiian jury acquitted him in 1890. *Divergence:* the rising is against the Territorial Council, and the Regent puts it down with the Queen's Guard, refusing the naval party's marines.
- **The economy of 1873–78 (E-2a).** The model shows two shallow dips in Pacifica (1873–75 and 1877, output two or three per cent below its path) and steady deflation; the confederal treasury runs surpluses. "The slack years after 1873" follows the model, not the Union's depression of 1873–79.

## CR-075 — the aims of 1861 and why California and Hawaii agreed (integrated 19 November 2026)

- **The Pacific-republic current and Burch.** *History:* John Chilton Burch (born 1 February 1826 in Boone County, Missouri; died at San Francisco in 1885) studied law at Jefferson City, came to California in 1850, was clerk and district attorney of Trinity County, sat in the state assembly (1856) and senate, and was one of the two pro-Southern Democrats California sent to the House in 1859 (Biographical Directory of the United States Congress; Waite, *West of Slavery*, 119). His letter of 22 November 1860 proposed a Republic of the Pacific of California, Oregon, New Mexico, Washington and Utah (Ellison, *California and the Nation*, 181); Gwin and Latham drew a Rockies line (Ellison 180); Volney Howard argued in 1861 that a California free of the tariff could live on its own customs (Ellison 184–185). About as many Southern-born as Northern-born men lived in the state; the anti-war Democrats polled over a third in September 1861 (Ellison 183, 190–191). *Divergence:* Burch sits in the Confederal Assembly for Trinity from 1859; his letter invites the territories into the Republic, and his address of June 1861 is answered by the fourth object's narrow wording.
- **The land courts, Soscol and the eighteen treaties.** *History:* the Land Act of 1851 put every Mexican grant through litigation, and California was refused the land fund other new states received (Ellison 4–35); Vallejo lost Soscol at the Supreme Court in March 1862 (Rosenus, *General M. G. Vallejo*, 219). The eighteen treaties made with California's nations in 1851–52 were refused by the Senate in executive session on 8 July 1852 and kept under an injunction of secrecy lifted on 18 January 1905. *Divergence:* in Pacifica the register is the title (§5) and the treaties are the Republic's own; a returning Union would bring the Senate that refused them (CR-075 E3).
- **Seward's note and Baker.** *History:* Seward's circulars of spring 1861 called the South's men insurgents and its privateers pirates while the blockade implied a war (Jones, *Blue and Gray Diplomacy*, ch. 1–2). *Divergence:* Baker's note of May 1861 invokes the convention of 1848 against the insurgents; the joint answer turns on the article's words "in its wars". Text of the note and answer PROPOSED.
- **Beale and the Picos.** *History:* on 3 June 1861 Edward F. Beale, Lincoln's new surveyor general, wrote to Andrés Pico, the victor of San Pasqual and a Breckinridge Democrat, asking whether he stood for the Union at all hazards; Pico declared for the Union and offered his services, his brother Pío said the same, and the letters were printed (Thompson, *Edward F. Beale and the American West*, 130–131). Beale had come out with Gillespie's party, joined Kearny before San Pasqual (6 December 1846) and crept through the Californio lines to San Diego with Carson. *Divergence:* Beale writes in the last days of July 1861, after the preamble, asking whether Pico stands by the objects.
- **Hawaii's neutrality in 1861.** *History:* Kamehameha IV refused anything resembling recognition of the South; Wyllie wanted belligerent rights and believed the South would win (Kuykendall, *The Hawaiian Kingdom*, II, 65–66); the king made Victoria Kamāmalu Kuhina Nui in 1855 (Kuykendall II, 36); Wyllie sought a general guarantee of Hawaii's neutrality, 1857–63 (Kuykendall II, 55–57). Hawaii acceded in 1855 to the neutral principles of the Russo-American convention of 1854 (Kuykendall 63). *Divergence:* the king's refusal is his own; Victoria argues the case from the kingdom's weakness.
- **The guano islands, 1857–1867.** *History:* Kamehameha IV reaffirmed Nihoa in 1857 and Captain John Paty declared Laysan and Lisianski the kingdom's (May 1857). The schooner *Palestine*, a San Francisco coastal trader owned by San Francisco merchants (Robert G. Byxbee and Stoddard), chartered by William Parker and R. F. Ryan, sailed from San Francisco under Captain William R. Perriman and claimed Johnston under the Guano Act on 19 March 1858; she called at Honolulu in May 1858 on her way home, and the partners incorporated the Pacific Guano Company at San Francisco on 9 June 1858. In June the *Kalama* sailed from Honolulu under the king's commission and took down the American flag; the king proclaimed the annexation on 27 July 1858 and later that year withdrew because the American claim was prior (the author's research, 18 November 2026). The New York companies' islands (Baker, Jarvis, Howland) were worked from Honolulu with Hawaiian labour, G. P. Judd agent; the Navy's confirming voyage, the *St. Mary's* (1857), came from Panama. Captain N. C. Brooks of the Hawaiian-registered *Gambia* claimed Midway in July 1859; the *Lackawanna* took possession on 28 August 1867. G. P. Judd claimed Palmyra in 1859; the kingdom commissioned its taking on 26 February / 1 March 1862 in the names of the king and the Kuhina Nui, and it was taken on 15 April 1862. Sources: papahanaumokuakea.gov; Bishop Museum; en.wikipedia.org (Johnston Atoll; USS *St. Mary's*; Midway Atoll); doi.gov/oia/islands/palmyraatoll; imagesofoldhawaii.com; Kuykendall II, 213; Barrett's University of Hawaiʻi thesis (1960; scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu, handle 10125/35979). *Divergence:* the *Palestine* clears under the Republic's venture licence; the confederal rule of 1859 forbids licensed or cleared voyages to take land for a foreign power (Brooks and Judd lose their clearance); the sealed minute of 1861; the *Kalama* raises the flag on Kalama Island again in 1867. Story assumption: the *Kalama* is still in the inter-island trade in 1867.
- **Thomas Starr King.** *History:* born in New York City on 17 December 1824; self-educated for the ministry; minister of the First Unitarian Church of San Francisco from April 1860; stumped the state for Lincoln and was credited with keeping California in the Union; died 4 March 1864 (Lavender, *Nothing Seemed Impossible*, 154–155). *Divergence:* he preaches against the war, and is left free.
- **The flood of 1861–62.** *History:* the greatest flood in California's record; Sacramento was under water and the legislature moved to San Francisco on 22 January 1862 (Hunt, *The Army of the Pacific*, 32, 231). Agua Mansa, founded in the 1840s by families from Abiquiú with the guide Lorenzo Trujillo, was destroyed by the same flood (nps.gov, the Old Spanish Trail). *Divergence:* none in the flood; the legislature's move is the same.
- **The Union's Pacific strength in 1861.** *History:* the Pacific squadron was six wooden sloops, under 7,000 tons and about 100 guns (Hunt 301–303); Oregon and Washington had no fortifications, the money having gone to San Francisco Bay (Hunt 234). *Divergence:* the Bay was never American, so the money went to the Columbia bar and Port Townsend after 1859 (CR-059 H7).
- **Schoolbooks.** *History:* American schools have no single Civil War textbook; the books in common use for the early and middle grades (Hakim's *War, Terrible War*, Freedman's *Lincoln: A Photobiography*, Murphy's *The Boys' War*, the *What Was* series, Polacco's *Pink and Say*, Hunt's *Across Five Aprils*, Paulsen's *Soldier's Heart*, Keith's *Rifles for Watie* and others) frame the war by the stories they choose (the list the author supplied, 19 November 2026). *Divergence:* Pacifica's readings by year (CR-075 Part H).


## CR-076 — drink, opium and coffee (integrated 19 November 2026)

- **Turley and Taos Lightning.** *History:* Simeon Turley (born 1806 or 1809 in Kentucky; brothers Stephen and Jesse in the Boonslick, Jesse at Arrow Rock) ran a mill and distillery at Arroyo Hondo from about 1830; trappers traded furs there for his wheat whiskey, and traders carried it to the Colorado and Arkansas posts; he was killed in the Taos revolt of January 1847 (Lyckman, *Ayer y Hoy en Taos* 6, 1988; NRHP nomination). *Divergence:* Reuben's dealings at Turley's and the boy's morning on the Arkansas are story assumptions.
- **Lahaina, 1859.** *History:* after two days' drinking, Kamehameha IV shot Henry Neilson at Lahaina late on 11 September 1859; Neilson was moved to Honolulu, lived as an invalid in Alakea Street and died on 12 February 1862; Dr Robert McKibbin and M. B. White attended him (Honolulu Magazine, "Kamehameha IV and the Shooting of Henry Neilson"; Kuykendall II). *Divergence:* Lahaina's settled ship's surgeon (Noe's father) dresses the wound; Victoria's response.
- **Hawaii's drink and opium laws.** *History:* licensing from 1838; the ban on sales of spirits to Hawaiians upheld in *Rex v. Booth* (1863) and removed in 1882 (EB1911, "Hawaii"); the Act of 1860 licensed opium; the Act of 1874 restricted it to medicine; the Opium Act of 1886 licensed sales, the licence led to the bribery case of 1887 and the act was repealed in 1887 (*Rex v. Yat Sing*; *The King v. Young Tang*). *Divergence:* the racial clause ends in 1860 under Victoria and Lot; no opium licence is sold.
- **Salt Lake's city store.** *History:* Salt Lake City rented Brigham Young's distillery 1861–67; in 1863 the council said the liquor business brought in more than all city taxes; a grand jury found the city buying from Young and Wells at four dollars a gallon when better liquor cost a dollar and a quarter (Deseret News, 3 June 1876; Bagley, *Salt Lake Tribune*, 2001). *Divergence:* the confederal committee on the charters forces the supplier out in 1867.
- **Gothenburg and the science.** *History:* the Gothenburg company of 1865 (after Falun, 1850); Huss's *Alcoholismus chronicus* (1849; German 1852); Wernicke (1881); Korsakoff (1887–89); Sullivan on maternal inebriety (*Journal of Mental Science*, 1899); the UKTGPI's sectional mortality (temperance section's claims about 70% of expectation, general section about 96%; Moore, *Journal of the Institute of Actuaries*, 1904), a comparison of self-selected abstainers with other insured lives. *Divergence:* the Republic's label and stores follow these, and say no more than they show.
- **Thomas M. Logan.** *History:* Sacramento physician from 1850 (1808–1876; the cemetery marker gives 1806); drove Sacramento's permanent health board (1862) and California's State Board of Health (1870), permanent secretary with Henry Gibbons as president; proposed a national department of health (1871) (*California and Western Medicine*, January 1940, July 1945). *Divergence:* he compiles the 1852 table.
- **San Francisco's den ordinance.** *History:* the supervisors' ordinance of November 1875, the first anti-drug law in the United States, fined $50–$500 with up to six months in jail for keeping or visiting an opium den; it was aimed at Chinese-run dens and left patent medicines and morphine alone (Baumohl 1992; White 2014). *Divergence:* California's supreme court strikes it in 1876.
- **The Philippine opium farm and the Basi revolt.** *History:* the Spanish *estanco de anfión* (1843–44 to 1898) sold opium to the Chinese alone (Gamella and Martín, *Revista de Indias* 52, 1992; Bamero, *Social Science Diliman* 3, 2006); the Basi revolt of 1807 in Ilocos was against the wine monopoly. *Divergence:* the Philippine Council ends the farm and licenses everyone alike; no spirits monopoly.
- **Coffee.** *History:* San Francisco became the western centre of coffee importing and roasting after the Gold Rush (the Pioneer Steam Coffee and Spice Mills, 1850, later Folger's; Hills Bros., first to vacuum-pack roast coffee in 1900; MJB); the Philippines was reputed the fourth largest exporter by 1886 and the leaf rust of 1889 destroyed its trees; Kona had about 5,000 acres by 1930 and over a thousand farms in 1932, over 90% managed by Japanese families; Satori Kato showed soluble coffee at the Pan-American Exposition of 1901 and patented it in 1903 (US 735,777); the Union drank about 12–13 lb a head in the 1920s; the Union soldier drew about 36 lb a year in the Civil War. *Divergence:* the Philippine industry survives the rust; Kona is expanded; Kato works in San Francisco; consumption figures are MODEL (CR-076 L4).
- **Contrast only (not in the story):** the Trade and Intercourse Act's liquor ban in Indian country (1832–34); Whiteclay, Nebraska; *Oliphant v. Suquamish* (1978); the WCTU's scientific temperance instruction (1880s); national Prohibition (1920–33).
- **The duty schedule's anchors (R1).** *History:* the Union's whiskey tax rose from 20 cents a proof gallon (1862) to $2 (1865); under the $2 rate the taxed volume averaged about 13.3 million proof gallons a year against about 67 million at 50 cents (July 1868–1872), and spirits revenue rose from $14.3 million in 1868 to $33.7 million in 1869 (Wells, *Popular Science Monthly* 48); Britain's spirit duty stood at 10s. a proof gallon from 1860 to 1890 (EB1911, "Spirits"); price elasticities averaged −0.46 (beer), −0.69 (wine), −0.80 (spirits) (Wagenaar, Salois and Komro, *Addiction* 104, 2009). *Divergence:* Pacifica's share-of-price rule and its schedule (CR-076 R1-1).
- **Kansas and Davis Waite.** *History:* Kansas adopted constitutional prohibition in 1881; Davis H. Waite moved to Larned, Kansas, in 1876, sat in the Kansas legislature in 1879 and went to Leadville in 1879 and Aspen in 1881, becoming Colorado's Populist governor in 1893–95 (Denver Public Library biography; Colorado State Archives). *Divergence:* the Union's Colorado plains are attached to Kansas in the 1880s; Waite stays in Kansas and is its governor (CT4).
- **Agua Caliente.** *History:* the casino resort at Tijuana (1928) served Prohibition-era Hollywood. *Divergence:* Tijuana is Pacifican; the resort rises at Guaymas under Mexico's law.

## CR-077 — the Calculation Office to 1932 (integrated 20 November 2026)

- **Peirce and switching.** *History:* Peirce described electrical switching circuits for logic in a letter to Allan Marquand, 30 December 1886 (Peirce, *Writings* 5, Indiana UP, 1993, pp. 421–423); he found in 1880–1881, without publishing it, that one operation (neither–nor) suffices; "Logical Machines", *American Journal of Psychology* 1 (1887). He died at Milford, Pennsylvania, on 19 April 1914 ([History of Information](https://historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=4090); [Switching circuit theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switching_circuit_theory)). *Divergence:* the algebra is the Office's first course from 1886; he dies at Carquinez.
- **Cards and exchanges.** *History:* Strowger's automatic exchange patent (1891); Hollerith's Tabulating Machine Company (Washington, 1896), CTR (1911), IBM (1924); James Powers built the Census Bureau's machines for 1910 and founded his company in 1911; IBM's card-programmed calculator (1949); the Genealogical Society of Utah (1894). *Divergence:* card-programmed relay calculators by 1893; Hollerith's company at San Francisco; the Union's census builds its own machines rather than pay a Pacifican royalty.
- **Michelson's analyser.** *History:* 20-element analyser 1897, 80-element 1898 (Michelson and Stratton, *Am. J. Sci.* 1898; [Science Museum Group](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co60126)).
- **Counters and the pair.** *History:* Wynn-Williams's thyratron counters at the Cavendish (1931) and scale-of-two counter (*Proc. Roy. Soc. A* 136, 1932) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._E._Wynn-Williams)); Eccles and Jordan's trigger relay (1918); Wehnelt's oxide cathode (1904). *Divergence:* the soft valve's latching is the counter's switch from about 1900; the hard pair is published in 1910.
- **Broadcasting.** *History:* Charles Herrold's San Jose station transmitted music and news regularly from 1909 ([California Office of Historic Preservation](https://ohp.parks.ca.gov/ListedResources/Detail/952)). Listeners used crystal sets.
- **Fuller and Hebern.** *History:* Leonard Fuller (born Portland, 21 August 1890), chief engineer of the Federal Telegraph Company from 1913, built arc transmitters for the Army and Navy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_F._Fuller)); Edward Hebern (born Streator, Illinois, 1869; Madera; Oakland) drew a rotor cipher in 1917, patented in 1918, sold five-rotor machines for trial in 1925; Friedman broke them and Hebern never knew why his machine was not adopted ([Computer History Museum](https://computerhistory.org/blog/before-enigma-breaking-the-hebern-rotor-machine/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Hebern)). Punched cards were used against additive codes in the 1930s–40s.

## CR-083 — Caledonia and the Northern Association: British Columbia, the Yukon and the Pacifica–Canada settlement (integrated 24 November 2026)

Real-world facts behind CR-083, the divergences, and what is still unsourced. Decision record: `CR-083.md`.

- **The Fraser rush and the colony.** *History:* about 30,000 miners went up the Fraser in 1858, most from San Francisco, and Britain made the mainland a colony that year (The Canadian Encyclopedia on the Fraser River gold rush). *Divergence:* the miners are Pacificans.
- **Confederation, 1870–71.** *History:* British Columbia entered Confederation on 20 July 1871 on the promise of a railway; the scandal of 1873 over Sir Hugh Allan's syndicate followed (The Canadian Encyclopedia, "British Columbia and Confederation"; BCcampus, *Canadian History: Post-Confederation*, ch. 2.3). *Divergence:* the colony stays out. Pacifica offers a railway south to Puget Sound, a loan and free entry for coal, timber and fish, and asks for no flag; Britain keeps the colony and Esquimalt. No railway is promised by Ottawa, so the scandal of 1873 does not occur in this form. The earlier canon motive ("a strong Canadian buffer") is replaced: Nanaimo's coal, the Fraser's trade, and no second transcontinental power with a Pacific coast (PROPOSED).
- **The Yukon at the fork.** *History:* the Stickeen Territories were created by Order in Council of 19 July 1862 and ran north to the 62nd parallel under the colony's governor until the Act of 28 July 1863 returned the part north of 60° to the North-Western Territory; the North-Western Territory was transferred to Canada on 15 July 1870 (Legislative Assembly of British Columbia, "1862 Stikine Territories"; Wikipedia, "Stickeen Territories" and "North-Western Territory"). In 1869 Captain Charles Raymond fixed the Hudson's Bay Company's post at Fort Yukon as lying in Alaska, west of the 141st meridian (https://www.gi.alaska.edu/alaska-science-forum/early-ascent-yukon-river). *Divergence:* London reserves the later Yukon Territory, with the head of the Liard, from the transfer of 1870 and joins it to the colony; Canada's boundary is the continental divide and, north of 54°, the 120th meridian; the river grant of 1871 is made to Britain for the colony.
- **The railways.** *History:* the Canadian Pacific was completed to Burrard Inlet in 1885–87 as a federal undertaking. The Pacific Great Eastern was chartered in 1912 under provincial bond guarantees and taken over by the province in 1918 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BC_Rail). *Divergence:* the colony builds its own section from the divide to Burrard Inlet on gold-dollar bonds sold in San Francisco and grants the Canadian Pacific running rights; the Pacific Great Eastern is financed in San Francisco gold dollars beside London; the railway to Prince Rupert is the dominion's own. The debt's amounts are MODEL and unsized.

## CR-084 — the British retrenchment and the Malay barrier (integrated 24 November 2026)

The decision record is `CR-084.md` (revision 15), with the shared calendar `CR-083-086-calendar.md` and the workbook note `finance/CR-083-086-workbook.md` (annuity figures, MODEL). - **Singapore.** *History:* on 13 November 1929 Britain's Labour government told the Commons that work on the naval base at Sembawang "shall be slowed down as much as possible" and that "all work that can be suspended shall be suspended"; a floating dock had already been provided, and Hong Kong, the Federated Malay States and New Zealand had contributed £2,113,000 to that date (Hansard, https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1929/nov/13/singapore-base and https://api.parliament.uk/historic-hansard/commons/1930/jul/09/singapore-base ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singapore_Naval_Base ). Work was resumed later in the 1930s and the base opened in 1938 (general knowledge, to verify; not in the decision record's sources). *Divergence:* the halt is made permanent by treaty in 1932, with a peacetime cap on British capital ships east of Ceylon; New Zealand's share of the contributions is not in the source.
- **The National Government.** *History:* Britain left gold on 21 September 1931 and voted on 27 October; from 5 November 1931 the ministers included Ramsay MacDonald (Prime Minister), Stanley Baldwin (Lord President), Neville Chamberlain (Exchequer, succeeding Philip Snowden), Sir John Simon (Foreign Office), Sir Philip Cunliffe-Lister (Colonies), J. H. Sultan Ahmad Tajuddin was born on 22 August 1912 or 1913, acceded on 11 September 1924 under the regency of the Pengiran Bendahara and the Pengiran Pemancha, which ended on 19 September 1931, sailed for England in July 1932 and returned in August 1933, and died on 4 June 1950 ( https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmad_Tajuddin ). ## CR-085 — the Concord renewed without term, and the partners' dossiers (integrated 24 November 2026)

Web pages listed with a URL were verified on 23 or 24 November 2026. Items marked "from memory" are gathered again in the list at the end.

- **May 1932 in Tokyo.** *History:* naval officers and army cadets shot Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi (born 1855) on 15 May 1932 (the May 15 incident). His cabinet had held office since 13 December 1931; Takahashi Korekiyo, Finance Minister from that day, acted as prime minister until 26 May, when Admiral Saitō Makoto (born 1858) formed a cabinet in which Takahashi kept Finance (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inukai_cabinet ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takahashi_Korekiyo). Araki Sadao (born 1877) was army minister in both cabinets, and the genrō Saionji came up to Tokyo to recommend the next premier (from memory). Mori Kaku (Mori Tsutomu, 28 December 1882 to 11 December 1932) was Chief Secretary of the Inukai cabinet (https://www.ndl.go.jp/portrait/e/datas/568). Hiranuma Kiichirō (born 1867) was vice-president of the Privy Council (from memory). The London Naval Treaty, signed in April 1930, was ratified by Japan only in October, the Privy Council's handling of it being the loudest part of the delay (from memory). *Divergence:* the coup is aimed at the Concord's first renewal; Takahashi's caretaker cabinet resolves to propose a renewal without term and Saitō's confirms it on 26 May; the Privy Council advises ratification within a month of Hayama, with Hiranuma's dissent entered; Mori Kaku speaks against it in the Diet in June. Japan has had no navy apart from the Concord's since 1927.
- **The yen.** *History:* Japan re-imposed its gold embargo on its own in December 1931, under Takahashi (13 December, on the day recalled), and the yen then fell in disorder, by about 60 per cent against the dollar on the figure recalled (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takahashi_Korekiyo ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wakatsuki_Reijir%C5%8D ; the percentage and the day are doubtful and not checked in primary tables). *Divergence:* Japan stays on gold through the winter of 1931–32 under common finance, and in late July 1932 the yen's parity is reset in an orderly way, by 20 to 25 per cent inside a consultation band of 5 per cent (PROPOSED), by an agreement signed at Thalassia.
- **Hokkaido's rice.** *History:* Hokkaido's development agency opposed rice on its American advisers' counsel (Horace Capron's among them) until it opened a test station in 1893, although the pioneer Nakayama Kyūzō had grown *Akage* rice near Kitahiroshima from 1873; local breeding in Hokkaido began only in 1915, and the *Bōzu* and *Hashiri-bōzu* lines and *Fukoku* followed (https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jsbbs/69/3/69_19062/_html/-char/en ; https://d-arch.ide.go.jp/je_archive/society/wp_je_unu22.html). Satō Shōsuke (1856–1939), an agricultural economist, was acting head of Sapporo Agricultural College from 1891 and the first president of Hokkaido Imperial University, 1918–1930 (https://www.hokudai.ac.jp/bunsyo/images/Exhibition/Leaflet_S.Sato.pdf ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shosuke_Sato); Zusho Hirotake was the college's first president (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapporo_Agricultural_College). *Divergence:* the Realm's advisers, who at first counsel against rice as Capron did, are turned round by the Office's table of 1890; a breeding exchange with winter nurseries in Hawaiʻi begins in 1893, cross-bred cultivars reach the fields from 1901–03, and Hokkaido's paddy reaches about 100,000–130,000 chō by 1910 (MODEL), a generation early, at a cost in Ainu land.
- **Japan's rice supply.** *History:* Japan's crop was about 50.1 million koku in 1910, 57.5 million in 1920 and 60.3 million in 1932 (the baselines CR-082 uses); in 1929–33 output ran at about 60.5 million koku with about 10.7 million imported (https://ch.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ocw-files/lecture_files/eco_04/7/notes/en/CEHJ-7.pdf). The rice riots of July to September 1918 brought down the Terauchi cabinet (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rice_riots_of_1918; the cabinet's name from memory), and after 1920 Japan drove up colonial Korea's and Formosa's rice exports to the home islands (https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/repo/huscap/all/10987/40_p175-198.pdf), which depressed Japanese farm prices: the stagnation Hayami and Ruttan identified. *Divergence:* the home crop runs 5 per cent above history's in 1910, 8 per cent in 1920 and 13 per cent in 1932 (about 68.1 million koku against a need of 68.4 million for 62.2 million residents; MODEL, CR-082's percentages); there is no physical shortage in 1918; Korea is never annexed, so there is no colonial rice programme, and Korea's and Formosa's rice reach Japan by contract in modest amounts.
- **Japan's electrochemical makers.** *History:* Noguchi Shitagau (26 July 1873 to 15 January 1944) founded Nippon Chisso (Nichitsu) in 1908 and bought the Casale ammonia process in 1921 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shitagau_Noguchi). Mori Nobuteru (21 October 1884 to 1 March 1941) founded Showa Fertiliser in October 1928; it made ammonium sulphate by 1931, and its predecessor firms poured Japan's first domestic aluminium at Ōmachi in January 1934 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobuteru_Mori ; https://www.ndl.go.jp/portrait/e/datas/580 ; https://the-shashi.com/en/tse/4004/). Mitsubishi at Nagoya and Nakajima at Ōta were the centres of Japan's airframe industry (from memory). *Divergence:* Japan fixes its nitrogen on Pacifican turbines and at Pacifica's open royalty; primary aluminium smelting in Japan is barred until the Concord's first review in mid-1937, with joint ventures outside Japan in 1932–37; a licensed all-metal aircraft line runs at Nagoya and Ōta from 1928.
- **The atom in Japan.** *History:* Nishina Yoshio (6 December 1890 to 10 January 1951) (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshio_Nishina); the Tokyo Electric Light Company (Tokyo Dentō) was Tokyo's leading utility in the 1920s (from memory). There was no power reactor anywhere before the 1950s. *Divergence:* the Tokyo station of about 80 MW is paid for by Tokyo Dentō and a utility syndicate in yen and by a twenty-year fuel lease from the Works; a second station for Osaka and Kobe is announced on 29 April 1932.

## A. The change

**A1. The line (APPROVED; author, 24 November 2026, 07:19 and 08:22; its first bullet is PROPOSED).**
Canon says of the Western line that "the detailed boundary follows the mountain front, divide crests" (`L629 ("divide crests")`) and leaves its surveys PROPOSED. In the far north this CR fixes it.
- From Marias Pass north the line follows the Continental Divide, which there is the crest of the Lewis Range (*Hist.*). This is PROPOSED (`T-3`): canon gives "the mountain front" in Montana and does not say where the line passes from the front to the divide, which is OPEN. The map agrees with it from Marias Pass to about 48°27′ N. For the years before 1887, "the Realm" in this CR means the Confederation.
- **What `CR-071`'s map drew, before this CR.** `CR-071`'s Missoula County was our timeline's county of 1887 whole, and its eastern edge is a meridian at about 113°28′ W for its whole length. South of about 48°27′ N the ground between that meridian and the divide is another of the Realm's counties, which ends in a point where the divide crosses the meridian; up to there the Realm's outer line is the divide. North of that point the old map ran Missoula on along the meridian to the 49th parallel. As that map drew it, the Realm held everything between the divide and that meridian: the trough, and also the Belly valley, Many Glacier, Chief Mountain and the head of the St Mary, about 510 square miles, with about 27 miles of border with Alberta (measured from the atlas; MODEL). The meridian is a legislature's line of 1867 and follows nothing on the ground.
- At the north end of Flattop Mountain the divide leaves the Lewis Range and crosses west to the Livingston Range, which it follows into Canada (*Hist.*). The line does not go with it.
- The line stays on the crest of the Lewis Range, past the head of the Mokowanis, a branch of the Belly, and Mount Cleveland, the range's highest summit, and meets the 49th parallel on the ridge east of Upper Waterton Lake (*Hist.* for the crest and the summit; the exact point is to be read from a topographic map).
- The Realm keeps the trough between the two ranges north of Flattop, about 90 square miles (MODEL): the upper Waterton valley and the head of Upper Waterton Lake. Geologists draw the seam between the ranges through that lake and valley (*Hist.*).
- On the 49th parallel the Realm runs from the tripoint with British Columbia and Alberta, on the divide, at 114°04′ W, east to the Lewis crest: about ten miles, across the lake at Boundary Bay. That stretch is the Realm's only border with Alberta, and, once `CR-083` takes British Columbia out, with Canada.
- East of the Lewis crest everything is the Union's: the St Mary and Belly valleys, Many Glacier, Chief Mountain and the plains. Against `CR-071`'s old map, that is about 420 square miles passing from the Realm to the Union, and with it the easy road into Alberta by the Belly valley and under Chief Mountain (`T-2`).

**A2. Why the line runs there (APPROVED as rewritten; author, 24 November 2026, 10:45; the particulars are PROPOSED).**
- **The words.** The peace of 1865 describes this part of the line as "the crest of the main range of the Rocky Mountains" (the phrase is APPROVED; author, 24 November 2026, 13:08; canon had no words for this stretch and says of Montana only "the mountain front", `L632`; see `T-3`). The Union's own treaty of 1855 with the Blackfeet runs that nation's limit "to the main range of the Rocky Mountains, and thence southerly along said range" (*Hist.*, article 4), and the Union's commissioners want the two descriptions to agree so that no strip is left between them. That argues for one line and not for the eastern one. The Realm's surveyor supplies the rest from the peace itself: "In the Rockies the line follows what each side can hold, not the water" (`L632 ("not the water")`), and a peace that puts holding above the water (`L632`) need not follow the watershed over the last ten miles, where the divide leaves the higher range.
- **The meridian.** The peace said "crest" from the start. The meridian is not the peace's line. It is a county line. In our history Missoula County dates from 1860, and the meridian comes from an exchange of ground with Deer Lodge County on 20 November 1867 (*Hist.*, the atlas's chronology); here western Montana's own bodies draw it in the same years (which body, on which day, is OPEN), before any survey and, north of about 48°27′ N, further east than the treaty allowed, across the Belly country on the Blackfeet's side of the mountains. South of that point the meridian stays the county's edge and is not in question. The Union protests it from the first, and when the joint survey arrives the Confederation withdraws it as a matter of course. Nothing is given for it, because it was never the Realm's by treaty.
- **The survey.** The joint survey reaches the north end in 1874, the season in which the survey of the 49th parallel reaches the old monument on the divide, on 18 August (*Hist.*; another account gives 8 August for the last marker, at Waterton Lake). The British commissioners, coming west along the parallel, do not know until the crests are settled whose surveyors they will meet over the last ten miles. North of Flattop the joint survey finds two crests and one phrase. The only contest is between them. The Realm's surveyor argues for the eastern crest, which is the higher and carries the highest summits of the two, and which is the range the Union's own treaty of 1855 means. The Union's argues for the western, where the waters part. The commission finds for the eastern. The trough is uninhabited by settlers, has no pass a wagon can use and no mineral anyone has found, and the finding keeps the Blackfeet line and the Western line on the same words.
- **What nobody intends.** Neither side is thinking of Canada. That the Realm now touches the North-West Territories, later Alberta, on a mountain lake is an accident of the phrase, noticed in Ottawa before it is noticed in Washington.
- **The loser.** The Union's surveyor. He files a protest that the divide was the natural line, and he loses about 90 square miles of trough to a commission that wants to go home. The Union as a whole loses little, and nobody makes a cause of it afterwards.

**A3. The Blackfeet are not asked, and the valley is not settled (PROPOSED).**

The crest line forecloses nothing: the Piegan remain across the line, on the Union's side, as `CR-060` N5 leaves them, and a later compact could still be made.

Nobody tells the Piegan either thing in 1874.

**A4. Roads: surveys only (APPROVED as to "surveys only"; author, 24 November 2026, 07:19; the routes and dates are PROPOSED).**

The corner is British Columbia's, and with `CR-083` Caledonia's from 1932, so the road needs more than one government. It is advocated and not built, as in history.

## Checked after integration (24 November 2026, 20:30)

- **Woosung as the cables' landing.** The Great Northern's sea cable from Hong Kong ended at Woosung, with a line up the river to the company's Shanghai office (Foreign Relations of the United States, 1874, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1874/d141; 1878, https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1878/d85). The cable companies' agreement with China of 1889 names Woosung, Sharp Peak, Kulangsu and Fusan as the places where their cables were already landed (CO 129/248, https://hkintexts.histsyn.com/series/histsyn-co129-engine/items/CO129-248%20-%20Public%20Offices%20&%20Others%20-%201890?p=348). The Manila cable of 1906 at Woosung is the story's.
- **Athabasca Landing as the northern railhead.** The Canadian Northern's last rails reached Athabasca on 14 May 1912 and the first train arrived on 25 May (Library and Archives Canada, https://epe.lac-bac.gc.ca/100/205/301/ic/cdc/athabasca/html/commboom/index.htm). From the 1880s the Hudson's Bay Company's steamers ran from the Landing down the Athabasca, Slave and Mackenzie, about 3,000 km to the delta (https://www.nwttimeline.ca/?p=3210). The railway reached Fort McMurray, beyond the Grand Rapids, only in 1917–21 (https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/article/athabasca-landing-trail). Great Bear's ore on this route in the 1910s is the story's.
