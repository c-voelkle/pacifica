## Cadence and limits

One major commitment per phase/decade is the planning rule, with a few short operations in the 1870s explicitly admitted by the source. Never two fronts at once; no new military operation after the 1878 strike before 1880; no foreign military adventure within a year of the 1887 succession. Cheap treaties, nodes, talent and technical programmes fill the intervals. Every acquisition targets payback within ten years.

No populated mainland annexations outside North America: Sonora, Korea, Chinese provinces and Java remain outside territorial acquisition. No port is taken from China; Japan is an equal ally without a sovereign Pacifican base. Formosa's revenue administration preserves Chinese sovereignty but creates acknowledged tension over a prize Japan wanted. No Pacifican land war against Russia, no British war before 1900, and no race with the Royal Navy. Local western Pacific superiority is the standard.

## Phase I — 1865–1872: win the peace

The decade's single military commitment is Grant's war of 1870–71. Everything else is cheap: talent, treaties and nodes picked up while the Union, France and Spain are distracted.
### 1865 · Settle the interior — RETAINED

- Anchor: the 1865 armistice line; the Mobile naval station and Galveston commercial rights follow in 1871–72; the Western treaty and the Alaska purchase are ratified in 1867.
- Move: make integration the first priority. A Pacifican homestead law (PROPOSED) and religious freedom for the Mormons (APPROVED) bind the new territories.
- Gain: a continental base from the Pacific to the divide, with Comstock silver, Montana gold and Utah coal.
- Risk: the $40m vendor credit runs to 1872, which keeps the early budget tight.
### 1863–65 · Navajo treaty and Apache peace — APPROVED / PROPOSED

- Anchor: the Navajo Long Walk of 1864 and internment at Bosque Redondo until 1868; the Apache wars after the Bascom affair (Hist.).
- Move (APPROVED, 18 October): avoid the Long Walk through a homeland treaty in 1863–1864 instead of removal and return. Make peace with the Apache bands on the terms Howard and Cochise later agreed: homeland reservations, honest agents, paid Apache scouts, prosecution of settler massacres. Pair it with a convention ending Mexico's scalp bounties.
- Gain: the interior garrison shrinks and the future Sunset route runs through quiet country.
- Risk: settler backlash and continued raiding into Sonora.
### 1865–67 · Harvest the talent of two armies — PROPOSED

- Anchor: thousands of Confederates emigrated to Brazil, some founded colonies in Mexico, and about fifty American officers served the Khedive of Egypt from 1869 (Hist.).
- Move: offer land, rank and contracts to naval officers, ironclad builders, railway engineers and West Point engineers from both sides. Keep the Sierra railway's Chinese crews employed on interior lines.
- Gain: a decade's head start in shipbuilding, ordnance and railway construction at bargain prices.
- Risk: divided loyalties, and Union anger at Pacifica sheltering former enemies.
### 1866–67 · Back Juárez, buy Baja — PROPOSED

- Anchor: France withdraws from Mexico in 1866–67; Maximilian is shot in June 1867 (Hist.).
- Move: supply Juárez with arms and credit through Sonora from 1865. In return: the purchase of Baja California, a railway concession to Guaymas with a free port, and the Apache convention.
- Gain: a grateful southern neighbour, the Gulf of California, Magdalena Bay and a safe San Diego, without annexing populous Sonora.
- Risk: Mexican nationalism over the sale; price to the audit.
### 1867 · Midway — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the US Navy claimed Midway in August 1867 (Hist.).
- Move: claim it in the same year as the Alaska purchase.
- Gain: a coaling and later cable station between Hawaii and Japan.
### 1868–72 · Lock in Japan — RETAINED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: the Meiji Restoration of 1868, the Boshin War to 1869, Japan's first foreign loan in 1870, and the Iwakura Mission's first landfall at San Francisco in January 1872 (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): the technical mission, the Aizu refugees and the Wakamatsu colony, Pacifica's loan to Japan, and the 1870–71 treaty with full tariff autonomy, Yokosuka dockyard rights and a Hakodate coaling lease.
- Move (PROPOSED): with France beaten in 1870–71, Pacifica replaces the French army mission and pre-empts Britain's 1873 naval mission. Japanese officers train in Pacifica and Japanese yards build to Pacifican designs and calibres.
- Gain: Japan's state, army and navy grow up on Pacifican standards, which makes later cooperation natural and later rivalry costly for Japan.
- Risk: Japan learns too well; the Kuroda faction's later resentment starts here.
### 1868–72 · Train China's future elite — RETAINED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: the Burlingame mission of 1868; Yung Wing's Chinese Educational Mission sent 120 boys to Connecticut in 1872–75 and was recalled in 1881, partly because the US refused them West Point and Annapolis (Hist.). The railway engineer Zhan Tianyou and the later premier Tang Shaoyi were among them.
- Move (RETAINED): Pacifica's treaty with the 1868 family article and no missionaries.
- Move (PROPOSED): host the Educational Mission in San Francisco and open the naval academy to it.
- Gain: China's first modern engineers, officers and diplomats are Pacifican-trained.
- Risk: Qing conservatives recall the mission anyway; Pacifica must keep it useful to Li Hongzhang.
### 1867–69 · Hold the transpacific route — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Pacific Mail opens San Francisco–Yokohama–Hong Kong steam service in 1867; the Suez Canal opens in November 1869 and shortens Europe's route to Asia (Hist.).
- Move: subsidize a national mail line with coal at Honolulu, Midway and Hakodate.
- Gain: passengers, mail and silks keep moving through San Francisco despite Suez.
### 1870–71 · Grant's war — RETAINED

- Anchor: the second, shorter North–South war under Grant, followed by a guaranteed armed peace.
- Discipline: while it lasts, the Pacific stays quiet. No Asian commitment overlaps it.
### 1872 · The Greeley affair — APPROVED

- Anchor: Horace Greeley, candidate of the Liberal Republicans and Democrats, lost in 1872 and died on 29 November, before the electoral college met (Hist.).
- Canon: Greeley wins the 1872 election (RETAINED).
- Effect: the president-elect dies three weeks later. The Union faces a disputed succession between his running mate and Congress, right after the failure of Grant's war. The party of reconciliation loses its leader, and the Union enters the depression of 1873 without a settled government.
- Pacifica's move: strict silence and correct diplomacy. The Union's confusion speaks for itself.
## Phase II — 1873–1879: the Spanish window and the Long Depression

The Philippines are the decade's one big move. The depression that starts in 1873 is the second opportunity: Pacifica holds gold while capital, labour and assets elsewhere become cheap.
### 1873 · Gold, the trade dollar and the Big Bonanza — RETAINED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: Germany's move to gold and the US Coinage Act of 1873 push silver down; the Comstock Big Bonanza is struck the same year; the Panic of 1873 opens the Long Depression (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): Pacifica adopts gold with a silver trade dollar.
- Move (PROPOSED): mint the trade dollar to compete with the Mexican peso in Chinese ports; buy distressed Union railway and mining securities; keep immigration recruiting and irrigation works running while other countries cut.
- Gain: Asia stays on silver, so Pacifica's gold buys more there every year. Settlers and engineers come when no one else is hiring.
- Risk: falling silver hurts Comstock owners; tight credit limits new borrowing.
### 1872–76 · The Philippines — RETAINED, with PROPOSED refinements

- Anchor: the Cavite mutiny and the execution of the three Gomburza priests in 1872, with liberals deported to Guam; Spain's First Republic, cantonal revolt, Carlist war and Cuban war (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): a schooner seized off Jolo in 1873, a one-day action at Manila Bay during the cantonal revolt, purchase in 1876, Crown dependency with its own council, courts and organic act (CR-021).
- Move (PROPOSED):
  - no ground operations before the purchase; a Pacifican loan to Madrid in 1874–75 for the Carlist war, secured on the islands;
  - the sale includes the Carolines and Palau (APPROVED, CR-015 B3); Germany contests the Carolines in 1885;
  - the deported reformers on Guam are freed when the Marianas change hands;
  - a Philippine Crown dependency with council/courts and a provincial elected council, its form resolved by CR-021 G/I6; friar estates bought out and a treaty with the Moro sultanates (PROPOSED).
- Gain: Manila Bay and Guam, Manila hemp, a labour pool, and a Catholic, Spanish-speaking link to New Mexico.
- Risk: if the assembly is hollow, a larger reckoning can still come in the 1890s. No pre-purchase ground operations; the smaller post-purchase insurgency ebbs in the 1880s under the proposed plan.
### 1873–77 · The Union's depression and the great strike — APPROVED

- Anchor: Jay Cooke & Co. fails in September 1873 over Northern Pacific bonds and the Long Depression begins. The disputed election of 1876 is settled only by a bargain over the South, and in 1877 troops fire on railway strikers, killing around a hundred (Hist.).
- In this timeline: the Union's railways have no West to build into, so the bubble bursts harder. The 1876 dispute has no South to trade, and the 1877 strike runs straight into it, close to insurrection.
- Pacifica's move: keep recruiting while the Union lays off. Union engineers and skilled workers move west across the divide, and the Pacifican press prints its first wage comparisons.
- Gain: talent, and the first image of Pacifica as the place where work pays.
### 1874 · Hawaiian crisis — RETAINED

As in canon. It is the year's only other commitment, and it is short.
### 1874–79 · Japan's Formosa expedition and the Ryukyu settlement — RETAINED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: Japan's 1874 expedition to Formosa, for which the US withdrew chartered ships under British pressure; the 1879 Ryukyu dispute, which Grant tried to mediate on his world tour (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): Pacifican transport for Japan and brokerage over Ryukyu.
- Move (PROPOSED): Pacifica formally mediates in 1879. Japan keeps Ryukyu; China receives trade guarantees backed by Pacifica.
- Gain: both capitals accept Pacifica as the fair broker of East Asia.
### 1876 · The fuel-oil programme — APPROVED

- Anchor: the first commercial well at Pico Canyon in 1876. California crude is heavy and poor for lamp oil, then the main product. Caspian steamers at Baku burned refinery residue by the late 1870s; the US Navy tested petroleum in 1867 and rejected it (Hist.).
- Move: a state geological survey and burner trials from 1876. Oil locomotives on the desert stretch of the Sunset route and oil-fired torpedo boats in the 1880s. The Los Angeles and Kern River fields are found around 1885–88 instead of 1892 and 1899.
- Gain: by the early 1890s Pacifica's light forces run on Pacifican fuel, while Britain's Pacific squadron still depends on Nanaimo coal.
- Risk: volume. A fleet of the 1895 size needs about a million barrels a year, which California historically reached only in the mid-1890s. Everything rests on the early finds.
### 1876–80 · Korea's first Western treaty — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Japan forces the Ganghwa Treaty on Korea in 1876; the US failed with gunboats in 1871 and signed only in 1882, through Li Hongzhang (Hist.).
- Move: Pacifica signs through Chinese mediation around 1878–80, with no gunboats.
- Gain: first Western treaty partner, which prepares the 1882–87 advisers and Korean neutrality of canon.
### 1876–79 · Famine relief for North China — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the North China famine of 1876–79 killed roughly 9–13 million people (Hist.). California was then one of the world's great wheat exporters.
- Move: shipments of California wheat through Tianjin, with Chinese Pacifican relief committees working alongside Li Hongzhang's officials.
- Gain: goodwill at court and among the people, which opens the door to the Inspectorate in 1885.
- Risk: inland transport failed many historical relief efforts; cost a few million dollars.
### 1877 · The Satsuma Rebellion — RETAINED

Pacifica's role as set in CR-015.
### 1878 · Russia checked at sea — RETAINED

- Anchor: the Russo-Turkish War; the British fleet passes the Dardanelles in February 1878; Russia buys cruisers in the US to raid British trade (Hist.).
- Move: the concentrated February 1878 action at Vladivostok, where Marcus Vance I dies; no Siberian land army.
- Gain (PROPOSED): Russia's Pacific ambitions slip by a decade, and Britain owes Pacifica recognition of the Philippine purchase.
- Risk: 1873–78 now holds four operations. Nothing else military should be added before 1880.
### 1879 · War of the Pacific — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Chile defeats Peru and Bolivia in 1879–84; US mediation fails (Hist.).
- Move: strict neutrality, then mediation and credit to the victor.
- Gain: a long-term contract for Chilean nitrate, the raw material of gunpowder, and Chile as Pacifica's partner on the South American coast.
## Phase III — 1880–1889: the network decade

Pacifica takes no populated territory in the 1880s. It builds the things others will have to route through: the Chinese customs, coal, a mid-Pacific fortress, a steel navy and the rights to the canal. The decade also has to absorb the managed 1887 succession and abolition in the Confederacy.
### 1880–85 · Stay open when others close — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the US restricts Chinese immigration in 1880 and excludes it in 1882; the Australian colonies restrict it through the 1880s; Canada imposes a head tax in 1885 (Hist.).
- Move: Pacifica keeps the family article and offers naturalization. A Chinese merchants' bank in San Francisco handles remittances from the whole diaspora.
- Gain: overseas Chinese capital and merchant networks, plus the bilingual staff the Inspectorate will need.
- Risk: nativist politics. Rock Springs (1885) and Hells Canyon (1887) both happen and are promptly prosecuted (APPROVED, CR-015 I3).
### 1880s · Recruit from Europe's crises — RETAINED

The Irish Land War, the German emigration peak, Russian Jewish emigration after 1881 and Italian emigration, as already listed in canon.
### 1882–87 · Korea advisers — RETAINED

Korea stays neutral under Chinese and Pacifican guidance; the advisers stand aside from the 1884 Gapsin coup.
### 1883 · Sunset route and the first steel warships — RETAINED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: through service on the Sunset route in 1883 (RETAINED). San Francisco's Union Iron Works began building steel cruisers in the mid-1880s (Hist.).
- Move (PROPOSED): the first all-steel programme comes from Pacifican yards and Pacifican steel, not from British builders.
- Gain: Pacifica can build and repair its own fleet in wartime.
### 1879–90 · The electrical programme — PROPOSED

- Anchor: San Francisco opens the first central power station in the US in 1879. Tesla reaches New York in 1884, leaves Edison in 1885 and is digging ditches by 1886–87; Westinghouse buys his AC patents in 1888 (Hist.).
- Move: a state technical institute teaching electrical engineering from about 1880. CR-020 settles Tesla’s San Francisco arrival in 1884, the electrical institute in 1886, and Steinmetz and Baekeland in 1889. One public water code covers irrigation and power. AC at a single national frequency is fixed around 1888–90, with generators from the 1885 turbine programme.
- Gain: the AC system becomes Pacifican. Sierra hydropower reaches the coast cities around 1890, five to ten years early, and coal is freed for the fleet and the railways.
- Risk: Germany and the Union are strong in the field and patents leak. A clear lead over Britain is realistic; against Siemens and GE, parity.
### 1884–85 · Keelung and the Inspectorate — RETAINED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: France attacks Keelung in August 1884 and destroys the Fujian fleet at Fuzhou (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): Pacifica protects Keelung; in 1885 the Inspectorate passes to Edmund Fong Kai-ming (方啟明), born in San Francisco in 1859 to a Taishan merchant family, a customs officer since 1878, with a small agency fee and the Keelung coal and railway concession.
- Move (PROPOSED): China rebuilds the Fujian fleet in Pacifican yards on Pacifican credit. Historically its battleships came from Stettin.
- Gain: sight of all of China's foreign trade, Keelung coal, and shipyard orders that tie China's navy to Pacifican parts and training.
### 1885 · The Carolines against Germany — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Germany takes New Guinea in 1884 and the Marshalls in 1885; the 1885 Caroline crisis is settled by Pope Leo XIII (Hist.).
- Move: with the Carolines and Palau included in the approved 1876 sale, Pacifica accepts papal arbitration and gives Germany trading and coaling rights, as Spain did historically.
- Gain: the island line from Guam to Palau stays Pacifican without a quarrel with Berlin. The Marshalls are left to Germany.
### 1885–87 · Port Hamilton — RETAINED

- Anchor: the Panjdeh crisis brings Britain and Russia close to war; Britain occupies Port Hamilton from 1885 to 1887 (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): a near-war that Britain loses diplomatically.
- Settlement (PROPOSED): Britain leaves in 1887 against a guarantee of Korean territory by China, Russia and Pacifica.
- Gain: Pacifica becomes a formal guarantor of Korea without owning any of it.
### 1886–89 · The managed succession — CR-016

Kaiulani adopts Julian while his mother lives and raises him as successor; the regency is established without civil war. The unrelated decorated commander provisionally named Rafael Soberanes replaces the active Marcus II role. Kiyoshi, Elias’s eldest son by a Satsuma mother, leads the Japanese naval party.

PROPOSED implementation: Truce negotiated in 1886–1887, signed spring 1887 while Elias lives; factional council and constitutional designation; one autumn Donner Pass standoff where Caleb dies saving Soberanes. Exact shooter remains open. Pacifica consolidates in 1887–1889; no foreign military adventure is scheduled within a year of the succession.
### 1887–1900 · Kaiulani's measure of nations — APPROVED

- Doctrine: from the regency settlement, no general suffrage, which would hand the Union the Texas and Hawaii playbook. Earlier republican representation remains governed by CR-014. A narrow franchise, earned by examination, votes only in certain referendums (APPROVED). Legitimacy comes from fairness and outcomes people can measure and see. The contest is fought on Pacifica's scoreboard, not the Union's. This is Kaiulani's lifelong project.
- Anchor: Prussia's statistical bureau under Ernst Engel; The Economist, founded 1843; the International Statistical Institute, 1885. Carleton Watkins's Yosemite photographs helped persuade Congress to protect the valley in 1864. Muybridge photographed motion in sequence at Stanford's Palo Alto farm in 1878 and projected it in 1879. Jacob Riis published his tenement photographs in 1890 (Hist.).
- Move:
  - a comparative statistics bureau, founded about 1888, publishing an annual ranking of nations and cities on infant mortality, life expectancy, typhoid deaths, real wages against food prices, hours of work, conscript heights, literacy, homicide, clean water and electric light per household, and the cost and time of travel;
  - a prestige weekly in English, French, German, Spanish, Japanese and Chinese, reporting against those measures with the cable's speed;
  - images: lantern-slide lectures and stereoscope cards from the 1870s, halftone photographs in the weekly from 1890, Muybridge's work funded until projected film around 1888–90, and newsreels in music halls from Chicago to Berlin in the 1890s;
  - study tours for foreign teachers, doctors, trade unionists and journalists.
- The bargain at home: clean water and sewers, cheap food from irrigation, good wages from the electrical economy, cheap transport, fair and fast courts, schools, and social insurance on Bismarck's model of the 1880s.
- Rule: never fake a number, and publish Pacifica's weak spots too. One exposed fabrication would end the bureau's authority.
- Gain: Union reformers study in San Francisco as Americans once studied in Germany, and Union politicians start campaigning on "Pacifican methods".
- Risk: the Union answers with images of Pacifica's failures; a downturn hurts a regime that promised results; the image-makers know the most.
### 1887–90s · Pearl Harbor — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the US gained exclusive rights to Pearl Harbor in 1887, but the bar was dredged only after 1900 and the base came about 1908–11 (Hist.).
- Move: dredge the bar and build a dry dock from 1887.
- Gain: a fleet base two decades ahead of history, 2,000 miles in front of the coast.
### 1888–89 · Abolition in the Confederacy — RETAINED

Abolition with compensation bonds; the fugitive agreement lapses in 1889 and the Hapa ideal is proclaimed. Pacifica's moral position in Asia improves the same year.
### 1888 · From open borders to selective immigration — APPROVED

- Anchor: the late 1880s close doors everywhere: Canada's head tax of 1885, the Australian colonies' restrictions of 1888, the US Immigration Act of 1891 barring anyone "likely to become a public charge", Natal's literacy test of 1897 (Hist.).
- Move: Kaiulani replaces the early republic's open door with a programme of skilled migrants and sponsored families. The family programme with China, Japan and the Philippines continues; unskilled drifters, above all from the Union, are refused.
- Gain: selection lifts every figure the statistics bureau reports.
- Cost: the switch comes just as the Confederacy frees its slaves in 1888–89, after two decades of the fugitive agreement, and in the same year as the Hapa ideal. Critics, Frederick Douglass among them, call it anti-Black racism. A quiet understanding with the Gulf planters, who fear losing their labour after abolition, may be the real motive; the novel can leave it open.
### 1889 · Samoa — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the Samoan crisis; a cyclone wrecks the American and German squadrons at Apia in March 1889; a three-power condominium follows, then partition in 1899 (Hist.).
- Move: take a seat in the condominium, then Tutuila and Pago Pago at the partition.
- Gain: the best harbour in the South Pacific, on the route to Australia.
### 1880–89 · Let France dig, then buy the canal — APPROVED

- Anchor: Colombia grants the Wyse concession in 1878. De Lesseps's company starts in 1881, keeps a sea-level design until 1887, loses an estimated 22,000 workers to disease and goes bankrupt in February 1889. The US later paid $40m for its remains. Godin de Lépinay proposed a lock-and-lake canal in 1879, and Carlos Finlay proposed in 1881 that mosquitoes carry yellow fever (Hist.).
- Move, 1880: a treaty with Colombia guaranteeing transit and neutrality, with Pacifican first refusal on any transfer of the concession. Pacifica takes control of the Panama Railroad before the French can buy it, and its engineers and contractors work on the French project.
- Move, 1889: buy the works, plant, records and concession from the liquidator. Build Lépinay's lock-and-lake plan with mosquito control from the first day. Lease a Galápagos coaling station to guard the western approach.
- Gain: France pays for the learning and the deaths. The canal opens about 1900–02 for roughly $20–40m plus $200–250m of construction, against $375m for building from 1880. It becomes the regency's achievement.
- Risk: the Union or Britain bids for the remains; the treaty and the railroad are what stop them. Colombia's Thousand Days' War (1899–1902) can still delay the final years.
## Phase IV — 1890–1900: harvest the great-power crises

In the 1890s every other power stumbles at least once: London in 1890, the Union in 1893–95, China in 1894 and 1898, Russia in 1898, Britain in 1899. Pacifica gains most by lending, mediating and settling its open questions while rivals are busy.
### 1890 · The Baring crisis — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Barings nearly fails over Argentine debt in November 1890 and London lending seizes up (Hist.).
- Move: San Francisco syndicates, backed by the treasury, refinance Chile, Mexico and Japan in gold.
- Gain: San Francisco becomes the Pacific's second money market, and Pacific trade starts to be billed in Pacifican dollars.
### 1890 · Wounded Knee — APPROVED

- Anchor: on 29 December 1890 the US Seventh Cavalry killed some 250–300 Lakota at Wounded Knee, many of them women and children, and photographs of the frozen dead circulated (Hist.). Here it happens on Union soil.
- Move: the weekly's first great campaign. Photographs from Wounded Knee run beside the Apache peace of 1865 and the bureau's figures on mortality and rations on Pacifican reservations, followed by lantern lectures across Europe and the Union's own cities.
- Gain: the Union's Indian policy becomes the standard example of its backwardness, in Europe and among Union reformers.
- Risk: the Union answers with Rock Springs, Hells Canyon and the Philippine insurgency, so those records must be clean by 1890.
### 1891 · Chile's civil war — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Congress defeats President Balmaceda in 1891; a sailors' brawl at Valparaíso brings the US and Chile close to war (Hist.).
- Move: quiet support for the Congressional side, which wins.
- Gain: Chile, with the strongest navy in South America, stays Pacifica's partner.
### 1891 onward · Russian railway ambitions — revised by CR-016-R1

- Anchor: construction begins at Vladivostok in May 1891, the same month the Tsarevich is attacked at Ōtsu (Hist.). Historical completion dates are comparisons only; Russia never completes its own Pacific through route, while Japan completes the eastern link under the later approved ownership sequence.
- Move: Japan takes Vladivostok and the eastern railway before Russia joins its trunk to the Pacific. Japan completes the eastern link and holds the terminal; China owns the Manchurian section with Japanese running rights and a Pacifican wartime board (CR-018). Retaining Russian gauge is proposed. Russia retains its own Transbaikalian section and advances into Manchuria in the new 1904–1905 war. Fleet readiness and capacity remain audit targets.
### 1891–1900 · Electrifying Asia to Pacifican standards — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Kyoto opens Japan's first hydro plant in 1891 with American-style equipment. Tokyo buys German 50 Hz equipment in 1895 and Osaka American 60 Hz in 1896, splitting Japan's grid to this day (Hist.).
- Move: Pacifican firms, financed through the alliance credit lines, equip Japanese, Chinese and Philippine cities to one Pacifican standard. At home: electrolytic copper refining at Butte and in Arizona, aluminium and carbide at the dams, electric pumps in the Central Valley, electric streetcars and electric gear on warships.
- Gain: Asia's grids are locked into Pacifican equipment for decades, and Pacifica sells both the copper and the machines. Nitrogen fixation from about 1903 later ends the dependence on Chilean nitrate.
- Risk: the economy-wide payoff comes only when factories are rebuilt around electric motors, in the 1910s and 1920s. By 1900 the edge is in particular sectors.
### 1892–1900 · The Pacific cable — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the San Francisco–Honolulu–Guam–Manila cable came only in 1903, after a British line to Australia in 1902 (Hist.).
- Move: lay San Francisco–Honolulu in the mid-1890s and continue through Midway and Guam to Manila by 1900.
- Gain: Pacifica reads the Pacific's news first and sets the cable rates for everyone else.
### 1893–94 · Panic, Coxey's Army and Pullman — APPROVED

- Anchor: the Panic of 1893 brings mass unemployment; Coxey's Army marches on Washington in 1894; federal troops break the Pullman strike the same year (Hist.).
- Move: the bureau's wage and unemployment tables, newsreels of Pacifican works and relief, and open recruitment of Union skilled workers.
- Gain: the Union's own voters make the comparison, a year before Pacifica rescues the Union's gold.
### 1894 · The Bread March — APPROVED

- Anchor: Coxey's Army and the other industrial armies of 1894; Colorado's populist governor Davis Waite, who in 1893 preferred blood "to the horses' bridles" to surrender on silver (Hist.).
- Cause: the door has been closed since 1888 and the Union is in depression. Industrial armies gather in Colorado, Waite looks away, and armed men try to force the passes into Pacifica. A border post is attacked and men die.
- Move (APPROVED): Pacifica detains the marchers by the hundreds and tries the armed men.
- Sentences: precise terms and detention location remain PROPOSED. The approved course is fair trials on Pacifican film and return to the Union in the 1897 prisoner deal; do not assume an already selected Guam penal settlement.
- Gain: revanchism and silver populism are tied to a bloody fiasco.
- Risk: in 1895 Dreyfus goes to Devil's Island and penal islands become a byword for injustice; the Union's press will make the comparison.
### 1893–95 · The Union gold rescue — RETAINED

- Anchor: the Panic of 1893 and the Union's 1895 gold crisis, which historically needed the Morgan–Belmont syndicate (Hist.).
- Move (RETAINED): Pacifica rescues the Union's gold reserve.
- Price (PROPOSED): Union recognition of Pacifica's canal rights, its neutrality in any Asian war, and tariff concessions.
### 1894 · Kaiulani brokers the peace — APPROVED

- Anchor: the Donghak rising brings Chinese and Japanese troops into Korea in mid-1894. Japan wins at Pyongyang and the Yalu in September. Cixi wants peace before her sixtieth birthday in November and China seeks mediators; Japan refuses, fearing the European intervention that comes in April 1895 (Hist.).
- Move: Kaiulani backs Japan's case for Korean independence with coal and equipment, but no Pacifican combat. In October–November 1894 she brokers an armistice: Korea independent and neutral, China gives up suzerainty, a moderate indemnity, the commercial rights Japan won at Shimonoseki, no mainland territory. Pacifica guarantees the terms against any European challenge.
- Gain: no Port Arthur massacre, no Liaodong cession and no Triple Intervention, so Russia loses its pretext. China is not bankrupted and Cixi stays a friend. Japan's smaller indemnity leaves its gold standard partly resting on Pacifican loans.
- Risk: Japan's army and public feel robbed of victory. Formosa stays Chinese, with Pacifican administration of camphor, Keelung coal and customs revenues securing the indemnity and paying Japan (APPROVED, 18 October). The tension over Japan's desired prize remains.
### 1895–97 · Japan's reserves in San Francisco — PROPOSED

- Anchor: Japan moved to gold in 1897 with the Chinese indemnity, kept mostly in London (Hist.).
- Move: the smaller indemnity and a Pacifican gold loan, both held in San Francisco, fund the change.
- Gain: Japan's currency rests on Pacifican deposits, which is quiet but decisive leverage.
### 1895–97 · Korea under the 1894 settlement — APPROVED

- Anchor: Queen Min was murdered by Japanese-backed assassins in October 1895, and King Gojong fled to the Russian legation in 1896 (Hist.). Both follow from Japan's victory and Russia's rise, which the 1894 settlement prevents.
- Settlement: Korea is independent and neutral under the guarantee of China, Japan and Pacifica. Japan gains economic predominance: the Seoul–Busan railway, mines and ports.
- Gain: Russia has no opening in Korea, and Japan's interests are served without annexation.
- Risk: Japan's army still calls the armistice a stolen victory. Its outlet is the Primorye war.
### 1896 · Li Hongzhang's world tour — PROPOSED

- Anchor: at the 1896 coronation in Moscow, Li grants Russia the Chinese Eastern Railway across Manchuria; he then visits Europe and America (Hist.).
- Move: offer railway credit before he leaves China, so that China builds its own Manchurian line and gives Pacifica the Hankow–Canton trunk.
- Gain: Russia's shortcut to Vladivostok is blunted; Pacifica finances China's main north–south line.
- Risk: Russia is provoked earlier; Pacifica must not ask for too much.
### 1896–97 · President Whitney — APPROVED

- Anchor: in 1896 the 36-year-old Bryan won the Democratic nomination with his Cross of Gold speech and lost to McKinley. William C. Whitney led the gold Democrats at that convention; he had been Navy Secretary in 1885–89, launching the steel navy, and was a New York street-railway magnate (Hist.).
- In this timeline: the silver heartland is Pacifican and the Bread March has tied silver populism to a fiasco. The prestige weekly and a Union business elite holding Pacifican shares back the gold wing. The Democrats nominate Whitney, who beats McKinley's platform of tariffs and recovering the West.
- Congress: the 1894 midterms gave the revanchists the House, which obstructed in 1895–96. The 1896 election returns a supportive House. The Senate, chosen by state legislatures, follows only once the recovery under Whitney is visible (APPROVED sequence: after the 1898 state elections, with the Senate following in 1899).
- Policy: compete with Pacifica, don't fight it. Neutrality in the Primorye war, Union capital in Pacifican projects, and a recovery from 1897 fed by Klondike and South African gold that passes through Pacifican banks.
- 1897 prisoner deal (APPROVED, CR-015 E10): the Bread March prisoners come home in exchange for a commerce and arbitration treaty with Pacifica, Whitney's first diplomatic success.
- Risk: the charge of a bought president; revanchists go underground.
### 1889–1900 · The Confederacy splits: the Gulf Republic — APPROVED

- Anchor: Mississippi's 1890 constitution disenfranchises Black voters; cotton falls to about 5 cents a pound in 1894; the Farmers' Alliance and the Populists are strongest in the South. The Republic of Texas stood alone from 1836 to 1845, and the German customs union of 1834 preceded unification by decades (Hist.).
- Pressures: abolition in 1888–89 removes the Confederacy's founding purpose. The Panic bankrupts cotton, Richmond misses coupons on the compensation bonds and Pacifica's guarantee pays them, and Whitney's Union offers the Upper South a way home. Confederate presidents serve single six-year terms, so the 1897 election is the breaking point.
- Sequence (PROPOSED dates):
    1. 1891: a Deep South nationalist narrowly wins the presidency, and the Gulf states feel shut out.
    2. 1895: a Gulf economic compact with Pacifica, with lower tariffs, rail and port agreements and a currency pegged to the Pacifican gold dollar. No free movement of people.
    3. 1897: the election deadlocks between the Gulf, Deep South and Upper South blocs; Whitney offers the Upper South a customs union.
    4. 1898–99: Virginia and North Carolina leave and negotiate reunion with the Union. Pacifica stays neutral.
    5. 1899–1900: Texas, Louisiana and Arkansas secede and form the Federal Republic of the Gulf, with Indian Territory as Sequoyah, with a trade treaty and a mutual defence pact with Pacifica. Pacifican ships at Mobile deter Richmond.
    6. From 1900: a rump Confederacy of South Carolina, Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi and Florida survives, tied to Lancashire's cotton.
- The Federal Republic of the Gulf (República Federal del Golfo; République fédérale du Golfe), the Gulf Republic for short (APPROVED, 18 October). Austin is the capital and New Orleans the economic centre. The constitution follows Pacifica's model: a strong executive, a merit civil service, independent courts and equal civil rights, with a narrow franchise earned by examination that votes only in certain referendums. It has its own citizenship, so there is no free movement into Pacifica before accession, and Galveston opens to Pacifican ships under the defense pact. Mobile and Birmingham remain in rump Alabama under inherited treaty rights (PROPOSED mechanism); Galveston is the fallback base.
- Accession (APPROVED): three consecutive years at or better than the weakest Pacifican member for infant mortality, literacy, real wages, homicide and typhoid deaths. A sixth measure limits the Black–white gap, tribal freedmen included. Statistical definitions and the permitted margin still require specification.
- Armistice map (APPROVED): Tennessee is Union from 1865. Indian Territory is Confederate except the Public Land Strip; the proposed 1871 convention completes the northern transfer. Arkansas joins the Gulf Republic; Alabama stays in the rump.
- Risk: New Orleans depends on the Mississippi, which runs through the Union; Texan republicanism may resist accession; critics call it a planters' republic under Pacifican protection.
### 1895–1902 · Cuba: the Union fights, Pacifica shapes the peace — APPROVED

- End state (APPROVED): an independent Cuba under Pacifican influence; Puerto Rico to the Union; no foreign bases in Cuba.
- Anchor: the Cuban war of independence from February 1895, with Martí killed in May; Weyler's reconcentration camps of 1896–97, with deaths in the tens of thousands and possibly well over 100,000; Spain's failed autonomy grant of January 1898. The Maine exploded in Havana harbour on 15 February 1898, killing about 260; a US naval court blamed a mine, while the 1976 Rickover study found a coal-bunker fire more likely. The Teller Amendment of April 1898 renounced annexation (Hist.).
- Sequence (PROPOSED):
    1. 1895–97: the exile junta works from New Orleans, as the 1850–51 filibusters did. The weekly campaigns against the camps, and Pacifica sends medical aid.
    2. February 1898: the Maine explodes. Pacifican engineers, who know coal bunkers from the fuel programme, privately suspect a bunker fire. Kaiulani keeps the finding in a drawer.
    3. April 1898: Whitney delivers an ultimatum to Spain and renounces annexation from the start.
    4. April–August 1898: the Union wins at sea, lands in Puerto Rico and blockades Cuba. The Cuban army holds the countryside and disease destroys the Spanish garrisons. Pacifica, committed to the Primorye war, stays neutral; the Gulf states let supplies through to the insurgents.
    5. Late 1898: Cuba independent; Puerto Rico ceded to the Union; Spain compensated. Pacifica takes over Cuba's share of the Spanish colonial debt at a discount, secured on Cuban customs and administered on the model of the Chinese Inspectorate. The Union occupies no part of Cuba, so there is no Platt Amendment; the debt convention takes its place.
    6. 1898 elections: victory and recovery give Whitney's party the state legislatures, and the Senate follows in 1899.
    7. 1899–1901: Carlos Finlay, a Cuban from Camagüey working in Havana and funded by Pacifica since 1881, frees Havana from yellow fever.
    8. 1901–02: Cuban constitution and the guarantee treaty.
- Guarantee (APPROVED): Pacifica, the Gulf Republic and the Union jointly guarantee Cuba's independence. Any intervention needs all three, so the Union is inside the tent but cannot act alone, and every Cuban crisis plays out on a shared stage, as in the three-power Samoan condominium of 1889.
- First president (APPROVED): Tomás Estrada Palma of the New Orleans junta; inauguration and electoral detail remain unchosen.
- Pacifica's levers: the customs administration, Finlay and public health, the sheltered exiles, Havana's tramways and electric light built to Pacifican standards, Cuban students in San Francisco, and Gulf trade through New Orleans.
- Nicaragua (PROPOSED): a victorious Union without Pacific access will want its own canal; historically the Maritime Canal Company dug in Nicaragua in 1887–93. Pacifica buys exclusive Nicaraguan canal rights around 1899–1900, as the US did in 1914.
- Risk: Cuban sugar's natural market is the Union. And if the Maine finding ever comes out, the Union learns Pacifica let it fight over a probable accident.
### 1896–1900 · The Klondike and Canada — APPROVED and PROPOSED

- Anchor: gold is found in the Yukon in August 1896; the 1897–98 rush runs through Skagway and Dyea after the all-Canadian routes fail. The White Pass & Yukon Route was built in 1898–1900 with London money, the Mounted Police collected customs at the summits in 1898, a US assay office opened in Seattle the same year, and the boundary was settled on the US line in 1903 (Hist.).
- Policy (APPROVED): firm but fair. No territory is given up and the panhandle line holds. Pacifica seeks a win-win that deepens Canada's dependence.
- Arrangement (PROPOSED): Pacifica builds and owns the Skagway–Whitehorse railway with a Canadian minority stake. Transit is bonded for a modest fee, with Canadian customs at the summits. Klondike gold is assayed, banked and minted in Pacifica, and Seattle supplies the Yukon. Yukon profits come from transit and trade, not mining.
- Gain: the Yukon's only practical artery is Pacifican, and Canada has no reason to drift toward the Union.
- Risk: Canadian resentment over the boundary.
### 1897–99 · The scramble and the Primorye war — APPROVED

- Anchor: Germany seizes Kiaochow in November 1897 and Russia demands an ice-free port. Russia's Far East has no rail link to Europe until about 1903: the Ussuri line joins Vladivostok only to Khabarovsk, in 1897, and Vladivostok has about 29,000 people (Hist.).
- Trigger: Russia's demand for Port Arthur or a Korean harbour, against the 1887 and 1894 guarantees.
- Move: Japan fights on land in 1898–99. Pacifica holds the sea so that Russia cannot reinforce through Suez, and lends the money; China stays benevolently neutral. No Pacifican land army.
- Settlement: Japan takes the Ussuri region with Vladivostok and the Razdolnaya valley, and all of Sakhalin; China recovers the Amur's north bank; its economic predominance in Korea is confirmed. Pacifica takes no port in China and declares against partition.
- Gain: Russia loses its southern Far Eastern bases and the named coastal territories. CR-018 fixes Kamchatka, the Okhotsk coast and Nikolayevsk as Russian until 1921. The Chinese-owned Manchurian railway, with Japanese running rights, remains the lifeline to Japan’s terminal and later Siberian holdings. Pacifica’s guarantee remains valuable while Japan’s growing strength drives the compact renewals.
- France (APPROVED): neutral; the alliance focuses on Germany, and Fashoda and the Dreyfus crisis distract Paris. French lending continues to support Russia. Russia's drive for a warm-water port shifts to Manchuria and northern Korea, so those guarantees must hold.
### 1898 · Shelter for reformers and revolutionaries — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the Hundred Days' Reform ends in a palace coup in September 1898; Kang Youwei and Liang Qichao flee, and Liang spends 1899–1900 in Hawaii. Sun Yat-sen, schooled in Honolulu, founded the Revive China Society there in 1894 (Hist.).
- Move: Honolulu and San Francisco shelter both reformers and revolutionaries.
- Gain: whoever governs China after the Qing owes Pacifica.
### 1899–1900 · Britain's Boer War — PROPOSED

- Anchor: the Boer War strains Britain from October 1899; in 1901 it conceded canal control to the US in the Hay–Pauncefote Treaty (Hist.).
- Move: settle everything with London at once: the canal, the Alaska boundary, the Samoa partition and recognition of Pacifica's Pacific position.
- Gain: British acceptance is fixed before Britain recovers.
1900 · The Boxers and the canal — PROPOSED and APPROVED
- Anchor: the Boxers besiege the legations; the southern viceroys keep their provinces out of the war; Russia occupies Manchuria. The US later returned part of its indemnity as scholarships (Hist.).
- Move (PROPOSED): a contingent for the relief force, a guarantee for the southern viceroys, a prompt withdrawal, and the whole indemnity turned into scholarships.
- Move (APPROVED): the canal opens about 1900–02 as the regency's work.
- Gain: influence in the Yangtze valley and the south, a new cohort of Chinese students, and the gate between the oceans.
