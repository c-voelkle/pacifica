Elias's Rise to Power, 1844–1861
16. Okt. 2026 · @Andi
Pacifica is born on 31 July 1856 in Honolulu, when Elias signs its declaration. He marries Kaiulani in February 1857, once both states have ratified. This draft traces how a foreigner who arrives in 1844 with Caleb and a horse reaches that table in twelve years.
How to read this
Each phase is a table. The last column uses the master storyline's labels, plus one more:
• Hist. A real event with a real date, used as an anchor. Where the novel changes its outcome, the entry reads Hist. → PROPOSED.
• APPROVED / RETAINED / PROPOSED / OPEN as defined in the master storyline. Almost everything here is PROPOSED.
Three assumptions run through the whole draft:
• Elias is born c. 1824, so he is about 13 at the 1837 foreclosure, 20 on arrival and 32 at the founding.
• Kaiulani is born c. 1838. 
• Elias's power base is the treasury, which pays for the yards, the arsenal and the army. He holds no head-of-state office before 1856.
What this draft changes in the master storyline:
• The wedding moves from "late 1850s or early 1860s" to February 1857, after both ratifications.
• "Pacifica" gets a definition: a confederation of California and Hawaii, declared 31 July 1856 and in force from November 1856.
• The 1852 reorganization gets a cause (the 1851 Vigilance Committee) and an office for Elias.
• Kaiulani is Kuhina Nui from 1855, which gives her a constitutional check on Elias and a legal claim to the later regency.
• Elias becomes Protector of Pacifica by a legal path written into the 1856 Articles.
• A parallel industrial and military track (1847–61), assisted immigration to about 1.2 million people, and Prussian-style universal service bring Pacifica to Fort Sumter able to mobilize about 180,000 soldiers and some 90 warships by 1864: decisive in a three-sided war, too weak to fight the Union alone.

Boundaries, royalty and debt
California settles for roughly its real-world borders, a 5% royalty collected at the mint and the port, and £3 million of Mexico's London debt. All three are PROPOSED; the figures are approximate and from memory, to be checked before they go into prose.
The eastern boundary. On paper Mexican Alta California ran east to the Rockies, south of the 42nd parallel. In practice Mexican government reached only the coast and the inland valleys; the Great Basin belonged to Paiute, Shoshone and Ute peoples and, after 1847, to the Mormons. A republic that cannot police the Basin should not claim it.
• The 1850 Treaty of Monterey fixes the line that California's own 1849 convention chose historically. It runs south from the 42nd parallel along 120°W to Lake Tahoe, then diagonally southeast to the Colorado River at 35°N, then down the Colorado to the Gila.
• The southern line comes from the 1848 settlement with Mexico: from the Colorado–Gila junction to the coast just south of San Diego, as historically.
• Why both sides accept it. The United States gets the overland trails, Salt Lake and the Basin; California gets peace, recognition and the Colorado crossing at Yuma.
• A ripple in the United States. With no free state of California to admit, the Compromise of 1850 is fought only over Texas, New Mexico and Utah, which changes the North–South arithmetic of the 1850s.
• A seed for later. The Comstock silver lode, found in 1859 in Washoe, lies just east of the line in US territory, but San Francisco money works it. That makes the Washoe crisis of 1859–60, Pacifica's first setback (Phase 6).

Structure of the Confederation. These are proposed terms, written so that later events have something specific to break.
• Members: the Republic of California and the Kingdom of Hawaii, each self-governing at home.
• Shared: defense and the navy, a customs union, the gold currency and policy toward third powers.
• The Confederal Council: equal delegations, presided over by a Chancellor. The Chancellery is Elias's new power base, above both states and answerable to neither electorate.
• Article XIV, the Protectorate: an emergency office, described in the next section.
• Clauses Kaiulani wins: Hawaiian land cannot pass to foreigners without royal consent; either member may withdraw on notice; and any Protectorate needs the Kuhina Nui's countersignature. Elias later overrides all three.
• Japan is not a member. The Shimoda agreement makes it an associated partner, leaving room for later change.

The war base: people, money and service
To matter in a continental war, Pacifica needs about 1.2 million people by 1861, three times the historical California. It gets them the way Australia's gold colonies did: gold, cheap public land, and passage paid from land sales. Victoria grew from about 77,000 people in 1851 to about 540,000 in 1861 (approximate). All figures here are PROPOSED placeholders.
People. The Immigration Law of 1850 uses public land sales to pay passages from Europe, as South Australia and Victoria did. It naturalizes immigrants after three years and an oath, or at once after military service.

Service. The Defense Law of 1855, passed after the Walker affair and the Hawaiian crisis, follows the Prussian model:

Readiness in April 1861
In April 1861 Pacifica has a larger trained army than the Union, about 110,000 regulars and reserves against 16,000 federal regulars. But it can never match the Union's mobilized peak, so it wins by being the third weight in a war the Union is already fighting. No one was ready in 1861. The Union's two million soldiers and 671 ships are wartime totals, reached by mobilizing. Readiness therefore means the capacity to mobilize: trained reserves, stockpiled rifles, rail, a staff, and ships on order.

Path to Protector (1856–65)
Elias becomes Protector of Pacifica without breaking a law: each step uses a clause, an office or a vote that already exists. Each step is defensible taken alone; the total is a coup. All steps are PROPOSED except the historical anchors.
1. 1856, Article XIV of the Articles. In war or grave threat to the Confederation, the Council may name a Protector by a two-thirds vote, signed by California's president and countersigned by Hawaii's Kuhina Nui. The Protector commands the joint forces and customs and may issue emergency decrees, for one year. Renewal needs both legislatures, and a Protector cannot amend either member's constitution. Elias wants the clause because he has read Plutarch on the Roman dictatorship; Kaiulani wrote its limits; British observers think of Cromwell.
2. 1859–60, the Washoe crisis. After the Pyramid Lake ambush, California sends Hays's column across the line. When Washington threatens war, the Council debates naming a Protector, and Kaiulani, as Kuhina Nui, refuses to countersign. Pacifica withdraws. Elias learns that the limits he accepted can be used against him, and that he must wait for a divided United States.
3. Apr–May 1861, first invocation. The American Civil War begins (Fort Sumter, 12 Apr 1861, hist.). Confederate privateers, Union demands on Pacifica's ports and gold, and new Washoe incidents push the Council to invoke Article XIV. Vallejo signs; Kaiulani countersigns, the hardest decision of her marriage. Elias is Protector for one year.
4. 1862 and 1863, renewals. Both legislatures renew under wartime pressure, and renewal becomes routine.
5. 30 Nov 1863. Kamehameha IV dies; Lot becomes Kamehameha V. (Hist.)
6. Aug 1864. Lot imposes a new constitution that abolishes the office of Kuhina Nui. (Hist.) Article XIV's countersignature now has no holder, and Elias's lawyers argue the requirement has lapsed. Kaiulani loses her office through her husband's closest friend.
7. 1865, the amendment. As the American war ends, the Californian Assembly and Lot's legislature amend the Articles. The Protectorate becomes permanent, and the emergency becomes the constitution. OPEN: permanent office, or life tenure for Elias.
