# Pacifica — War Base, Finance, Immigration and Industrial Assumptions

**Status:** Author-approved **scenario direction**, numerical details **provisional pending plausibility review**.  
**Source:** [rise_to_power.md](sources/rise_to_power.md), *Industry and arms, 1847–61* and *The war base: people, money and service*.  
**Narrative:** [master_storyline.md](master_storyline.md). This document supports the story; it does not replace it.

These figures describe what the world of the novel would need to support if Pacifica is to enter the Civil War as an exceptionally organized, heavily armed but still materially constrained third power. They are **not** automatically established historical facts or final canonical numbers. Sources and discrepancies are recorded so we can revise without silently inflating the empire.

## 1. Population around 1861

| Population group | Working count |
|---|---:|
| American-born | 350,000 |
| European immigrants | 300,000 |
| Chinese | 250,000 |
| Mexican, Sonoran, Chilean | 120,000 |
| Californios and Indigenous residents | 100,000 |
| Hawaii | 70,000 |
| **Total** | **1,190,000** |

This implies about 1.2 million inhabitants in the California–Hawaii Confederation. **Compare:** historical California's 1860 census counted **379,994** inhabitants (not necessarily a full Indigenous count), and Hawaii counted about **69,800** in 1860. Their combined historical order of magnitude is about 450,000. An alternate 1.19 million is therefore about **2.6 times the contemporary combined historical population** and requires a very large, sustained immigration divergence.

Sources:
- [California Department of Finance, state census series](https://dof.ca.gov/media/docs/forecasting/Demographics/2020-census-demographics/California-Apportionment-1860-2020.pdf): California 1850 92,597; 1860 379,994.
- [University of Hawaii historical censuses](https://guides.library.manoa.hawaii.edu/c.php?g=105181&p=684171): Hawaii 1860 approximately 69,800.
- [Australian Parliament's migration history](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Migration/MigrationPathway/Report/Chapter_2_-_Migration_and_the_Formation_of_Modern_Australia): Victoria grew from about 77,345 to 538,628 in 1851–61 with gold and significant migration. A rapid rise is possible, but Victoria is an analogy—not proof that California could finance triple its historical population.

### Specific demographic pressure points

The **250,000 Chinese residents** are especially demanding. Actual 1860 California counted **34,933** Chinese residents, roughly 7.2 times fewer. A larger stream might be motivated by war and poverty in southern China, demand for skilled workers, access to Pacific ports and legal security absent in historical California. But the story needs ship capacity, credit-ticket finance, labor conditions, return migration, gender balance, and how much the new republic honors its stated civic equality. [1860 historical Chinese population series](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED111888.pdf).

The Hawaiian 70,000 is approximately the actual whole kingdom. It should be **included once** in the confederal total and never added again in ship crews or migrant numbers as if they were separate populations.

The Indigenous count cannot be casually merged with 'Californio' without specifying what is enumerated: many Indigenous communities are autonomous, different registries omit people, and political membership is not equivalent to census classification.

**Approved mechanism:** immigration financed partly by public land sales and encouraged through favorable naturalization terms; seasonal and contract labor, including abusive credit-ticket arrangements, creates future legal and moral conflicts. **Unresolved:** what specific land is saleable, how Indigenous territorial agreements constrain grants, who advances passage money, and whether all groups can obtain citizenship and participate in military service.

## 2. Government revenue around 1860 (millions of dollars/year)

| Source | Working annual revenue |
|---|---:|
| Customs | $12.0 million |
| Property and license taxes | $4.0 million |
| Public land sales | $3.0 million |
| Gold royalty at 5% | $2.5 million |
| Railway, telegraph, mint | $1.5 million |
| Quicksilver | $1.0 million |
| **Total** | **$24.0 million** |

The model assumes defense spending around **$11 million** annually, about **46% of revenue**, supporting trained personnel, ships, powder and foreign contracts. Other public services, infrastructure, debt service, immigration subsidies and a credible public cash reserve must all be funded from what remains or from borrowing.

At $50 million annual gold production, a 5% royalty yields $2.5 million **only if all chargeable gold is recorded and collected**. Smuggling, unregistered streams and disputed rights reduce that figure; sampling the mint and ports cannot guarantee perfect compliance. The inherited mining law may authorize leases and public royalties but does not prove any specific 5% assessment as historical fact.

In the 1848 Mexican recognition arrangement, California assumes **£3 million** of Mexican London debt at a proposed **5% annual coupon**. Annual nominal interest would be **£150,000** before amortization, financing costs and risk premiums. The debt places British creditors in a position to veto adventures indirectly by withholding refinancing. **Open:** exact debt instrument, maturity, conversion, creditor claims and whether Mexico would accept these terms.

## 3. Military manpower

| Force category | April 1861 working scenario | Wartime peak (1863–65) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Pacifican regulars | ~20,000 | Included below |
| Pacifican trained reserve | ~90,000 | Included below |
| Pacifican field mobilization | ~110,000 potentially trained | ~180,000 |
| Allied units | Limited | ~20,000 |

The Defense Law of 1855 is inspired by European models: two years with the colors at age twenty, then five in reserve and seven in territorial militia. With a new intake of **15,000 men annually**, two overlapping cohorts imply **about 30,000** with the colors at steady state, not 20,000. Likewise a force of 90,000 trained reserves needs detailed cohort arithmetic, exemptions and attrition. **Resolve before finalizing figures.**

Early service also has to account for foreign workers and the birth-based and naturalized populations. If citizenship can be accelerated through military service, the script must not simultaneously exclude large parts of the immigrant workforce without explanation. Chinese volunteer battalions and their officers' status must be specified, not just attributed to a general commitment to integration.

A mobilization of ~180,000 from 1.19 million is about **15%** of the population, an extreme wartime effort requiring replacement workers, tax collections, food imports, and household consequences. The central literary premise is **not** that Pacifica could defeat the United States outright: its strength matters because it fights within a three-sided Civil War where Union and Confederacy already consume one another's resources.

## 4. Navy, heavy industry and supply

| Capability | April 1861 scenario | Wartime ambition |
|---|---|---|
| Ships | ~35 steam warships, of which ~4 are armored | ~90 warships total |
| Ironclads ordered | ~4 from British yards | Up to ~22 armored ships by 1864 |
| Rifles | ~150,000 stockpiled; manufacture proposed ~25,000/year | Must replace combat losses and equip new recruits |
| Imported systems | Armor plate, pig iron, coal, Armstrong/heavy guns | Reduced but **never** eliminated by local production |
| Infrastructure | Benicia, Mare Island, Carquinez works, Sierra construction, telegraph, Colorado steamers | Rail over the Sierra completed around 1864 |

### Reality checks

The historical Union Navy entered the Civil War with roughly **42 ships in commission**, grew rapidly through purchase and conversion, and reached hundreds of ships by war's end. The count includes transports, patrol craft and numerous shallow-water types, so raw vessel counts do not compare like-for-like with oceangoing armored squadrons. [American Battlefield Trust](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/ready-war-union-navy-1861), [Smithsonian National Museum of American History](https://www.americanhistory.si.edu/explore/exhibitions/price-of-freedom/online/civil-war/war-at-sea).

The **22 ironclads by 1864** are the hardest fleet benchmark. They require hundreds of thousands of tons of fuel, iron supply chains, steam-engine capacity, dock space, crews and replacements. Building them in Britain's yards also raises neutrality, export-control and American diplomatic risks. We should audit the **types** (wood-plated river batteries, armored converted steamers, true seagoing ironclads) rather than assuming 22 similarly capable battleships.

A **25,000-per-year** rifle factory introduced around 1860 cannot itself explain a **150,000-rifle** stockpile by April 1861. Significant imports, earlier production or lower stock numbers must account for the rest. The story should distinguish ammunition supply from firearms stock: percussion caps, cartridge manufacturing and saltpeter become important vulnerabilities.

### Plausible industrial sequence

| Years | Working industrial developments |
|---|---|
| 1847–1849 | Carquinez powder works and boatyard; Peter Donahue's foundry, public contracts for engines and mining machinery |
| 1850–1852 | New Almaden quicksilver and cap production; Benicia arsenal; military college; mine and mint standardization |
| 1853–1855 | Telegraph, modest naval force against Walker, Mare Island yard; contracts for foreign rifles, coal and plate |
| 1855–1858 | Japanese exchanges, Sacramento railway construction, Colorado steamboats, imported advanced artillery |
| 1858–1861 | Sierra telegraph and rail grade, imported or locally armored vessels, domestic small arms and engines; heavy equipment still imported |

Industrial growth should be represented through workshops, labor relationships, failures, financing and training, not unexplained time skips producing fleets by decree.

## 5. Political and strategic dependencies

The infrastructure and its vulnerabilities generate specific plots:

- **1856 Vigilance crisis:** law versus privately organized force; one of Elias's first costly actions against Californians, with William T. Sherman a possible historical interlocutor.
- **1857–58 Utah War:** the republic's weak eastward rail access exposed.
- **1858 Fraser rush and British Columbia:** British control of northern approaches complicates expansion.
- **1859 Comstock discovery:** California's finance reaches into neighboring U.S. territory.
- **1860 Paiute / Washoe crisis:** the crossing of a recognized U.S. boundary reveals Pacifica's weaknesses in legitimacy; the Californian army's action against Paiute people is not treated as a morally neutral security exercise.
- **1860 Carson Convention:** humiliation without outright military destruction; Kaiulani's constitutional veto matters.
- **1861–65 Civil War:** continued finance, alliances, mobilization and loss; neither American belligerent is automatically incompetent.

## 6. Model status

**Approved as dramatic and institutional direction:** serious peacetime professionalization; a gold-rich but debt-bearing economy; managed immigration; a diverse military; dependencies on British credit, foreign coal and plate; a major third-party force by the Civil War; corresponding labor and political costs.

**Working figures still subject to audit:** 1.19m population, composition by ethnicity/origin, customs revenue, mine royalty receipts, 15k conscripts per cohort, regular and reserve numbers, 150k rifles, 25k annual domestic rifle output, 35 steam warships in 1861, 90 warships/22 ironclads in 1864, and 180k wartime personnel.

**Remaining work:** Build one integrated demographic/production/mobilization model with lower, central and upper scenarios. Reduce numbers if that proves necessary without weakening the story's central premise. The need for credibility outranks retaining a striking round number.
