## 3. Confederate relationship and character requirements

Davis, Benjamin, St. Clair and Armistead retain independent purposes and the values developed in CR-012. Davis's presidency ends in February 1868; Johnston succeeds, followed by Gordon in 1874. Benjamin's later senator/Secretary of State route remains proposed. Logan's Mobile and 1870 mine work expands his consequences without selecting a completed private business empire. ## 4. Slavery and constitutional sovereignty

The Confederacy survives with slavery after 1865. Pacifica prevents fugitives entering under its association agreement while the Union pursues escape networks, market pressure and citizenship. Free-womb reform in 1874–1875, state abolition and the 1885 sexagenarian law lead to final abolition in 1888–1889. Compensation bonds secured on co-administered customs deepen dependence. Proposed border and court-override mechanics remain qualified; emancipated citizenship and coerced labor remain conflicts rather than solved consequences.

CR-016 transfers the active Polytechnic role to an unrelated commander; Marcus II’s former birth/parentage remains retired provenance. ### C. Northwestern campaign

**OPEN-013-07:** What gives Elias legal and political authority to invade Oregon and Washington under the defensive provisions of Article XIV? Does Kaiulani support, oppose or attempt to limit the operation?

**Decision status:** `approved_direction_with_details_pending`

**Accepted direction:** The Hawaiian packet affair gives Kaiulani her own reason to initiate emergency protection. A Union captain acts on credible intelligence about privateering arrangements, exceeds his authority by removing passengers from a neutral Hawaiian vessel, and sends detainees to Fort Vancouver. A sailor dies during the boarding. The suspected Confederate agent really has been seeking vessels, credit and recruits; this is not a manufactured false flag. Elias has quietly permitted preliminary commercial discussions with Southern agents, and Kaiulani requires disclosure.

The Council's required vote and assent formally recorded by both member governments through their authorized procedures activate Article XIV. Vallejo supports Californian assent and Kaiulani helps secure Hawaiian support; neither role establishes an exclusive personal confederal countersignature. A separate resolution authorizes limited coercive operations against installations supporting continued interference. Kaiulani requires civilian protections, no annexation by emergency decree, no forced declarations of allegiance and renewed negotiations if adequate redress becomes available. Defensive purposes may authorize offensive military action; that argument does not establish the proportionality or legitimacy of every subsequent territorial operation.

Washington later offers release/investigation in exchange for withdrawal. Elias demands broader strategic guarantees and protection for collaborators. The larger war results from choices on both sides, including his refusal to return to the initial limited objective. Kaiulani can organize political opposition and condition renewal; she has no newly invented unilateral power to terminate an active mandate.

**Pending:** Packet name, naval captain and vessel, passengers' identities and roles, exact evidence, custody and release dates, legal instruments, communications chronology and later American counterclaims. The 1849 French seizure of Hawaiian government installations is a historical background candidate for Kaiulani's fear of coercion, not a newly fixed personal biography.

### D. The war in the Mississippi Valley and East

**OPEN-013-13:** Which specific Union units, naval assets, transports or resources are diverted toward the Northwest, and what operations elsewhere are delayed or weakened?

**Accepted direction:** Washington sends a limited expedition to preserve its surviving Pacific foothold and retain a basis for eventual recovery. The defense succeeds enough to justify this commitment. Candidate diversions are the 11th and 13th Iowa Infantry, allocated in autumn 1861 and transported during winter, followed by the 15th and 16th Iowa as organization permits in early 1862. They are separate contingents, not an already formed Crocker brigade.

The proposed route is eastward by rail, by sea to Panama, across the existing isthmian railway under appropriate transit arrangements, and north aboard chartered seagoing steamers. A defended northern Sound anchorage provides the destination. First waves help hold the enclave; later arrivals support limited counterattacks and tie down Pacifican forces. Do not assume every regiment has arrived before Shiloh.

Lancaster and an available Pacific consort concentrate on convoy/base protection; this is redistribution within the Pacific, not a new withdrawal from Farragut. Pensacola is the candidate seagoing warship diverted to the Pacific in autumn 1861 instead of joining the Gulf force; Cape Horn transit, coal and fitting-out determine its arrival. Tennessee river gunboats remain in their theatre. Distinguish the 11th/13th's effect on Grant's established line from the 15th/16th's effect on battle-week reinforcements.

**Pending:** Final regimental assignments, strengths and muster dates, departure/arrival schedules, chartered vessels and costs, neutral transit/coal arrangements, consort identity and Pensacola's actual deployment. Losing one Gulf warship alone does not explain Farragut's defeat.

## 8. Acceptance criteria

The revised story must explain causally how a comparatively small Pacific state affects the outcome of a continental Civil War without overwhelming an intact United States through implausible technological or military superiority.

The Northwestern campaign must have credible geography, military opposition and consequences for federal resource allocation.

The changed outcomes at famous Civil War battles must follow demonstrable causes, not simply reverse history for narrative convenience.

The Union must retain a functioning government and significant industrial capabilities after peace. The Confederacy must obtain genuine independence, with its later relationship to Pacifica emerging through negotiated agreements and changing state interests.

The growing Pacifican order must reflect the competing agency of local populations, political leaders and constituent states, including enslaved people and Indigenous nations.

Finally, continental dominance should compound gradually after the war, creating the rival sovereignties, political grievances and institutional weaknesses that shape Pacifica's later history.

**This change request does not authorize a rewrite of manuscript prose. It is a proposal for revising the master storyline and its supporting documentation.**

## 9. Accepted character direction and consolidated wartime chronology

The following is the working sequence accepted in direction. Seasonal placements, office titles, particular scenes and mechanisms remain provisional unless independently identified as fixed. All three characters are fallible; Thorne does not monopolize professional insight. The post-1865 outcomes of Sherman and Logan are not settled here.

### 9.1 William T. Sherman — a Pacifican professional and internal opponent

Sherman returns to independent California as a civilian banker around 1853. His historical American military service in an occupied California after 1846 cannot simply be copied into this timeline. Business, household and civic commitments develop before the Civil War. Ellen and the children's preferences matter; neither a higher salary nor sudden resentment alone explains the change of allegiance.

The 1856 defense of constitutional government creates a substantive attachment. When banking work ends around 1857, an institutional military appointment under Vallejo keeps Sherman in California. Permanent service and naturalization around 1857–1858 precede the war. Citizenship procedure, household details and exact appointment remain to be finalized.

Sherman supports Kaiulani's 1860 restraint at Washoe. In 1861 he accepts protection and redress for the packet affair but opposes the expansion into an indefinite territorial war. The proposed wartime office is Inspector-General of the Pacifican Army, responsible for readiness, training and reserve organization. His work strengthens the war effort even when he challenges its policy; remaining in service does not make him morally or politically detached from it.

He refuses the Confederate military assignment offered in 1862. Marcus accepts it. Sherman remains a committed Pacifican with professional and political limits, not a secret Union field commander or a simple personal enemy of Thorne. His historical American constitutional allegiance is a deliberate major character divergence that the preceding decade must earn.

### 9.2 Marcus Vance I — field command and the price of commitment

Marcus builds Pacifica's army and staff system before the war, then commands the Northwestern expedition. He makes decisions Thorne could not make from headquarters. He owns both the Columbia successes and his share of responsibility for the Townsend reverse, even if he warned about inadequate support.

Sherman's subsequent inspection criticizes operational failures but rejects an attempt to make Marcus solely responsible for objectives chosen by the government. Their disagreement involves evidence, competence and institutional responsibility rather than automatic personal hostility.

After containing federal counterattacks and handing Northwestern command to a capable deputy, Marcus travels east with a small staff in late 1862. He heads a mission agreed with Richmond, assesses aid requests and coordinates the limited Pacifican contribution. Confederate officers retain command of their armies. Precise staff size, deputy, travel dates and treaty authority remain open under OPEN-013-06 and 19.

### 9.3 Logan Turner — engineering success, loss and commercial potential

Logan is born in a Kentucky holler around 1838–1840 on the working age model and reaches California as an adolescent under CR-014. Developing institutions and Carquinez workshop experience train him as a Californian naval engineer. Ericsson mentorship is accepted in direction; a Donahue apprenticeship is the working entry route. He leaves for the eastern mission in 1861 before the Columbia ship fights. Exact birth year, locality, family, migration, employment and early connection with Thorne remain open.

His gift is diagnosing machinery under real operating conditions and learning from pilots, crews, shipwrights and workers. He contributes to New Orleans' defense as part of a small technical mission. His accurate, practical reports earn Thorne's attention. He is not the solitary inventor of Confederate success and does not command Farragut's opponents.

His later commercial future is a strong potential, not a fixed fortune. Procurement, common specifications, repair networks, transport and finance may grow into a major enterprise. Government contracts and Southern relationships create obligations and conflicts, rather than granting an uncomplicated reward for genius.
