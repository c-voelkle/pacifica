## Summary

1. **One set of organs, renamed only once, in 1887.** Pacifica has a head of state, an executive, two chambers, a court, two independent estates (the Polytechnic and the Office of Public Measures) and **124 counties**. Each has one name per era, and each change has a dated instrument.
2. **Power flows to the centre by design.** The guiding principle is power and the ability to adapt quickly, not representation. Residual power lies with the Realm, counties hold enumerated powers, and their autonomy is **graded and earned** on measured results. No level below the centre holds a veto. Counties are heard in the Senate, not obeyed.
3. **English is the language of power; other languages buy reach.** English is the language of the Realm everywhere. Spanish and Hawaiian are entrenched in the historic lands, other county languages are granted by law on the Swiss model, Japanese joins in 1927, and speaking several languages is rewarded in the franchise.
4. **Every transition is classified.** Elias's rise is legal; the 1874 incorporation of Hawaii is illegal; the 1887 constitution is legal in form; Julian's 1927 provisional application is illegal and is cured in 1928 by an Act of Indemnity, as Bismarck's budgetless rule was in 1866.
5. **The crown comes from Hawaii.** The House of Thorne-Kamehameha is the Hawaiian crown, in abeyance since 1874 and restored by the 1914 Act of Settlement.
6. **The Pacific Concord (太平洋協約, *Taiheiyō Kyōyaku*) joins two co-equal crowns.** The alternat governs precedence. The Sovereign of Pacifica holds the Concord's presidency for **five-year terms** (1927, 1932, 1937) because Pacifica pays and commands the fleet. Japan keeps its crown, Diet, cabinet and home army, and can refuse each renewal.
7. **The Concord rests on two men who understand each other** and on institutions meant to outlast them. The 1932 renewal tests whether they can.
8. **Every territory has one of seven statuses**, from county of the realm to treaty partner.
9. **The state is spread across seats.** Thalassia, on Kāneʻohe Bay, holds the government. San Francisco holds the money, Monterey the law, Sacramento the interior, Carquinez the Polytechnic, Honolulu the Hawaiian crown and Pearl Harbor's outer lochs the fleet. Each faction of the 1887 Truce has its own city, and they meet only through the Crown.

## B. The division of powers — APPROVED

### B1. The principle

Elias, Kaiulani and Julian ask of every power one question: **where must it sit so that Thalassia can see a change first, decide fastest and act everywhere at once?** The answer gives a three-part rule:

- **The centre keeps everything that compounds:** money, credit, force, land and water, talent, the networks and the law.
- **Counties get what is cheap to delegate and expensive to run from the centre:** police, primary schools, roads, sanitation, local courts and the land tax.
- **The estates hold what needs continuity and expertise more than speed:** examinations, audit, statistics and the General Staff.

**Nothing below the centre holds a veto.** Counties are heard through the Senate, where their boards choose the senators. That voice is real, and it is where Julian meets resistance (D, 1915–17 and 1927), but it is a voice, not a lock.

### B2. Who holds what

| Power | Where it sits | The power logic |

| Navy, army, General Staff | The Realm (General Staff inside the Polytechnic); the navy common from 1927 | The county **reserve** is commanded by the Commissioner, not the board |

### B3. The county

- **The Board of Supervisors** (Californian heritage) is elected on the plural franchise. It passes the budget and county by-laws within the enumerated powers, and **chooses the county's senator**, as US state legislatures chose senators before 1913.
- **The Commissioner of the County** is appointed by the Sovereign from the examined civil service. He executes the Realm's law and commands the reserve. He may **suspend a county act for thirty days** pending the Court of the Realm. He **never serves in his home county** and is **rotated at least every five years**, on the Chinese imperial "law of avoidance", so that he never becomes local.
- **County courts of first instance** have judges appointed by the Realm.

**Three grades of county.** Pacifica applies to its own counties the logic it applies to the Gulf Republic: autonomy is earned and kept by measured results.

| Grade | Condition | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Chartered county** | Meets the Office of Public Measures' county standard (infant mortality, examination pass rate, revenue per head, order) for five consecutive years | Home rule: sets its own tax rates within bands, writes its own charter and may apply for a second county language. The Commissioner only supervises. Precedent: California's charter cities of 1879. |
| **Ordinary county** | The default | The Commissioner executes; the board legislates within the enumerated powers |
| **Administered county** | Three years below the standard, or a disorder declared by the Sovereign | **Receivership**: the board is suspended and a commission governs for up to five years, renewable by the Senate |

**Crown cities.** Thalassia, the capital district on Kāneʻohe Bay, is governed directly by the Crown. San Francisco is a city-county, as it has been since the Consolidation Act of 1856. Others may be chartered as Crown cities (OPEN-021-12). The roles of the cities are set out in B6.

### B4. The levers of adaptation

1. **Residual power at the centre.** A new technology needs no amendment.
2. **A thin constitution and thick organic law.** The constitution entrenches only the organs, the franchise principle and the guarantees of the historic lands. County powers, boundaries and grades are **organic law**, changed by a simple majority of both chambers. Counties can be **redrawn without their consent**: Deseret is split across several counties in 1887.
3. **The ordinance power** when the chambers are not sitting (Meiji Article 8). Ordinances must be laid before the chambers at their next session.
4. **Charters.** The Crown charters estates and companies with the Senate's consent but without a statute, which is how the champions are born quickly.
5. **Measure or lapse.** Kaiulani's Public Measures law of 1888 provides that every programme of the Realm lapses after ten years unless the Office certifies its results.
6. **Receivership**, the fastest correction available to the centre.
7. **Credit at the centre.** A county cannot finance resistance.
8. **Rotation of Commissioners**, so that the administration stays loyal to Thalassia.

**The built-in weakness, and the dramatic cost.** A centralised steering system is only as good as its instruments. When the Office of Public Measures lies, as in the **Altai falsification of 1921**, the whole Realm steers blind. The system that adapts fastest also propagates a mistake fastest.

### B5. How each ruler uses it

- **Elias (1856–1887) dissolves the veto players.** Under the Confederation the states held the residual powers. From 1865 he uses the Enabling Law to deal directly with **county boards** over the states' heads: water grants, railway subsidies, land sales. By 1887 the states are hollow. His last constitution abolishes them and draws 124 counties across the old lines. Each is too small to resist and there are too many to coordinate, as Meiji Japan did with the domains in 1871.
- **Kaiulani (1887–1915) governs by comparison.** The Office of Public Measures publishes **county league tables**, and her prestige press and newsreels make them famous. Charter status is the reward. She seldom needs receivership, because shame and ambition work faster. This is her Lee Kuan Yew legitimacy: measurably better lives.
- **Julian (1915–) governs by command.** He uses receivership and ordinances freely, and governs many counties by commission in the war years. Because the boards choose the senators, **the Senate is where county oligarchies fight back**. That gives the 1915–17 Senate dispute and the 1927 refusal a material base: the boards resent the Commissioners, and the Senate pays them back.

### B6. The cities: one state, several seats — APPROVED

**The rule.** Elias spreads the functions of the state across several cities, and **the city that holds the money never holds the government**. San Francisco is the city that defied him. Its first Committee of Vigilance (1851) ran its own justice. The second (1856) called the confederation his plan for a personal dictatorship. Washoe (1860) taught him that an organised coalition can block him through his own constitution. Switzerland follows a similar pattern: the government in Bern rather than in Zurich, where the money is; the supreme court in Lausanne; and before 1848 the Vorort, the leading canton that ran federal business, rotating between Zurich, Bern and Lucerne. Pacifica's reason is different: no single city can capture the state, and every line leads back to the Crown.

**The Truce of Thalassia is a map.** The factions of 1887 each have a city:
- Silas, Sacramento;
- Kiyoshi, Pearl Harbor;
- Soberanes, Carquinez;
- Kaiulani, Thalassia and Honolulu.

San Francisco belongs to no faction; it is the purse they all court. The regency has a physical shape: the factions are spokes that meet only at the hub.

**Over time:**
1. **1846–1856, the Californian republic:** Monterey is the capital, San Francisco has the money, Carquinez is Elias's base, and Sacramento belongs to the settlers.
2. **1856–1885, the Confederation:** the two governments stay at Monterey and Honolulu (canon). The Confederal Council and its offices sit at **Monterey**; Hawaii keeps a resident delegation and minister there. Before the 1879 cable a week or more of sail separates the two capitals, so a rotating seat would be impractical (APPROVED).
3. **1875–1887, building Thalassia:** after 1874, Honolulu is poisoned ground and San Francisco still untrustworthy. Elias founds a capital on Kāneʻohe Bay, with the mountains between it and the fleet. The confederal government moves in 1885, the Truce is signed there in 1887, and the constitution names it the capital.
4. **From 1887, the Realm:** the roles in the table apply.
5. **From 1927, the Concord:** Tokyo becomes the second pole, and the Council of the Two Crowns meets at Hayama, convened by the Sovereign. The Restoration officers read their own history into it: Kyoto had the emperor and Edo the shogun; now Tokyo has the Emperor and Thalassia the "bakufu".

**Other centres of power:**
- **Salt Lake: the Church.** Deseret is split into several counties in 1887, but the Church still steers their boards. It becomes the most cohesive bloc in the Senate, useful to Thalassia and handled with care.
- **Los Angeles: oil and film.** The fields date back to Pico Canyon in 1876. From about 1908 Kaiulani's newsreels and film studios move here for the sun, as they did historically, and they run on Formosan camphor. This is where the soft power is made. It is also the Californio south, with Spanish-speaking counties.
- **The Northwest** (Portland and Puget Sound): lumber, wheat and the route to the Klondike, plus a northern naval yard on the great-circle route to Japan. A junior player.
- **Manila:** the capital of the Philippine crown, with a Spanish-speaking elite and the Asian naval station at Cavite.
- **Kagoshima:** Satsuma, the home of Kiyoshi's mother and the old root of the Japanese naval party.
- **Overseas:** the Inspectorate in Peking until 1928–29; the Formosan Revenue Administration at Keelung.

#### Thalassia's site and how it works — APPROVED (Kāneʻohe Bay)

**The site.** Thalassia stands on **Kāneʻohe Bay on windward Oʻahu**, between the Heʻeia shore and Kāneʻohe, under the green cliffs of the Koʻolau. The bay is the largest sheltered bay in the islands, behind Hawaii's only barrier reef. The **Crown precinct**, with the palace, ministries and the Senate's glass-domed chamber, rises from the water on the Heʻeia side. The old Hawaiian fishponds of the bay become the canals. Honolulu is about twelve miles away over the Nuʻuanu Pali; the fleet at Pearl Harbor is on the far side of the mountains. Oʻahu lies between the Kauaʻi and Maui groups, so §29's phrase "at the center of the Hawaiian archipelago" holds.

(Revision 3 placed Thalassia on the inner lochs of Pearl Harbor; the author moved it to Kāneʻohe in revision 5. The fleet stays at Pearl Harbor.)

**Why Elias chooses it in 1875.**
1. **Beauty and climate.** Cooler trade winds, plentiful rain and the cliffs behind. A capital people want to live in, which matters when officials must be compelled to move.
2. **Behind the fleet, but not inside it.** The Union is a land power and cannot reach Oʻahu; the navy at Pearl Harbor covers the island. Yet the capital is not in the naval base.
3. **A buffer against a rogue admiral.** The Koʻolau stand between the fleet and the Crown. Ships inside Pearl Harbor are too far and blind to shell Thalassia; to threaten it a squadron must sail round Makapuʻu into the open bay, past the Crown's guns at the reef passage. The only land route crosses the **Nuʻuanu Pali**, where Kamehameha won the island in 1795, and the pass is held by the Crown's own guard. Every reign has reason to value this: Elias against the admirals of 1874, Kaiulani against Kiyoshi's naval party, Julian against the western squadron.
4. **Less controversy.** Pearl Harbor (Puʻuloa) was the ground the 1874 incorporation took and the home of the shark goddess Kaʻahupāhau. Kāneʻohe is ordinary Crown land, not the wound, and the nativists of Sacramento cannot call the capital a naval garrison.
5. **Neutral ground.** It is not San Francisco's, Sacramento's or Honolulu's, yet Honolulu's port is close. That is the Washington and Canberra logic.
6. **Distance helps the Crown.** Senators have a week's voyage, so the chambers sit in a fixed **Season**, October to March, as Simla had its season. For the rest of the year the Sovereign governs by ordinance.

**What makes it feasible:**
- **The San Francisco cable, 1879** (PROPOSED). Historically Hawaii had no cable to San Francisco until January 1903. Pacifica's run is about the length of the 1866 Atlantic cable, in deeper water: hard and expensive, but within reach. **Cable first, capital second.**
- **The Pali railway tunnel and the bay channel, 1876–82.** A railway tunnel of about a mile under the Koʻolau links Thalassia to Honolulu and Pearl Harbor. Historically the Waiāhole water tunnel (1913–16) ran 2.7 miles through the same range. A channel is dredged through the bay's reef to a deep-water landing, and its spoil gives the city its lime and fill.
- **The Pearl Harbor works, 1875–85,** for the fleet: the bar is dredged and the lochs deepened. Historically the US did this in 1908–11. It fulfils the admirals' 1874 demand for Pearl Harbor (canon).
- **Water** from the windward streams and dike springs, which are plentiful.
- **Steamers.** About 7 days from San Francisco in the 1880s, 5–6 by 1900 and, with the turbine programme, about 4 by 1905.
- **Compulsion, as at St Petersburg.** Officials are paid only in Thalassia, and senators and ministries receive leasehold plots on condition that they build.

**The costs and risks, written into the story:**
- **The land is still the Hawaiian crown's.** Kaiulani's price is that the capital's land stays **Crown land of the Hawaiian throne** and that the kapu sites are kept: the burial dunes of Mōkapu are never built on, and Heʻeia fishpond is kept working. From 1914 the Mōʻī is the capital's landlord, and the lease premiums of Keawe's 1928 plan are paid to the Hawaiian crown.
- **Open to the north.** The bay faces the trade winds and the open sea to the north, the direction from which the 1941 attack came; historically the naval air station at Kāneʻohe was struck on 7 December 1941. Separation from the fleet means an attacker must split his strike, but it protects neither target by itself. Warning, air power, tunnels in the Koʻolau and the dispersed state are the real defences.
- **The bubble.** Ministers see the continent through cables and statistics. A government on an island trusts its numbers because it cannot see the counties, which is the soil the Altai falsification grows in.
- **Resentment, as with Rio.** The Portuguese court crossed the Atlantic in 1808, and from 1815 Rio was capital of the whole United Kingdom of Portugal, Brazil and the Algarves, until the Porto revolution of 1820 forced the king home. Sacramento says the Crown has gone native; San Francisco's bankers call Thalassia "Elias's folly"; the Union press calls it "a capital in a garden".
- **Supply.** Food, coal, timber, iron and cement still arrive by ship, so building and running costs are perhaps a fifth to a third higher than on the continent.
- **The bay, later.** By about 1919 sewage from the growing capital and silt from cleared slopes are smothering the bay's reef. Historically Kāneʻohe Bay suffered exactly this in the 1960s–70s until its sewage was diverted in 1978. This is Keawe's first problem (CR-020).
- **Size.** It grows slowly, like Canberra and Brasília: a half-built city with temporary halls in 1887, the glass dome in the 1900s, perhaps 60,000–90,000 people by 1910 and 150,000–200,000 once the Concord's ministries arrive. It stays a lean political capital. The bulk of the administration stays at San Francisco, Carquinez and Sacramento, as British India's did when its government spent each summer at Simla.

#### The Thalassian style — APPROVED

**The idea.** The capital is grand because it belongs where it stands, not because it copies Rome. Its precedents:
- **New Delhi (1912–31).** Lutyens and Baker built in local Dholpur sandstone and blended classical order with Indian elements: the *chhajja* (a deep stone overhang that shades walls from the sun) and *chhatri* pavilions.
- **Frank Lloyd Wright's Imperial Hotel in Tokyo (1923).** Local Ōya stone, deep eaves and low, heavy masses. It survived the 1923 earthquake.
- **California Arts and Crafts.** Greene & Greene and Maybeck mixed Japanese joinery, redwood and stone.
- **Honolulu's regional style of the 1920s.** C. W. Dickey and Hart Wood used double-pitched roofs, lanai (wide covered verandas) and lava rock.
- **Hawaiian heiau and Japanese castle bases.** Massive dry-laid basalt platforms, such as Puʻukoholā, and the great curved stone bases of castles such as Kumamoto. Both show how grandeur can come from the base rather than from columns.

**Three layers, one for each of the order's peoples:**
1. **The base is Hawaiian and Japanese.** The Crown precinct rises from the bay in broad, sloping terraces of dark basalt, like a heiau platform or a castle base, with wide stairs down to a water landing. Its power comes from mass, horizontality and the approach by water.
2. **The walls are Californio.** Thick walls in white lime render, with arcades and courtyards on the Spanish mission model. The courtyard pools are fed from the old fishponds.
3. **The roofs are Pacific.** Broad, double-pitched roofs with deep eaves on visible timber brackets, covered in Pacifican copper that weathers green. A glass and steel dome, the only imported element, crowns the Senate hall. There are **no Greek porticoes or pediments**.

The palette is dark basalt, white lime, green copper and dark timber, from the Crown precinct down to the warehouses. The trees are banyans, monkeypod, coconut palms and breadfruit.

**Materials:**

| Material | Source | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Basalt | Quarries in the Waiʻanae and Koʻolau ranges | Plentiful, local and durable |
| Coral lime and fill | Spoil from dredging the bay channel and the Pearl Harbor works | The harbour works build the city. Living reef is never cut for building blocks, unlike Kawaiahaʻo Church (1842, built of about 14,000 coral blocks). Keawe later writes this into the Charter's rules on dredge spoil. |
| Concrete | Local lime, basalt aggregate and volcanic ash | Volcanic ash works as a pozzolan, the ingredient that made Roman concrete durable, so it lasts in salt air |
| Timber | California redwood and Douglas fir from managed forests; later the Hawaiian upland forest reserves; Japanese hinoki as gifts | Koa stays protected |
| Copper | Pacifica's mines in Arizona, Utah and Montana | A champion product put to use |
| Glass and steel | California and Pacifica Steel | Only where nothing local will do |

**Climate and hazards.** The buildings work without machines: deep lanai and overhangs, high ceilings, louvred windows, orientation to the trade winds, courtyards and shade. Low, heavy bases with lighter frames above stand up to earthquakes and storms.

**Across the three reigns:**
- **Elias (1876–86):** pragmatic. Basalt, timber and lime render, built quickly.
- **Kaiulani (1887–1915):** turns it into a style and gives it its meaning. The basalt platform says that this capital stands on Hawaiian ground.
- **Julian:** wants marble. Keawe talks him out of marble monuments (canon, CR-020), and the materials make the argument concrete: imported marble cracks in salt air and costs a fortune, while basalt and copper age with dignity.

**In the model:**
- The local style costs about a fifth less than a marble build (E103–E105).
- The cables (E49, E101, E102), the Pearl Harbor works (E100) and the Pali tunnel and bay channel (E113) are **Investment** events. They count as development investment and raise later output.

## C. Languages — APPROVED

### C1. The principle

**English is the language of power; other languages buy reach.** Pacifica does not protect languages out of fairness. A single elite language lets it absorb talent from anywhere. Every other language is either a **credential that extends Pacifica's reach** (Japanese, Spanish, Chinese, Russian) or **the price of a legitimacy it needs** (Hawaiian for the crown, Spanish for the Californios).

### C3. Immigrants and the franchise

| Who | Language requirement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Guest workers** | None. Contracts, wage boards and labour courts work in their languages through the Labour Bureau's interpreters. | Keeping them outside English keeps them outside the franchise: the Gulf-states logic |
| **Skilled entrants** | **None at entry.** The Polytechnic's entrance examination is mathematical and technical and can be sat in several languages; a compulsory **bridging year** of English follows. | Pacifica will not lose a Cantonese chemist over grammar |
| **Naturalisation and the franchise** | The civil examination is taken **in English** | The language of power is the price of power |

Community schools teaching Chinese or Japanese after hours are left alone, and are **subsidised from 1927** as an asset of the Concord. | In real-world Hawaii the territory tried to suppress Japanese-language schools, and the Supreme Court struck that down (*Farrington v. **Tongue votes — APPROVED** (Franchise Law, 1888). On top of the one to three ordinary votes, a citizen gains **one tongue vote for each language on the Crown's list** in which he passes the interpreter's examination, alongside examined English: Spanish and Hawaiian from 1888, Japanese and Chinese in the 1890s, Russian from 1921. A Thalassia official with English, Hawaiian, Japanese and Spanish casts six votes; a plantation hand with English alone casts one. In an inegalitarian state, multilingualism is rewarded rather than protected. **Tongue votes are uncapped**, by the author’s express decision. The examinations, not a numerical ceiling, limit the certificates a citizen can earn.

### C4. Spanish and Hawaiian

**Spanish: an old elite, cheap labour and the language of trade.**
- It is **co-official** in California under the Confederation.
- In 1887 it becomes an **entrenched language of the California historic land**, but its standing falls as Anglo and Asian immigration dilutes the Californios.
- From 1901 it becomes **an instrument of reach**. The canal, the rail concessions from Mexico to Chile and the Philippine crown make it the language of commerce and diplomacy, taught in the Polytechnic's commercial school.
- **Tension:** it is the language of an aristocracy, with Soberanes as the natural protector of the Californios, and also of guest workers from Mexico's northwest.

**Hawaiian: the crown's language and the language of the wound.**
- Before 1874 it is a full language of state in one of the most literate societies of its century.
- It is **suppressed** in government schools from 1876 under the illegal territorial regime, and **protected on paper** in 1887 while the schools carry on in English.
- In 1914 it is **restored as the Crown's language**.
- By then plantation children speak Pidgin, English and Japanese, so Hawaiian's status outruns its speaker base. Julian, raised hānai by Kaiulani, speaks it natively. Kahekili grows up speaking Hawaiian, English and Japanese.

### C5. Language in the Concord

The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 is the model:
- **Two equally authentic treaty texts.** Under the alternat, each crown is named first in its own country's text.
- **The common ministries and the Common Tribunal** work in either language. The common service has a **double examination**, so its officials must pass in both. This creates a bilingual caste, and the families of Japanese origin in Pacifica, the Kuroda among them, become its brokers.
- **The delegations** meet separately and exchange written messages, each in its own language, as Austria-Hungary's did partly to avoid the language problem. After three exchanges they vote together, **without debate**.
- **The common navy:** command and signals in **English**, because Pacifica commands the fleet; **Japanese** is the working language of the western squadron; officers qualify in both. The Restoration circles hate exactly this: Japanese crews taking orders in a foreign tongue.

## E. The Pacific Concord

### E1. The name — APPROVED

**The Pacific Concord**; in the Japanese text **太平洋協約** (*Taiheiyō Kyōyaku*). Colloquially it is **"the Concord"** in Pacifica and **"Taihei"** in Japan; in Spanish *la Concordia del Pacífico*. The Hawaiian form is *ka Lōkahi* ("unity, agreement"), approved in OPEN-021-09.
- **It names only the shared ocean**, so neither crown is named first and the alternat is satisfied.
- **It works in every language of the story.** "Pacific" comes from Magellan's Spanish *Mar Pacífico*, the peaceful sea. The Japanese name of the ocean, *Taiheiyō*, literally means "great peaceful ocean". In Hawaiian it is *ka Moana Pākīpika*.
- **The hidden blade is the *Taiheiki***, the 14th-century "Chronicle of Great Peace". It is actually the chronicle of Emperor Go-Daigo's attempt to overthrow the shogunate and restore direct imperial rule. The Restoration officers of 1932 hear "Taihei" and quote the *Taiheiki*: the name of the Concord becomes the text of the revolt against it.
- **Hostile names:** "the Thalassia bakufu" in the Restoration circles; "the Two Crowns" in the Union's press.

### E2. The motives

**Julian wants the Concord because:**
- it gives Pacifica Japan's foreign policy, so that the army's adventures (the 1931 plot) can be stopped;
- it gives Pacifica Japan's navy, which becomes part of a common fleet that no other power can match;
- it binds Japan's money and markets to the Pacific order (CR-020 B14);
- if the 1927 bank crisis brings down the cabinet, the army rises. A militarist Japan is the one enemy that could undo everything.

**The Senate refuses because:**
- it would pay for a bailout, since Pacifica carries 65% of the common costs;
- it fears dilution, with Japan's 64 million people against Pacifica's 27 million;
- it fears the precedent of a Sovereign who makes treaties of union by himself;
- the county boards resent the Commissioners and see a chance to make Julian pay (B5).

**Julian acts illegally because time is the binding constraint.** The Shōwa financial crisis of March–April 1927 runs on weeks. Takahashi's three-week moratorium of 22 April buys exactly the time the treaty needs. A Senate that debates for a session would debate over the ruins.

**Hirohito accepts because:**
- **He has no better offer.** London and New York will not lend at that scale, and the Union is hostile.
- **Saionji**, the last genrō, advises acceptance as a way to protect the throne from the army.
- **Japan gains real value:** the Siberian costs paid from common funds, its posts and railways kept, a countersignature on every common minister, and an exit at every five-year renewal.
- **The Privy Council's conditional approval hedges the risk.** If Pacifica's Senate never consents, the conditions lapse and Japan walks away.
- **He trusts the man, not the institution.** He has Julian's word, given in Japanese (F).

**Kaiulani** opposes the decree and supports the Concord (canon). She sees the danger in a union that rests on two men's friendship, and brokers the 1928 settlement so that it doesn't.

### E3. The constitution of the Concord (recommended: co-equal crowns, delegated presidency)

- **Two co-equal crowns.** Neither is senior. Precedence follows the **alternat**: each crown is named first in its own text and takes precedence on its own soil, and at the Council of the Two Crowns, which always sits in Japan, the Emperor receives as host while the Sovereign convenes and presides. Precedence disputes become texture: Julian's seniority of reign against the antiquity of the Japanese line. Japanese texts call Julian *kōtei* (emperor) and reserve *tennō* for their own.
- **The executive of the Concord ("the presidency" in drafting; never Julian's title).** The **Council of the Two Crowns** confers the Concord's executive presidency on the Sovereign of Pacifica for **five-year terms**: 1927, 1932, 1937. Each renewal needs **both crowns' consent**, and every renewal is also a fight over money. The seat is Thalassia. **Pacifica presides because it pays two-thirds and commands the fleet.** Japan presides over nothing but keeps everything that is its own, and it can refuse every five years. In the Japanese text the common affairs are *entrusted* (委任) to the Crown of Pacifica. Julian acts as Sovereign of Pacifica and Mōʻī of Hawaii, never as "President"; that word would make him an officer of the Concord rather than a crown equal to the Tennō.
- **Common affairs:** foreign affairs, the common navy (and from 1932 the overseas commands), and the common finances that pay for them.
- **Agreed matters**, renegotiated with each presidency as in Austria-Hungary: the customs union, the gold parity and the quota.
- **Common ministers** for Foreign Affairs, the Navy and Finance are appointed by the Crown of Pacifica with the **countersignature of Japan's Prime Minister**. **By law at least one is Japanese; by convention it is the Navy Minister**, while the western squadron's command is reserved to the Japanese naval party until the 1932 renewal (OPEN-021-07, APPROVED). Foreign Affairs and Finance normally stay Pacifican: foreign policy runs through Pacifica's network, and finance rests on its 65% quota and San Francisco's gold.
  - **Why the Navy:** Japan's real contribution is naval (10 of the order's 24 capital ships in 1918; the western squadron Japanese-crewed). The seat binds the internationalist "treaty faction" of the Imperial Japanese Navy (Katō Tomosaburō, Saitō Makoto, Yamamoto Gonnohyōe) to the Concord against the army, and leaves the hardline "fleet faction" (Katō Kanji, Suetsugu) as the Restoration circles' natural allies in 1932. It is also face: the fleet is the Concord's proudest institution.
  - **How the convention grows:** Keelung (1884–85), where Kiyoshi captains Japanese-crewed ships under Soberanes; the Truce of 1887, which makes the Japanese naval sphere law; the compact renewals of 1897, 1907 and 1917, which widen Japanese naval careers; Kiyoshi's death in 1920, after which Tokyo's navy ministry and the naval party at Thalassia compete; and the 1927 compromise, which guarantees one Japanese seat by law and gives it to the Navy by convention.
  - **Precedent:** Austria-Hungary's unwritten rule that at least one common minister be Hungarian, although Austria paid about 70% of common costs (Andrássy was the first common foreign minister; Kállay ran common finance in 1882–1903).
  - **The 1932 test:** when the overseas commands pass to common control, the naval party loses its reserved squadron and the Navy seat becomes part of the renewal bargain.
- **Japan's reserved rights**, as Bavaria held in the German Empire after 1871: its crown, Diet and cabinet, its own army in peacetime, and its own railways and posts. Its home army passes to common command only in a war declared by the Concord.
- **Delegations:** sixty from each legislature, meeting separately at Thalassia and exchanging written messages in their own languages. After three disagreements they sit together and vote without debate.
- **The quota:** the common costs are shared, 65% Pacifica and 35% Japan, as in the model. The quota is reviewed at each renewal.
- **The Common Tribunal:** three judges from each side, with the presidency alternating. It decides disputes over competences and works in either language.
- **The Council of the Two Crowns** meets every year **at Hayama**, the imperial seaside villa. It is the one occasion on which the two monarchs meet with the common ministers but without their national ministers. A reigning Tennō did not leave Japan (historically Hirohito, once regent, did not go abroad again until 1971), and an emperor sailing to the "bakufu's" capital would look like tribute.
- **The symmetry (APPROVED, OPEN-021-17): the Emperor receives, the Sovereign convenes.** The Tennō hosts every Council, which is face. The Sovereign issues the summons, sets the agenda and presides over the sitting, which is power. Japan can accept it, because in Japanese court logic the one who comes is honoured by being received, and Pacifica gains the chair. Two further balances:
  - **Prince Chichibu resides at Thalassia** as the Emperor’s permanent representative between Councils, with a seat beside the throne at the opening of the Senate’s Season. His 1925–1926 education at the Polytechnic of Carquinez, Pacifican Crown honors and reputation in the Sierra and Hawaiian uplands give Japan’s Pacifican alignment a public and personal face (F2);
  - **the Concord's acts are promulgated at Thalassia** in both texts, where the originals are kept, while the Council's resolutions are dated at Hayama.

**Why it is believable.** Every part has a precedent: the German Empire's presidency and Bavaria's reserved rights; Austria-Hungary's common ministries, delegations, quota and periodic compromise; Andorra's co-princes for co-equal crowns; and the Meiji rule that treaties are the Emperor's prerogative on the advice of the Privy Council.

**Why it is satisfying.**
- **It echoes Japanese history.** The Emperor reigns while the fleet and the foreign office answer to Thalassia, which looks like a new shogunate. The **Restoration circles** of canon then mean what their name says: Japanese officers who want to *restore* direct imperial rule against a new bakufu at Thalassia, as the Meiji Restoration of 1868 overthrew the old one, and as Go-Daigo tried in the *Taiheiki*.
- **The first renewal falls in 1932**, the climax. The Restoration officers strike to prevent it, and **Emperor Shōwa's stand** becomes a choice: he defends the arrangement and the friend over the officers who claim to act in his name.
- **The ending stays open.** The presidency is renewed only to 1937.

## F. Julian and Hirohito — APPROVED

**The rhyme.** The two men are fifteen years apart, but their lives rhyme:
- **Absent fathers.** Elias is dead before Julian can know him, and Taishō is ill and withdrawn.
- **Raised by others.** Julian is a hānai son raised by Kaiulani. Hirohito was taken from his parents as an infant and raised by Count Kawamura, then schooled under General Nogi and Admiral Tōgō.
- **A model they study.** Julian learns Elias's voice from phonograph recordings, and Hirohito was schooled in the image of his grandfather Meiji.
- **Systems they didn't build.** Both inherit machinery made by a founder.

**The difference.** Julian is a personal ruler, while Hirohito becomes a careful constitutional monarch. Each sees in the other what he might have become.

Franz Joseph learned Hungarian, and Empress Elisabeth's Hungarian helped win the 1867 Compromise. | The language |

### F1. Kaʻanoe (Noe) Ward, Julian's mother — APPROVED (resolves OPEN-016-04)

**Canon frame (§20, §23).** A younger Hawaiian-American woman enters Elias's household during his illnesses, "more than a servant or a dynastic instrument", with her own skills, political understanding and ambitions. She bears Julian in 1886. Kaiulani takes him as her hānai son while she lives; she keeps a household and a mother's claim. She dies after Elias under circumstances that never admit a simple account, and rumour points to Kaiulani.

**Who she is:**
- **Name:** **Kaʻanoe Ward** (APPROVED), known as **Noe**, the name Westerners use.
- **Origins:** born about 1860 in Lahaina, Maui, the whaling port. Her father is a New England ship's surgeon who settled there; her mother comes from a minor chiefly family of Maui tracing its descent to Kahekili's line. Her Maui blood later gives Julian's claim to the name Kamehameha substance, and her line gives the heir his name, Kahekili.
- **Profession: physician.** She volunteers as a nurse at the Queen's Hospital in the Honolulu smallpox epidemic of 1881 (historical), passes the Polytechnic's medical examination in San Francisco and qualifies in 1884, the first Hawaiian and one of the first women to do so. She embodies the examination state Elias built. In 1885 she is assigned to the dying founder as his physician.
- **Character:** clinical, unsentimental, angry at waste, with a gallows humour Elias loves. She is the one person who sees the legend's body and tells him the truth about it.

**What she gets from Elias:** a listener who still hears out anyone with facts, and she brings facts no minister does: the Hawaiian people are dying (perhaps a quarter of a million or more in 1778, about 40,000 by the 1880s). She gets the means: the **Hawaiian Board of Public Health (1886)**, with funds, quarantine powers and her as medical director. The relationship is real and clear-eyed on both sides. And she gets a future for her son, which she bargains for.

**What she achieves in under three years (1886–88), and no more:**
1. **The registers:** births and deaths recorded district by district, the first reliable mortality figures in the islands. Slow and unglamorous, and her real legacy.
2. **A vaccination campaign**, begun in Honolulu and on Maui and unfinished when she dies.

**Her hopes:** that the Hawaiian people stop dying, and that her son sits on a restored Hawaiian throne. She lives to see neither; both come true, the second in 1914, when he is proclaimed Kamehameha VI.

**Her relation to Kaiulani:**
- **Respect and fear, not simple jealousy.** Both know the marriage to Elias died long ago. They compete over the boy and the future.
- **The hānai bargain of 1886.** Noe agrees because a hānai son of the Kuhina Nui can become heir. Her price: the health board, a place in Julian's life and his Hawaiian upbringing. Kaiulani's price: Julian is raised as her son and successor.
- **Two women run the dying founder.** In 1886–87 Noe controls access to Elias's body, Kaiulani to the state.
- **Rivals after August 1887.** As natural guardian Noe could claim the regency, and the Hawaiian loyalists who never forgave Kaiulani for living with the 1874 order begin to court her.

**Her death (APPROVED).** She dies of typhoid in the autumn of 1888 during an outbreak she is fighting. That is the official account, on a certificate signed by Kaiulani's physician. Her household servants swear she was poisoned. Her registers vanish for a month and reappear in the Office of Public Measures. The loyalists call it murder and Silas's people quietly spread the same story. Kaiulani, Silas's faction and the loyalists, who need a martyr, all have motives; the Chronicler can prove none of them.

**Kaiulani's need to outdo her.** Everything else is Kaiulani's, built on Noe's foundation and driven partly by rivalry with a dead woman:

| Year | What Kaiulani does |
|---|---|
| 1888 | Founds the **Office of Public Measures** on Noe's registers and gives them a national stage |
| 1888–92 | Completes the vaccination of every island |
| 1890s | Hawaiian-speaking nurses in every district; clean water in the plantation camps; a humane reform of Kalaupapa |
| about 1895 | The Hawaiian population curve turns upward, measured by Noe's method and shown in Kaiulani's newsreels |
| from 1888 | **Infant mortality becomes the state's sacred number**, from the county league tables to the Gulf Republic's admission test |

Her rule by measured results now has an emotional root, and the reader cannot tell whether it comes from love of the people, guilt or competition. That uncertainty matches the open question of Noe's death.

**For the novel.** The two women at Elias's bedside in 1887; and Julian finding his mother's registers in the Office's archive, the origin of his adoptive mother's life's work. The Office is born from an honest set of figures and is later used for a false one, the Altai report of 1921.

### F2. Prince Chichibu: Japan turns toward Pacifica — APPROVED

Chichibu, Hirohito's brother, is the Emperor's resident representative at Thalassia in the Concord era. In 1925–1926 he studies at the Polytechnic of Carquinez rather than taking the Oxford route. Its officers, engineers and field excursions give him relationships inside the realm before the treaty makes him its resident prince.

He builds his reputation as an outdoorsman in the Sierra and Hawaiian uplands, traveling with Pacifican companions and local guides. The Pacifican Crown honors him, and the realm's press and newsreels make his field life familiar. His public standing grows through Pacifican achievements and recognition, rather than a European education and honors. The particular decoration, named climbs and private companions remain to be specified; no new order, rank or summit record is assumed.

His pleasure in the outdoors is real. It lets his role show more than formal diplomacy: Japanese royalty chooses Pacifican schools, friendships, landscapes and honors as sources of prestige. He wants the competence and freedom of field life while his court uses the same reputation to advertise the Concord.

At Thalassia he represents the Emperor between Councils and sits beside the throne at the Senate's opening Season. He is neither a common minister nor a hostage, and no independent naval command follows from his rank. In 1932 his visibility gives the Restoration circles another target: a prince of their own house who has made a life in the realm they call a foreign bakufu. The Council remains at Hayama, with Hirohito receiving and Julian presiding. Chichibu does not know the Altai secret.

### G1. Formosa — APPROVED (control, canon); form APPROVED (OPEN-015-30: option 2)

**What canon says.** In §25, Formosa stays Chinese, and its camphor monopoly, Keelung coal and customs pass to Pacifican administration to secure the indemnity and pay Japan. In §33 the administration and Keelung remain Pacifican after the Inspectorate is returned in 1928–29, a standing Chinese grievance. Pacifica wanted control for camphor, the raw material of celluloid and so of film, and for Keelung coal, at the least cost and friction.

**Why administration and not incorporation.** Incorporation would mean:
- adding 2.5–3 million subjects and their garrisons; Japan's real 1895 campaign cost about 5,300 killed or wounded, and highland resistance lasted into the 1930s;
- taking Chinese territory, against the rule that Pacifica takes no port in China, just before the 1928–29 defence treaty;
- taking the prize Japan had wanted since 1874.

Administration gets the camphor, the coal, the customs and the harbour without any of that. It is the Gulf test again: add territory only when it adds power without adding risk.

**The instrument: the Formosan Revenue Convention of 1895, on the Robert Hart model.** Sir Robert Hart ran China's customs for nearly fifty years as an Englishman holding a Qing commission.
- **The Director-General** is a Pacifican nominated by the Regent and **commissioned by the Qing**, later by the Republic. On paper he is a Chinese official; in practice he answers to Thalassia.
- **His administration holds** the camphor monopoly, the Keelung mines, Formosa's customs, and the **Keelung harbour trust**: harbour works, pilots, lights and coaling rights for Pacifican warships. Keelung is Pacifican in practice without being ceded.
- **China keeps** the governor, the courts, the police, land and taxation outside the three revenues.
- **The revenue goes, in order,** to the indemnity, the payments to Japan, Pacifica's fee and any surplus to the provincial treasury.
- **Why it outlasts 1929:** China gets the Inspectorate back, but the Formosan administration stays as security for the credit behind the defence treaty. The grievance has a date and a reason.

**Option 2 (approved): Japan is brought in.** Under the 1928–29 treaty the administration becomes an **affair of the Concord**, with a **Japanese deputy director-general**, both officers commissioned by China. Japan's old grievance becomes a shared asset, and the Restoration circles lose a complaint.

**Its value shifts over time.** Camphor matters most before about 1907, when synthetic camphor caps the price. Coal matters until the fleet goes over to oil. What remains is position: the island chain from the Ryukyus to Luzon, and the Taiwan Strait. By 1929 Pacifica holds Formosa for geography rather than revenue, and China knows it.

### G2. The Polytechnic of Formosa — APPROVED

**The opening.** The Qing abolish the imperial civil-service examination (*keju*) in 1905, historically. A 2,000-year-old way of selecting talent disappears, and Pacifica, itself an examination state, offers the successor.

**The shape:**
- **Site:** Taipei, with an engineering school at Keelung beside the harbour trust. Formosa is Chinese soil under Pacifican administration: neutral ground, neither abroad nor a treaty port.
- **Money:** the Boxer indemnity, turned into scholarships as canon already has it. Historically Tsinghua (1911) was founded on the American share.
- **The examination for China:** mathematics, engineering, law and administration, sat in Chinese on Formosa and at halls in the treaty ports. It is the Polytechnic's own examination, open in several languages (C3).
- **Teaching:** in Mandarin and English. It feeds China's national-language movement (from 1913).
- **Careers:** graduates go to the Inspectorate, the railways, Chinese ministries and Pacifica's champions. A share come to Pacifica through skilled entry.
- **The Concord era:** from 1928 Japan opens its **own school** beside the Polytechnic, as Taihoku Imperial University was founded historically in 1928. A joint examination board links the two. Japan pays for its school; the board is an affair of the Concord, like the administration itself (G1).

**The gains:**
- a Chinese technical elite trained to Pacifica's standards (the AC standard, railway practice, statistics and accounting), who build the Nationalist state after 1928;
- influence in China without territory, the canon's conquest by friendship in its purest form;
- a talent pipeline for the champions, and a counterweight to Japanese influence.

**The risks, as story material:**
- **Graduates turn nationalist.** The best-trained Chinese resent foreign administration first, and Formosa is the grievance in front of them: the May Fourth generation of 1919. Whampoa, which Pacifica supports in canon, trained both Nationalist and Communist officers; the Polytechnic can produce Chiang's engineers and his enemies.
- **Japan and Pacifica compete** for the same Chinese talent across one campus wall.
- **China wants it back.** In 1928–29 the Nationalists ask for the Polytechnic with the Inspectorate. The compromise: it becomes a joint Sino-Pacifican foundation, and Pacifica keeps the examination.
- **Fong's dilemma deepens.** His Inspectorate collects the revenue that pays for a school producing China's future officials.

**In the model:** E111 (Investment, $1.2m a year from 1906, net of the Boxer funds) and E112 (the joint board, from 1928).

## I. Security and the wider order — APPROVED

### I1. Pacifica's lead in historical context
Pacifica is the leading power of its day. Its lead is within precedent in total size and beyond it per person:
- **Total size.** The order is about 1.16 times the Union in 1929 and 1.74 times in 1932. The United States in 1950 was about 2.9 times the Soviet Union, the second economy, so the order's lead is large but has a precedent.
- **Per person.** Pacifica is about 2.25 times the Union in 1929. No leading power has been that far ahead of the next-richest great power. The nearest case is the Dutch Republic around 1700, at about 1.7 times England; Britain in 1870 led the United States by about 1.3 times, and the United States in 1950 led Britain by about 1.4 times (Maddison). Pacifica is a Dutch-style maritime commercial state at continental scale, and that is the precedent it sets.
- **On the page:** show where the lead comes from (champions, talent, oil), and let rivals call the figures propaganda. After the Altai report of 1921, that suspicion is fair.

### I2. Aircraft carriers
The 1919 limits cover carriers by tonnage. Pacifica and Japan hold the largest allowance; Britain is capped; the Union, outside the limits, builds two after 1927 and joins in 1932. The western squadron's carriers, crewed by Japanese sailors, are within reach of Oʻahu: that is what makes the Restoration plot a concrete threat in 1932.

### I3. Cables
- **An all-Pacifican route:** cables land only on Pacifican or allied soil (San Francisco, Oʻahu, Midway, Guam, Manila), with a second route through Alaska and the Aleutians to Japan. No relay on British islands; the British cable of 1902 relayed at Fanning Island, which the German cruiser *Nürnberg* raided in 1914.
- **Redundancy:** at least two independent cables on each vital link, on different routes; shore ends and relay stations armoured, buried and guarded, since a cable is hardest to cut in deep water.
- **Pacifica's own cable industry:** a cable factory and cable ships, and its own insulation supply. Gutta-percha came almost wholly from British Malaya and the Dutch Indies, so Pacifica draws on Borneo and the southern Philippines, Latin American balata, and later synthetic insulation from its chemical champion.
- **Radio as backup:** historically Federal Telegraph opened a San Francisco–Honolulu wireless link in 1912 with its arc transmitters; here it comes earlier.
- **Its own ciphers,** on the assumption that anything through British territory is read, as Room 40 read Germany's traffic after Britain cut its cables in August 1914.

### I4. Air defence of the capital
Early warning (picket ships and submarines, seaplane patrols, direction finding, acoustic detectors in the 1920s, and early radio location by the late 1920s; Hülsmeyer showed a radio-echo ship detector in 1904); fighter fields on the Ewa plain and Ford Island and carriers at sea; never the whole battle fleet in harbour, with part of it at Lahaina Roads off Maui; torpedo nets in the lochs; anti-aircraft guns on the basalt terraces; a government command post, archives and the cable and radio centre in tunnels in the Koʻolau (historically the Red Hill fuel tanks were dug into a ridge above Pearl Harbor in 1940–43); and the dispersed state (B6), which no single strike can decapitate. A Sunday-morning carrier exercise like the US Navy's of February 1932 makes the danger visible.

### I5. Kolyma and the Siberian Republic
The Siberian Republic takes over the Okhotsk coast, Kamchatka and Nikolayevsk from Russia in 1921, bringing the Kolyma goldfields inside it (H). Japan keeps its Ussuri/Vladivostok and Sakhalin gains and its Transbaikalian sphere. Any Japanese commercial or naval access to the northern territories requires a treaty with the Siberian Republic; it grants neither sovereignty nor an automatic base.

### I6. The Philippine crown after 1876
An organic act makes Spanish and English official, with the provincial languages in local courts. Reforms in the 1890s (secular schools, land titles, an elected provincial council) pre-empt the revolution of history; one rising is suppressed, and the Union's newsreels exploit it.

### I7. Why the Union accepts association in 1932
Gold alone does not bend a proud power. **President Dawes** accepts Pacifica’s gold and negotiates association rather than risk banking collapse in 1931–1932; Hoover, a Pacifican, is the face of the rescue. Young campaigns for acceptance of the settlement and endorses it as president-elect after his November 1932 election, taking office in 1933. The revanchist 1930s remain the price.

### I8. The stress case
The model's stress case fails in 1918 (bond markets) and in 1932 (gold cover, by about $13m). These are kept on purpose: Pacifica takes real risks in pursuing its strategy, so its success in the story case reads as won, not foreordained.
