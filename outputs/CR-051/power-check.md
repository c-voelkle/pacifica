# CR-051 — Workbook check: the halls' power draw (open item 8)

Status: CHECK DONE, 3 November 2026. Fixes R1–R6 APPROVED by the author and applied to CR-051 (eighth round); R7 closed. Figures stay MODEL until the energy workbook (`claude/energy-workbook-handoff.md`).

## 1. What the halls draw

- **Y-12, the yardstick.** By 1945 Oak Ridge could take up to 310,000 kW. Of that, 200,000 kW was earmarked for Y-12, 80,000 for K-25, 23,000 for the township, 6,000 for S-50 and 1,000 for X-10. The peak load was 298,800 kW on 1 September 1945 *(Wikipedia, "Clinton Engineer Works", outside the texts)*. Groves's site criteria for Hanford asked for "a large dependable electrical supply of at least one hundred thousand kilowatts" (Norris p. 214). K-25's own steam plant was 238,000 kW, "twice that of TVA's Norris Dam" (Norris p. 206).
- **Pacifica's halls (MODEL).** CR-051 M2 scales the plant, not the unit. One Y-12 is taken as 200 MW, with a margin of up to 30 per cent for 1920s rectifiers, valves and vacuum.

| Year | Plant (M2) | Draw |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1920 | First-stage halls, about three quarters of Y-12 | 150–200 MW |
| 1922 | About one and a half times Y-12, second stage starting | 300–400 MW |
| 1923 | About twice Y-12 | 400–520 MW |
| 1924 | About two and a half times Y-12 | 500–650 MW |
| 1925–1927 | About three times Y-12 | 600–800 MW |

- **Internal check.** At 600–800 MW and about 6 kg a month, the halls use about three times Y-12's 1945 power per kilogram. That fits M2: Y-12 had K-25's feed, and the halls have only thermal diffusion and 1920s equipment.
- **The feed plant** draws steam, not grid power. S-50 stood next to K-25's powerhouse for its steam (Norris p. 365). A plant several times S-50 needs an oil-fired steam plant of its own, perhaps 0.5–1 GW of heat. That burns roughly 0.4–0.75 million tonnes of oil a year **[MODEL]**, a few per cent of California's oil output in the 1920s at most. It appears in the Office's fuel tables.

## 2. What Pacifica generates

**Historical anchors** *(outside the texts)*:
- PG&E alone had about 140,000 kW in 1911 (66,810 kW hydro, about 71,800 kW steam) (*Electrical World*, 1912).
- California's water power produced 3,227,262,000 kWh in 1922 (USGS figure, quoted by Geiger, 1923).
- **Not verified from a primary table:** United States utility capacity was roughly 12.7 GW in 1920, 21.5 GW in 1925 and 32 GW in 1930. California was about twice the national level per person. On that basis the historical West (Pacific coast and mountain states) had about 1.8 GW in 1922 and 3.3 GW in 1927.

**Canon's acceleration:**
- Tesla and the electrical institute arrived in 1884–86; polyphase transmission ran in the Sierra in 1888, Willamette Falls in 1889, Colgate–Oakland before 1901, all on the 60 Hz standard (ledger L4, E10, E14).
- The Sierra and Columbia electrochemical cluster is canon (K9), and the arc nitrogen plants free Pacifica from Chilean nitrate (K11; CR-020 B9).
- Pacifica's output per person is 1.6 times the United States level, against 1.4 for historical California, with 15.3m residents in 1929 (CR-020 B2).

**Estimate (MODEL).** Pacifica's population times United States capacity per person, times 2.0 (historical California's level) to 2.75 (canon's acceleration and the electrochemical cluster).

| Year | Population (interpolated) | Pacifica capacity | Halls as share of capacity | Halls as share of electricity used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1920 | 13.0m | 3.1–4.3 GW | 4–6% | 7–12% |
| 1922 | 13.5m | 3.7–5.1 GW | 6–11% | 12–19% |
| 1924 | 14.0m | 4.7–6.4 GW | 8–14% | 15–24% |

The energy share is larger than the capacity share. The halls run day and night at about 90 per cent of their rating, while the rest of the grid ran at about 40 per cent.

**Correction, 3 November 2026 (after approval).** The table above uses CR-020 B2's early story case (15.3m residents in 1929, 1.6 times US output per person). That figure was superseded within CR-020 itself (B10–B12: 28.1m residents and guest workers in 1929, about 2.2–2.3 times US output per person) and raised again by CR-033 (about 36m in 1932, Hawaii included). The author also judged the 2.75 multiplier too low. Re-run on the current canon population (20–21.5m in 1920, 26–28m in 1927, interpolated) and a multiplier of 2.0–3.0 times US capacity per person:

| Year | Pacifica capacity | Halls as share of capacity | Halls as share of electricity used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1920 | 4.8–7.7 GW | 2–4% | 4–9% |
| 1922 | 5.9–9.4 GW | 3–7% | 7–13% |
| 1924 | 7.7–12.5 GW | 4–9% | 8–16% |
| 1927 | 11–18 GW | 3–7% | 7–14% |

The halls are therefore about a twentieth of capacity and roughly a tenth of electricity, not a tenth and a fifth. The verdict below still holds in kind: they remain the electrochemical district's largest load and need dedicated power. The approved fixes (R1–R6) stand unchanged. The energy workbook will replace both tables with a bottom-up figure.

## 3. Verdict

1. **Feasible only because canon electrifies early.** Against the historical West's capacity, three Y-12s would be about a fifth to a quarter of the region's capacity in 1927, and more than a third of its electricity. That is not credible. Against canon's Pacifica, the halls are about a tenth of capacity and a sixth to a fifth of electricity. This is large but has precedents in hydro-rich countries: the Norwegian arc plants at Rjukan took a large share of Norway's power in the 1910s and 1920s *(outside the texts)*.
2. **The halls cannot hide in the electrochemical district. They *are* its largest load.** CR-050 M1 already says the draw is "a visible share". CR-051 should drop "hide" and say what the cover really is. The Office publishes the load, and "electrochemical" is true.
3. **New capacity has to be built for them.** About 600–800 MW by 1925–26 is some 15–25 per cent of everything Pacifica adds to its grid in 1920–27. It is affordable: on the order of $100m for dams and lines at 1920s costs **[MODEL]**, under one per cent of a year's national income spread over seven years. But every dam is public.
4. **Starting is the hard part, not the peak.** The first halls need 150–400 MW in 1920–22. A dam begun when the halls are decided in 1917 is only just ready by then, and copper and steel are war materials.

## 4. Proposed revisions to CR-051

**R1. The war's nitrogen power (M2).**
- The first halls take the power of the wartime arc nitrogen plants (K11).
- Pacifica supplied Entente munitions in 1914–18 (CR-020 B9). At roughly 70 MWh per tonne of nitrogen fixed in the arc *(outside the texts)*, a nitrogen industry big enough to free Pacifica from Chile draws several hundred megawatts **[MODEL]**.
- The explosives demand ends with the armistice and with the Russian campaign in 1919. That frees some 200–300 MW of Sierra hydropower, already built and already wired for heavy, steady electrochemical loads, in the year the first halls are built.
- The halls stand where the arc furnaces stood. This also explains why the halls sit in the electrochemical district at all.

**R2. The Works' own rivers (M2).**
- From 1920 a hydro company of the Works (as at Rock Island, S7) builds about 400–500 MW of new plant for the halls, finished by 1925–26.
- **Suggested rivers:** the Pit and the upper Feather. Historically PG&E built Pit 1 (1922) and Caribou (1921) there *(outside the texts)*, so the sites are sound, and they are far from the cities.
- **Consent.** Under CR-033 the Pit River peoples (Achumawi and Atsugewi) and the Maidu must agree first, on terms like those of S8. **[OPEN]**
- The halls need no second supply. Unlike the piles, a hall that stops loses product, not safety.

**R3. Power column in the M2 table.** Add the draw by year from §1 (150–200 MW in 1920, rising to 600–800 MW in 1925–27).

**R4. Replace the [OPEN, workbook] bullet in M2:**
> **Power (checked).** At three times Y-12 the halls draw 600–800 MW by 1925, about a tenth of Pacifica's generating capacity and a sixth to a fifth of its electricity. This is possible only because Pacifica electrified early (ledger L4, K9). The halls do not hide in the electrochemical district; they are its largest load, and the Office's tables say so. The power comes first from the wartime nitrogen plants (R1) and then from the Works' own dams (R2).

**R5. The Tribune's evidence (S4, "what going big costs"):**
> Four years after the war, the Office's own tables show the Sierra's electrochemical load higher than at the height of the munitions effort, and the Works building dams on two rivers. Every figure is true.

**R6. Europe's reading (S5).**
- **Lindemann:** aluminium, carbide and nitrogen. Pacifica is merely modernizing.
- **Tizard:** the nitrogen output fell and the power did not. The power went to something else.
- The point goes into his 1923 file.

**R7. Open item 8:** closed by this check. The workbook keeps the dam costs, the transmission, the feed plant's fuel, and the Office's published tables as an exhibit.

**Fallback, not recommended.** Capping the plant at twice Y-12 brings the peak to 400–520 MW (5–8 per cent of capacity). It still yields about 275 kg by 1927: the test device, three devices and a thin reserve. This contradicts the author's "build big" decision, and visibility is not the binding constraint, because the load is published either way.
