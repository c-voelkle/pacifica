## Summary

1. **Where Noe's claim has force.** Under §24, the Realm regency is constitutional and not tied to guardianship, so a guardianship claim to the Realm would fail. The claim has force in Hawaii. There, the 1887 Constitution's regency article took effect only through the assent of the Territorial Council, which the Supreme Court voided in 1876. In addition, nobody has lawfully named an heir to Victoria's Hawaiian crown since Kalanimoku died.
2. **Her move.** In the winter after Donner, Noe petitions Hawaii's Supreme Court as Julian's natural guardian. She does not ask for the Realm. She asks who may speak for her son in the islands. Victoria cannot answer the petition without either defending the Council or overriding the 1876 judgment she let stand.
3. **Her purpose.** Noe does not want to win. She uses the petition to force a second bargain. In 1888 she withdraws it, acknowledges the regency and Victoria's upbringing of Julian, and refuses to lend her name to Albert Kūnuiākea's party. In return Victoria undertakes that **no body created by the ordinances will ever proclaim Julian heir to the Hawaiian crown**. Noe also sets down her Maui genealogy from Kahekili before loyalist chiefs. The chiefs who sign pledge to proclaim her son when they are lawfully asked.
4. **The result.** Noe dies in autumn 1888 as canon has it, by the official account of typhoid. Her death now gives more people a motive, not fewer, so the ambiguity is preserved and spread more widely.
5. **Effect on later canon.** The 1914 sequence stays as approved: the Council is dissolved, the legislature restored, the plebiscite held, the ʻAha Aliʻi proclaims Julian, and his claim rests on Noe's Maui descent and his adoption by a Kamehameha queen. This draft gives that sequence a root in 1888. It makes Noe one of the people who decide how her son becomes a Hawaiian king, and it takes no decision away from Victoria.

## B. Why this direction, and the constraints it keeps

**Noe's agency.** The register's caution says she must not be "reduced to a reproductive plot device; she must have her own agency." Under this draft she acts first, she chooses where to fight, and she gets something no one else could get for Julian: Hawaiian consent that does not pass through the Council. Her move matches her character. She is a physician who writes things down, she knows her claim's weak spot, and she has no use for a symbolic fight. She trades a claim she cannot win for a guarantee she will not live to see.

**Victoria's statecraft is preserved.** Victoria loses nothing she could lawfully have had. She keeps the regency uncontested, isolates Albert, splits the loyalists and gains a Hawaiian basis for her heir that she could not have obtained herself. What it costs her is that she owes it to Noe, and that she now has a promise to keep for twenty-six years. Her rivalry with a dead woman gains a concrete object.

**The death stays ambiguous.** CR-016 E2 noted that removing Noe's claim "strengthens the motive pointing at" Victoria. In this draft the claim is withdrawn before the death, but Noe alive could revive it. Other people now have motives too:
- **Victoria:** removes the only person who could revive the petition, and inherits the pledge's prestige without its author.
- **Albert's circle and the refusing loyalists:** they lose the woman who split them and gain a martyr whose death can be blamed on the Queen.
- **Silas's people:** the settlement had closed a wedge between the Regent and the Hawaiians, and Noe's death reopens it. They already spread the "Regent's year" story.
- **Those who need a martyr:** as in canon.

The draft adds no evidence for any of these and names no culprit.

**What the draft does not change:** the 1886 hānai bargain, Victoria's regency from 1887 under the regency article, the Truce, Donner, the 1888 restriction, the death in autumn 1888 and its certificate, the registers' month-long disappearance, Victoria's vaccination and Kalaupapa work, the absence of civil war, the absence of a throne abeyance, the 1914 Act and plebiscite, and Julian's accession in 1915.

## C. Draft replacement prose for §23 (for review; not applied)

> ## 23. Noe's petition, the registers and the heir (1887–1889)
>
> After Elias dies in August 1887, Noe retains a household, a mother's claim and her health work. As natural guardian she could claim a regency; Hawaiian loyalists who reject the ordinances begin to court her. **[APPROVED — CR-027]** With Emma dead since 1885 they also look to her cousin and heir Albert Kūnuiākea, by common knowledge a natural son of Kamehameha III, never acknowledged and the squanderer of his inheritance. Her rivalry with Victoria concerns the boy and Hawaii's future, not a simple contest for the dying man's affection.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** Noe does not wait to be used. The regency article binds the Realm, but in Hawaii it took effect only through the Territorial Council, the body Hawaii's Supreme Court voided in 1876. No one has lawfully named an heir to the Queen's Hawaiian crown since Kalanimoku died. In the winter after Donner, Noe petitions that court as Julian's natural guardian. She does not ask for the Realm. She asks who may speak for her son in the islands. Victoria cannot answer without defending the Council or overriding the judgment she let stand.
>
> Noe has no wish to win. In 1888 she makes the Queen bargain a second time. She withdraws the petition, acknowledges the regency and Victoria's raising of the boy, and refuses her name to Albert's party. Victoria undertakes that no body created by the ordinances will proclaim Julian heir to the Hawaiian crown; Hawaiians lawfully assembled will name him, or no one will. Noe sets down her Maui genealogy from Kahekili before chiefs of the loyalist party, and those who sign pledge to proclaim her son when they are lawfully asked. Albert's circle refuses and calls her the Queen's purchase. The loyalists divide over the first written promise anyone has made them since 1874.
>
> She dies in the autumn of 1888 during a typhoid outbreak she is fighting. That is the official account, certified by Victoria's physician. Her servants swear she was poisoned. The registers disappear for a month and return in the new Office of Public Measures. Loyalists call it murder, and Silas's people spread the story as part of "the Regent's year": Caleb, the Donner telegraphist burned in his hut that January, and now Noe. Silas suspects his own mother; his mother suspects him. Neither asks. The settlement has given many people a reason. The Regent is rid of the only woman who could revive the petition. The loyalists who refused the pledge have a martyr and no longer have the woman who divided them. Silas's people regain a wedge the settlement had closed. The Chronicler can prove none guilty.
>
> Victoria keeps the undertaking. She builds public legitimacy on Noe's honest figures. She completes vaccination across the islands in 1888–1892, brings Hawaiian-speaking nurses and clean water to plantation districts in the 1890s and reforms Kalaupapa. By about 1895 the population curve turns upward by Noe's method. Infant mortality becomes the state's sacred number, later carried into county league tables and the Gulf admission test. Care, rivalry with a dead woman and possible guilt remain inseparable to the observer.
>
> Julian grows up speaking Hawaiian with Victoria, English with tutors, Spanish among Soberanes's circle and Japanese with the naval party. He later finds Noe's registers in the Office's archive, and with them a copy of the pledge. His two mothers have given the same state its measurements and its political uses; his later falsification in 1921 corrupts an inheritance more intimate than an administrative rule.

## E. Open questions for the author

1. **Forum.** Hawaii's Supreme Court is recommended because the petition then turns the 1876 judgment into a succession weapon. The alternative is the Court of the Realm's constitutional chamber at Monterey, which would be a weaker and more Realm-centred fight. Also open: does the Hawaiian court still sit with independent justices in 1888, and how much does the Council tolerate it?
2. **Place and name of the pledge.** Lahaina, where Noe was born and Kahekili's line is rooted, or Honolulu. "The Lahaina pledge" is only a working title.
3. **Signatories.** Named chiefs or unnamed ones? Is this gathering a forerunner of the ʻAha Aliʻi, or unrelated? Canon does not date the ʻAha Aliʻi's origin, and this draft does not supply one.
4. **Who carries the genealogy.** Is it Noe's own family chanter, or Kalākaua as the Queen's printer of genealogical chants? Using Kalākaua would link it to the Queen's image-making and make it more ambiguous.
5. **Does Victoria's physician attend Noe after the settlement?** Leaving this open keeps the certificate as ambiguous as it is now.
6. **Mythic echo (optional).** A proposed counterpart to the existing "Pele and Hiʻiaka" echo for Noe's death: the genealogy as Noe's own small Kumulipo inside the Chronicler's larger one. This needs the CR-027 B9 source cross-check before scene use.

## F. Historical and legal points to verify before adoption

These belong in `historical_background.md` if the draft is adopted. They are **not verified here**, because no research was done for this draft.
- How guardianship and adoption worked under the Hawaiian Kingdom's civil code of the period, whether a birth parent could reclaim a hānai child, and whether a court would recognise a natural parent's guardianship against a written hānai agreement.
- Historical Article 22 of the 1864 constitution. From memory and unchecked: if the heirs of the named line fail, the sovereign appoints a successor with the consent of the Nobles. If the fictional Article 22 follows that text, it makes A1 much stronger. Victoria's only heir of the body is dead, Julian is not of her body, and the Nobles have been replaced by the Council, so no lawful appointment of a Hawaiian heir is possible until the legislature is restored. That restoration is exactly what happens in 1914. The fictional Article 22 is already used for Victoria's 1872 accession, so the treatment must stay consistent with that.
- Albert Kūnuiākea's historical dates and circumstances, to check against the approved role (CR-027).
- Hawaiian genealogical practice (kūʻauhau) as a public legitimating instrument, cross-checked against Fornander, Kamakau, Beckwith and Pukui, as CR-027 B9 already requires for mythic material.
