## Summary

Between the founder's death in August 1887 and her own in the autumn of 1888, Noe makes a move on the **Hawaiian succession**. She does not move on the Realm regency.

1. **She will not be the loyalists' figurehead, and she keeps that to herself.** They offer to make her the head of a counter-court against the Regent. She decides against it for her own reasons: the health board depends on the regency, a counter-court would make the boy a factional prize, and taking him back would cut away the half of his claim that comes through a Kamehameha queen. She does not announce the refusal yet. Announcing it is part of what she will give Victoria.
2. **She turns the courtship.** The loyalists have been looking to Albert Kūnuiākea. She offers them her son instead: Elias's child through her Maui line, which descends from Kahekili, raised in Hawaiian. She wins some chiefs, from the chiefly families of her Maui kin and among her Queen's Hospital patrons, all unnamed. She does not win the movement. Albert's partisans and the irreconcilables refuse.
3. **She makes a guardian's bargain.** She has a guardian's petition drafted for the Hawaiian Supreme Court, the court whose 1876 judgment Victoria let stand unenforced, and shows it to Victoria without filing it. Filed, it would force Victoria either to honour that court or to fail it a second time in public. Both women know Noe does not want the custody the petition asks for; what she can use is the hearing and its witnesses. Victoria keeps her own leverage in view and concedes by choice. She gets Noe's public refusal of the loyalists and the chiefs Noe has won, and an heir made by the Council would be no more hers than Noe's.
4. **Victoria's concession is narrow.** In a written undertaking she binds herself that Julian's Hawaiian heirship will be conferred only by her appointment with the consent of a lawful body of Hawaiian chiefs, never by the Territorial Council or by the Realm constitution. It names no body and no date. The chiefs witness it, it is not published, and the Queen keeps both the undertaking and the petition.
5. **Noe goes back to the typhoid wards.** Her death follows exactly as canon has it; the death paragraph is unchanged. The bargain adds possible motives a reader can infer but makes no new narrator statement about them. No culprit is established or implied.
6. **1888–1914.** The undertaking sets no date. Victoria does not name Julian her Hawaiian heir before the Act, within the delay canon already calls a deliberate brake on the heir, and the witnessing chiefs wait. In 1914 the ʻAha Aliʻi's proclamation is the Queen's appointment with its consent, as she had undertaken.

The draft adds **one new instrument** (the 1888 undertaking) and **one unfiled draft petition**. Both are new premises under R3, and section B lists their consequences. It adds no institution, sitting body, office, quantity, genealogy or culprit, and it makes **no legal claim about when any chiefly body can lawfully act**.

## B. The design and its consequences

### B1. Why the succession, not the regency

Canon says that "as natural guardian she could claim a regency" (master §23; CR-021 F1, APPROVED; character bible CT-JULIAN-MOTHER). Clio's dossier (H1 §2, §4, §8) finds no comparator that derives a public regency from private guardianship. The Realm's regency is filled by name in the 1887 article, and §24 distinguishes it from guardianship ("rather than as guardian obliged to surrender office"). No Hawaiian regency is vacant while Victoria reigns. The draft leaves the canonical sentence word for word and treats it as the political claim the loyalists court her for. Noe's move goes where she has a colourable lever: custody of the person, and the form by which a Hawaiian heir is made. **R1 note (AUM-09, pre-existing):** this draft reports the tension in the canonical "regency" sentence and does not resolve it. Any rewording belongs to the author (§F).

### B2. The lever and its limits

- **What the petition rests on.** Canon classifies the 1886 adoption as "hānai under Hawaiian custom" and records only *Elias's* written consent (CR-021 chronology, 1886 row; master §20). That supports a natural-guardian claim to custody of the person. It assumes Noe signed no statutory instrument (OPEN-033-04).
- **Why the petition has force.** Filed, it would make the Queen choose between honouring a Hawaiian court she once failed and failing it again in public, before chiefs. v2 states this first.
- **Why it is not filed (default).** It is Noe's tactic: a threat kept in hand is worth more than a ruling no one can enforce. Any order against the reigning Queen meets the enforcement problem of 1876.
- **Why the threat is not empty, though she does not want custody.** Both women know she will not take the boy back (§23, paragraph 1). The threat is the hearing: a public proceeding before chiefs over the form of the hānai, and a second public test of the court. v2 says so outright (M2). That is also why Victoria's concession has to be by choice.
- **Why Victoria concedes.** She is not compelled. Elias's written consent, her command of the state and the regency's purse over the health board are visible counter-leverage. She concedes because the price is small and the gain is real: Noe's public refusal of the loyalists, the chiefs Noe has won, and an heir not of the Council's making, which she wants too (A4).
- **What she concedes.** A promise about the *form* of a future Hawaiian heirship. It gives no date, no office for Noe, no custody change and no publication (by default).
- **The court (AUM-05).** Canon does not say whether the Hawaiian Supreme Court survives the 1887 constitution. That constitution abolishes the states, seats the Court of the Realm at Monterey, has county courts with Realm-appointed judges and makes Hawaiian land tenure the only Hawaiian civil-law exception. The default that the court still sits (OPEN-033-05 (a)) is therefore an assumption, not canon. Because the petition is unfiled, the story needs only a credible threat. If the author holds the court abolished, the lever re-bases on the chiefs' witness alone.

### B3. Historical and legal comparators (notes only; none of this goes in story prose)

Every item below comes from Clio H1 and is **[HIST-U], unverified in this run**. Confidence is as Clio gives it, and nothing here should be cited until checked.

- **The appointment clause.** Clio recalls that the historical 1864 Article 22 provides that, failing heirs of the body, "the successor shall be the person whom the Sovereign shall appoint with the consent of the Nobles, and publicly proclaim as such during the King's life" (high confidence on substance, moderate on wording). The undertaking's form, appointment plus chiefly consent, mirrors that clause without claiming the fictional constitution has the same text.
- **Alexander Liholiho**, the hānai son of Kamehameha III, was proclaimed heir under the 1852 constitution while his birth father lived (moderate to high confidence). This is the nearest Hawaiian precedent for a hānai heir.
- **Custody.** Clio cites the common-law priority of the mother of a child born outside marriage (*R v Nash* 1883; *Barnardo v McHugh* 1891), subject to welfare and to courts' reluctance to undo a deliberate surrender (*Chapsky v. Wood*, 1881). Moderate to high confidence on doctrine; Hawaiian application low to moderate.
- **Reclaiming a hānai child** was socially disapproved but recognized (moderate confidence). Noe's refusal to reclaim is her choice, not a legal bar.
- **Iturbide (Mexico, 1865–67)** is the closest structural analogue (moderate confidence). Here the mother does *not* reclaim, and she uses the threat of a court instead of foreign diplomacy.

**Correction to v1 (M16).** v1 reported Clio's suspicion that `historical_background.md` line 44 mislabels the 1864 Article 22 as fictional. Aumakua H5 §4 checked line 44 and did **not reproduce** a mislabel internally. The line calls Lot's *divergent* 1864 constitution fictional, consistent with line 42, line 69 and CR-027 A8/A10. Whether the historical Article 22 contained the appointment-with-consent clause remains a **[HIST-U] research question for Clio**. It is not a repository mismatch. This draft does not depend on it.

### B5. Burdens and dependencies created

- **Victoria is bound for 26 years.** The undertaking constrains how, not whether, she makes him heir. It adds no date pressure, and it is consistent with her delaying the Act.
- **The witnessing chiefs** gain a stake in Julian and a reason to remember the promise in 1913–14. They are unnamed. Waiting 26 years may also give them a grievance against the delay. That is usable tension, and nothing in the prose develops it (Aegis R-1 (ii) cost).
- **The petition is a latent liability against the adoption half of Julian's claim (Aegis I-3).** Its premise is that the customary hānai left Noe's guardianship intact. Canon relies on hānai as effective adoption, and the 1914 Act makes adoption with Senate consent a route of succession. A loyalist reading the petition later would find, in his mother's hand, an argument that the adoption half was legally incomplete, which is exactly what the hānai-line dispute (line 638) contests. Under the default (unfiled, kept by the Queen, then in the sealed papers Julian controls) the liability stays latent. Under OPEN-033-01 (b) or (c) it becomes a public record. Under OPEN-033-09 (b) (Noe kept a copy) its exposure is unknown.
- **The early-death gap (Aegis R-3), kept as stakes.** If Victoria had died before appointing him, Julian would hold no Hawaiian appointment, and she would have forsworn the Council and Realm-constitution routes. The baseline shares the gap, since he is not proclaimed before 1914 there either, but in the baseline an illegitimate fallback stayed open. v2 leaves the gap visible as stakes. It sharpens Kiyoshi's need to "outlast a Regent of seventy-five" (line 630). No testamentary appointment is supplied; that remains an author option (§F).
- **The Realm-Act coupling (Aegis §3.2): removed from the prose, conditionally.** v1's §26 rule made the only lawful consenting body arrive with the Realm's Act, so the undertaking made Julian's Hawaiian heirship wait on the Act's two-thirds. v2 makes no such claim, and the wait is Victoria's choice. The coupling **returns** only if the author decides that the ʻAha Aliʻi has no lawful standing before the Act (AUM-10) *and* that it is the consenting body (OPEN-033-03 (a)). Then the default story would again make the undertaking's performance depend on the Act.
- **The eight aliʻi Senate seats (Aegis I-1).** v1's rule contradicted them in part. v2 makes no claim about chiefly bodies, so there is no contradiction. Whether consent by aliʻi senators, who sit under the Realm constitution, could satisfy the undertaking remains inside OPEN-033-03 and is left unspecified (d).
- **Act packaging (Aegis I-2).** The approved line 634 sentence still sits inside the Act's paragraph. v2's separate sentence names the proclamation as the Queen's appointment with the ʻAha Aliʻi's consent, which answers most of the "conferred by Realm statute" argument. Fully separating the proclamation from the Act's provisions would mean restructuring approved text, and that is an author option (§F).
- **The Territorial Council and the Realm constitution** are named as routes the undertaking forbids. That is consistent with canon: the Council is illegal, the 1887 constitution's Hawaiian assent came from it, and loyalists reject it. No new institution is created.
- **Japanese and Realm constituencies** are unaffected. The undertaking concerns only the Hawaiian crown and does not touch the Realm Heir designation of 1887. With the death paragraph verbatim, no Truce-faction motive is added (AUM-03 resolved).
