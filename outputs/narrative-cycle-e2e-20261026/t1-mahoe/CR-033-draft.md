## Summary

Between the founder's death in August 1887 and her own in the autumn of 1888, Noe makes a move on the **Hawaiian succession**. She does not move on the Realm regency.

1. **She refuses to be the loyalists' figurehead.** They offer to make her queen-mother of a counter-court against the Regent. She declines for her own reasons: the health work depends on the regency, she gave her word in 1886, and a counter-court would make the boy a factional prize. Taking him back would cut away the half of his claim that comes through a Kamehameha queen.
2. **She turns the courtship.** The loyalists have been looking to Albert Kūnuiākea. She offers them her son instead: Elias's child through her Maui line, which descends from Kahekili, raised in Hawaiian. She wins some chiefs but not the movement. Albert's partisans and the irreconcilables refuse.
3. **She makes a guardian's bargain.** She has a guardian's petition drafted for the Hawaiian Supreme Court, the court whose 1876 judgment Victoria let stand unenforced, and shows it to Victoria without filing it. Victoria must choose between honouring that court and failing it a second time in public. She keeps her own leverage in view and concedes by choice, because Noe stops being the loyalists' banner and brings chiefs to the Regent's heir.
4. **Victoria's concession is narrow.** In a written undertaking she binds herself that Julian's Hawaiian heirship will be conferred only by her appointment with the consent of a lawful body of Hawaiian chiefs, never by the Territorial Council or the Realm constitution. The chiefs witness it and it is not published. Noe's death releases no one from it.
5. **Noe goes back to the typhoid wards.** Her death follows exactly as canon has it. The bargain adds motives without making any suspect likelier. No culprit is established or implied.

The draft adds one new instrument, the 1888 undertaking. It is a new premise under R3, and section B lists its consequences. It adds no institution, sitting body, office, quantity, genealogy or culprit.

## B. The design and its consequences

### B1. Why the succession, not the regency

Canon says that "as natural guardian she could claim a regency" (master §23; CR-021 F1, APPROVED; CR-016 E2, PROPOSED). Clio's dossier (H1 §2, §4, §8) finds no comparator that derives a public regency from private guardianship. The Realm's regency is filled by name in the 1887 article, and no Hawaiian regency is vacant while Victoria reigns. The draft leaves the canonical sentence word for word and treats it as the political claim the loyalists court her for. Noe's move goes where she has a colourable lever: custody of the person, and the form by which a Hawaiian heir is made. **R1 note:** this draft reports the tension in the canonical "regency" sentence and does not resolve it. Any rewording belongs to the author.

### B2. The lever and its limits

- **What the petition rests on.** Canon classifies the 1886 adoption as "hānai under Hawaiian custom" and records only *Elias's* written consent (CR-021 chronology, 1886 row; master §20). That supports a natural-guardian claim to custody of the person. It assumes Noe signed no statutory instrument, which is OPEN-033-04.
- **Why it is not filed (default).** Any order against the reigning Queen meets the enforcement problem of 1876. Its force is the choice it puts to Victoria and the chiefs who see it.
- **Why Victoria concedes.** She is not compelled. Elias's written consent, her command of the state and the regency's purse over the health board are visible counter-leverage. She concedes because the price is small and the gain is real: the loyalists lose their banner, and some chiefs come to her heir.
- **What she concedes.** A promise about the *form* of a future Hawaiian heirship. It gives no date, no office for Noe, no custody change and no publication (by default).

### B3. Historical and legal comparators (notes only; none of this goes in story prose)

Every item below comes from Clio H1 and is **[HIST-U], unverified in this run**. Confidence is as Clio gives it, and nothing here should be cited until checked.

- **The appointment clause.** Clio recalls that the historical 1864 Article 22 provides that, failing heirs of the body, "the successor shall be the person whom the Sovereign shall appoint with the consent of the Nobles, and publicly proclaim as such during the King's life" (high confidence on substance, moderate on wording). The undertaking's form, appointment plus chiefly consent, mirrors that clause without claiming the fictional constitution has the same text. "Heirs of her body, lawfully begotten" would exclude Julian, so appointment is his only Hawaiian route under such a clause.
- **Alexander Liholiho**, the hānai son of Kamehameha III, was proclaimed heir under the 1852 constitution while his birth father lived (moderate to high confidence). This is the nearest Hawaiian precedent for a hānai heir.
- **Custody.** Clio cites common-law priority of the mother of a child born outside marriage (*R v Nash* 1883; *Barnardo v McHugh* 1891), subject to welfare and to courts' reluctance to undo a deliberate surrender (*Chapsky v. Wood*, 1881). Moderate to high confidence on doctrine; Hawaiian application low to moderate.
- **Reclaiming a hānai child** was socially disapproved but recognized (moderate confidence). Noe's refusal to reclaim is her choice, not a legal bar.
- **Iturbide (Mexico, 1865–67)** is the closest structural analogue: a written placement bargain, a birth mother reclaiming, and the ruler's household holding the child (moderate confidence). Here the mother does *not* reclaim, and she uses the threat of a court instead of foreign diplomacy.

**Clio's possible repository mismatch, reported and not resolved:** `historical_background.md` line 44 may mislabel the 1864 Article 22 as fictional. This draft does not depend on that point.

### B5. Burdens and dependencies created

- **Victoria is bound for 26 years.** The undertaking constrains how, not whether, she makes him heir. It adds no date pressure, and it is consistent with her delaying the Act.
- **The witnessing chiefs** acquire a stake in Julian and a reason to remember the promise in 1913–14. They are not named.
- **The 1913 ʻAha Aliʻi stance.** Line 632 has the ʻAha Aliʻi resolved to "proclaim only Julian" in 1913, before the Act restores the legislature. Under the default reading of OPEN-033-03, this is a declared intention of chiefs who are not yet sitting lawfully, not a lawful act. The candidate wording keeps the baseline sentence intact. If the author reads the ʻAha Aliʻi as already constituted in 1913, OPEN-033-03 must take alternative (b) or (c).
- **The Territorial Council and the Realm constitution** are named as routes the undertaking forbids. That is consistent with canon (the Council is illegal; Hawaiian loyalists never accept the 1887 constitution) and creates no new institution.
- **Japanese and Realm constituencies** are unaffected. The undertaking concerns only the Hawaiian crown and does not touch the Realm Heir designation of 1887.
