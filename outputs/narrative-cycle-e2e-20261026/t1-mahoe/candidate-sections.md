## 1. §23, first paragraph, with the move added before the death paragraph (master line 509)

**Note:** The baseline paragraph is kept word for word, including the APPROVED CR-027 sentence on Albert Kūnuiākea and the sentence on natural guardianship and a regency (CR-021 F1). Three PROPOSED paragraphs follow it, before the death paragraph.

**Baseline (verbatim):**

> After Elias dies in August 1887, Noe retains a household, a mother's claim and her health work. As natural guardian she could claim a regency; Hawaiian loyalists who reject the ordinances begin to court her. **[APPROVED — CR-027]** With Emma dead since 1885 they also look to her cousin and heir Albert Kūnuiākea, by common knowledge a natural son of Kamehameha III, never acknowledged and the squanderer of his inheritance. Her rivalry with Victoria concerns the boy and Hawaii's future, not a simple contest for the dying man's affection.

**Candidate:**

> After Elias dies in August 1887, Noe retains a household, a mother's claim and her health work. As natural guardian she could claim a regency; Hawaiian loyalists who reject the ordinances begin to court her. **[APPROVED — CR-027]** With Emma dead since 1885 they also look to her cousin and heir Albert Kūnuiākea, by common knowledge a natural son of Kamehameha III, never acknowledged and the squanderer of his inheritance. Her rivalry with Victoria concerns the boy and Hawaii's future, not a simple contest for the dying man's affection.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** In the winter of 1887–88 **[OPEN-033-08]** the loyalists offer to make her their banner: the mother of the founder's heir, at the head of a counter-court against the Regent. She refuses. Her health board lives on the regency's money; she gave her word in 1886; a counter-court would make her son a prize among the factions the Truce has only just bound; and taking him back, however tempting, would cut away the half of his claim that comes through a Kamehameha queen. She turns the courtship instead. Where the loyalists look to Albert, she offers them her son as their future candidate: Elias's child through her own Maui line, which traces its descent to Kahekili, raised in Hawaiian by the Queen. Some chiefs come over to her **[OPEN-033-06]**. The movement does not. Albert's partisans keep their candidate, and the irreconcilables, who will take nothing from the Regent's house, keep their purposes and refuse her.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** With those chiefs behind her, she has a guardian's petition drafted for the Hawaiian Supreme Court, the court whose judgment of 1876 the Queen let stand unenforced **[OPEN-033-05]**. It asks the court to recognize her as the boy's natural guardian, because the hānai of 1886 was made under custom **[OPEN-033-04]**. She does not file it: a judgment against the Queen would end on paper, as the last one did **[OPEN-033-01]**. She shows it to Victoria. Filed, it would leave the Queen two courses: honour a Hawaiian court she once failed, or fail it a second time, in public and before chiefs. Victoria keeps her own leverage in view: Elias's written consent, her command of the state and a health board that depends on the regency. She concedes by choice, because the bargain also serves her. Noe stops being the loyalists' banner, and the chiefs she has won come to the Regent's own heir.
>
> **[PROPOSED — CR-033]** What Victoria concedes is narrow. In a written undertaking she binds herself that Julian's Hawaiian heirship will not be conferred by the Territorial Council or by the Constitution of the Realm, but only by her own appointment with the consent of a lawful body of Hawaiian chiefs. It does not say which body, or when such a body can sit **[OPEN-033-03]**. It is a promise about how he will be made heir, not that he will be. For Noe that is enough: an heir made by the Council would be the ordinances' heir, and the chiefs she has turned would never accept him. The chiefs Noe brought over witness it, and it is not published **[OPEN-033-02]**. It is the Queen's promise, and Noe's death would release no one from it. Noe keeps her registers out of the bargain. They are figures, not terms; she keeps them honest and unbargained and does not ask who will use them after her. Then she goes back to the wards: the rest of her year is vaccination in Honolulu and Maui, the district registers and, in the autumn, typhoid.
