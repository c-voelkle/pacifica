## 1. Hānai versus legal adoption

**[REPO] What canon fixes**
- The 1886 adoption is classified **"LEGAL: hānai under Hawaiian custom"** (CR-021 chronology, 1886 row, line 362). Canon describes a customary hānai. It does not describe a statutory adoption.
- Noe "agrees to his hānai adoption by Victoria, with Elias's written consent". Her price is the health board, a place in the boy's life and a Hawaiian upbringing. Victoria's price is that he be raised as her son and successor (master §20, line 476; CR-021 F1, line 508; character bible CT-JULIAN-MOTHER, line 81).
- CR-016 C rates the adoption APPROVED but its "timing, form" PROPOSED (line 74). The read files never say **whether Noe's own consent was written, acknowledged or recorded**. The written consent is attributed only to Elias.
- In 1914 the Act makes adoption with Senate consent a succession route: "the hānai precedent made law" (CR-021, 1914 row). Loyalists still dispute whether a hānai son may continue the Kamehameha line (master §27, line 638).

**[HIST-U] Custom (moderate confidence)**
- Hānai was a customary transfer of a child's rearing, very often among kin. The child usually knew its birth parents, and the relationship with them was not extinguished.
- Ethnographic sources describe birth parents occasionally taking a hānai child back. This was socially disapproved but recognized.
- Hawaiian usage also distinguished other forms, for example *keiki hoʻokama*, a child taken as one's own, sometimes with an expectation of inheritance.
- Check: Pukui, Haertig & Lee, *Nānā i ke Kumu* vol. 1 (1972), the "hānai" and "hoʻokama" entries; Handy & Pukui, *The Polynesian Family System in Kaʻū* (1958).

**[HIST-U] Statute and courts (low to moderate confidence)**
- I recall that the Kingdom had an early adoption law in the 1840s compilations. I also recall a later statute, which I place around 1876 without certainty, that regularized adoption by a written instrument, acknowledged and recorded.
- I recall Supreme Court decisions of the 1860s–1880s, which I think include a case reported as *In re Estate of Nakuapa* (3 Haw., c. 1872; the citation may be misremembered). As I remember them, they denied inheritance rights to children taken under custom alone, without the statutory form.
- Check: the 1841–42 *Translation of the Constitution and Laws*; *Civil Code* (1859); Session Laws 1876; *Compiled Laws of the Hawaiian Kingdom* (1884); *Hawaiian Reports* vols 2–7; and J. S. Modell, *A Sealed and Secret Kinship* (1994), which surveys the history of Hawaiian adoption law.

**[HIST-U] Documented disputes (low confidence on specifics)**
- Probate and estate litigation over the status of hānai and adopted children appears in *Hawaiian Reports*. Custody fights between birth parents and the families who had raised a child appear in guardianship files.
- I cannot name a royal custody dispute of 1864–88 from memory.
- Check: the Hawaiʻi State Archives Judiciary series, First Circuit Court probate and guardianship files.

**[INF] Does Elias's written consent change things?**
- At common law the putative father of a child born outside marriage had no custodial rights. In law, the mother's consent is what counts.
- Elias's writing therefore carries political and evidentiary weight: it acknowledges paternity and records the founder's wish. It does not carry custodial weight.
- One reading the repository allows: in 1886 Victoria was a married woman, so Elias's consent may also have served as a husband's consent to his wife's adoption.
- If the adoption was customary only, Noe's legal position as mother may have survived it. The canon phrase "natural guardian" (§2) assumes exactly that.

**Cannot be established here:**
- Whether a statutory instrument existed in canon.
- Whether Noe signed anything.
- The exact adoption statute in force in 1886.

## 2. "Natural guardian"

**[REPO] What canon fixes**
- "As natural guardian she could claim a regency" (master §23, line 509, with no label on the sentence). CR-021 F1 is APPROVED and reads "could claim *the* regency" (line 510). CR-016 E2 originated the idea as PROPOSED (line 102).
- §24 says Victoria governs "as Regent of a Realm whose crown has yet to be established, rather than as guardian obliged to surrender office on the boy's majority" (line 520).
- The read files do not say who holds Julian's guardianship in private law after August 1887. They do not say whether Elias left a will naming a guardian. They do not say whether Julian holds any estate in his own right.

**[HIST-U] Common-law doctrine (moderate to high confidence)**
- Guardianship of the **person** and guardianship of the **estate** were distinct. A "natural guardian" (father, then mother) had custody of the person only. Managing a minor's property required appointment by a court.
- For a child born outside marriage, English courts in the 1880s gave the mother the primary right to custody against the putative father and against strangers. The leading cases are *R v Nash* (1883) 10 QBD 454 and, confirming it, *Barnardo v McHugh* [1891] AC 388.
- That right was subject to the child's welfare.
- Agreements by which a parent surrendered custody were generally not specifically enforceable. Even so, courts often refused to return a child after a deliberate surrender when the welfare of a child settled with the new family pointed the other way. The classic American statement is *Chapsky v. Wood*, 26 Kan. 650 (1881), Brewer J.
- The Guardianship of Infants Act 1886 (UK) first let mothers appoint testamentary guardians. It applied in England, not in Hawaii.
- Check: the cited reports, and a contemporary treatise such as Schouler, *Domestic Relations*, 1880s edition.

**[HIST-U] Hawaiian application (low to moderate confidence)**
- Kingdom courts drew heavily on English and American authority. The 1859 *Civil Code* and later statutes gave guardianship and probate jurisdiction to justices of the Supreme Court sitting at chambers and to circuit judges. I am unsure how that jurisdiction was divided in 1887–88.
- Mothers routinely petitioned to be appointed guardians of their children's estates.
- For chiefly children, the sovereign historically intervened by decree in their upbringing and lands, for example by placing them in the Chiefs' Children's School. Custom also used the *kahu*, a chief's appointed guardian or attendant.
- Check: *Civil Code* 1859, the guardianship chapter; *Compiled Laws* 1884; judiciary acts of 1886–88; the archive guardianship files; Kuykendall vol. 1 on the Chiefs' Children's School.

**[INF] What follows**
- In a common-law frame, "natural guardian" supports a claim to **custody of the person**, and perhaps a petition to be appointed **guardian of any estate**.
- It supplies no claim to a **regency**. A regency is a public-law office created by constitutional text, not a private-law status.
- If the reigning sovereign is the adoptive parent, enforcing any custody order against her raises the same problem as the 1876 judgment, which was never enforced. I have not established whether the sovereign could be named in such proceedings at all. Check the Hawaiian case law on suits involving the sovereign.

**Cannot be established here:**
- Hawaiian statutory wording on the custody of children born outside marriage.
- Whether Julian has an estate.
- Who sits on the fictional Hawaiian bench in 1887–88.

## 3. Royal succession and heirs

**[REPO] What canon fixes**
- Under Article 22 of Lot's 1864 constitution, Victoria succeeds in December 1872 without an election (master §17, line 424; CR-027 A8 and A10).
- The 1887 Realm constitution names Julian "Heir of the Realm" (master §21, line 483).
- The ʻAha Aliʻi proclaims Julian heir to the **Hawaiian** crown only in 1914 (master §27, line 634).
- The read files do not establish whether Victoria proclaimed Julian Hawaiian heir before 1914, or what the ʻAha Aliʻi is in relation to the historical House of Nobles.
- CR-016 B (PROPOSED, a reused source claim): Liliʻuokalani and Bernice Pauahi were hānai children, and adoption of heirs was normal among the Japanese elite.

**[HIST-U] The 1864 Article 22 (high confidence on substance, moderate on exact wording)**
- The crown went to Kamehameha V and the heirs of his body lawfully begotten. Failing them, it went to **Princess Victoria Kamāmalu Kaʻahumanu and the heirs of her body, lawfully begotten**.
- Failing those, "the successor shall be the person whom the Sovereign shall appoint with the consent of the Nobles, and publicly proclaim as such during the King's life".
- Failing such an appointment, the Cabinet Council was to convene the Legislative Assembly, which would elect by ballot "some native Aliʻi". This is the route of Lunalilo in 1873 and Kalākaua in 1874.
- The 1887 "Bayonet" constitution kept the same structure, substituting Kalākaua and Liliʻuokalani.
- Check: the constitutional texts (Hawaiʻi State Archives; Lydecker, *Roster of Legislatures of Hawaii*, 1918); Kuykendall vol. 2, around pp. 125–133, which already appear in the CR-027 bibliography.

**[HIST-U] Precedents**
- **Alexander Liholiho** was the hānai son of Kamehameha III and was proclaimed his successor under the 1852 constitution, which required appointment by the King and the House of Nobles. His birth father Kekūanāoʻa was alive. This is the closest Hawaiian precedent for a hānai child proclaimed heir, and Victoria's own brother. (Moderate to high confidence; check Kuykendall vol. 1 and the 1852 constitution, Article 25.)
- **Albert Kūnuiākea**, Kamehameha III's natural son, was **not** an heir, because he was not lawfully begotten. (Moderate confidence.)
- **Lot**, after Victoria's historical death in 1866, named no heir. Bernice Pauahi reportedly declined when he asked her on his deathbed. The throne went to election.
- **Kalākaua** proclaimed Leleiōhoku in 1874. After Leleiōhoku's death in April 1877 he proclaimed Liliʻuokalani. How the Nobles' consent was obtained while the legislature was in recess is a detail I am unsure of.
- **Liliʻuokalani** proclaimed her niece **Kaʻiulani** on 9 March 1891 with the Nobles' consent. Kaʻiulani was a blood niece, not hānai.
- Liliʻuokalani's own hānai children, including **John ʻAimoku Dominis**, her husband's natural son by another woman, were never proclaimed. As I recall, she formalized his adoption only around 1910. (Low to moderate confidence; check Liliʻuokalani, *Hawaii's Story* (1898), a retrospective memoir; Kuykendall vol. 3; Liliʻuokalani Trust records.)

**[INF] Consequences**
- "Heirs of her body, lawfully begotten" excludes Julian. Under a Hawaiian Article 22 modelled on history, Julian can follow Victoria **only by her appointment, with the Nobles' consent, publicly proclaimed during her life**.
- Nothing in that clause bars an adopted appointee; only the election clause specifies "native Aliʻi".
- The House of Nobles' role is consent. It is a veto point, not a power to initiate.
- In the fiction, the appointed Territorial Council has taken over the legislature's business since 1876. Whether a lawful body able to give "consent of the Nobles" exists in 1887–88 is therefore an open question for canon.

**Possible repository mismatch (R1), to report and not resolve:**
- `historical_background.md` line 44 says "Article 22 of Lot's *fictional* 1864 constitution names Victoria heir". CR-027 A8 says Lot "names her heir", apparently as a divergence.
- My recollection, which is unverified, is that the **real** 1864 Article 22 already named her. If so, the divergence is her survival in 1866, not the clause.
- This needs checking against the constitutional text before the background document is relied on.

## 4. Regency for a minor

**[REPO] What canon fixes**
- The 1855 Kuhina Nui office gave Victoria "standing to exercise a regency" (master §6, line 155).
- Lot retained the office. Ruth resigned it in 1876, and it has been vacant since (master §17, line 432; historical_background line 140).
- The Realm's 1887 regency article names Victoria Regent until an Act of Settlement (master §21, line 483).
- The text of the fictional Hawaiian constitution's regency clause, if it has one, is not in the read files.

**[HIST-U] 1840 and 1852 constitutions (moderate confidence)**
- The Kuhina Nui exercised royal powers during a vacancy or minority. This matches the historical 30 November 1863 interval.

**[HIST-U] 1864 constitution, Article 33, carried substantially into 1887 (moderate to high confidence on structure)**
- The sovereign could appoint a regent while absent, and could **by will** appoint a regent or regents for a minor heir.
- If a sovereign died intestate leaving a minor heir, the Cabinet Council served as Council of Regency until the Legislative Assembly met and elected a regent or Council of Regency by ballot. Majority was set at eighteen.
- No parent or guardian had standing as such.
- Check: the constitutional texts as in §3; Kuykendall vol. 2 on the 1864 convention.

**[INF] Consequences**
- No Hawaiian regency is vacant in 1887–88. Victoria is a living adult sovereign, and Julian is not a minor sovereign of Hawaii.
- The only "regency" in play is the Realm's, and the 1887 article fills it by name.
- A claim by Noe would therefore be one of two things:
  - a political attack on the legitimacy of the 1887 article (§5, mechanism E); or
  - a contingent claim about who should govern for Julian if Victoria died while he was a minor.
- Even under the historical Hawaiian model, that contingency is decided by Victoria's will or by the legislature, not by the mother.
- Custom is a different matter. Regency-type power in Hawaiian history rested on chiefly rank and the Kuhina Nui office (Kaʻahumanu, Kīnaʻu). Noe's minor chiefly rank is a political constraint here, not a legal bar.

**Qualification on CR-016 B, a reused claim:**
- CR-016 B treats Kaʻahumanu's 1819 kuhina nui claim as costing "one short battle, at Kuamoʻo" (line 62). Historically, Kuamoʻo (December 1819) was fought against Kekuaokalani over the overthrow of the kapu system. It was not directly a contest over her office. (Unverified; check Kamakau, *Ruling Chiefs of Hawaii*; Kuykendall vol. 1.)

## 5. Loyalist politics, 1885–1888

**[REPO] What canon fixes**
- Emma is the counter-queen until her death in 1885. Queen's Hospital is her foundation, and Noe's first patrons are loyalists (CR-027 A16 and A20).
- After Emma, the loyalists look to **Albert Kūnuiākea**: "by common knowledge a natural son of Kamehameha III, never acknowledged and the squanderer of his inheritance". No reign and no successful revolt are supplied (master §23, line 509; CT-ALBERT-KUNUIAKEA, line 890).
- The loyalists reject the ordinances and the 1887 constitution (master §21, line 485).
- The read files name no loyalist organization or newspaper for 1887–88.

**[HIST-U] Albert Kūnuiākea (moderate confidence)**
- Born about 1851 to Kamehameha III and Jane Lahilahi Young, who was the sister of Emma's mother. That makes him Emma's first cousin, as canon says.
- He was raised in the royal household by Queen Kalama. He was excluded from the succession as not lawfully begotten.
- I recall that he contested or settled claims under Emma's will.
- **Possible tension with canon:** my recollection is that he was openly treated as the King's son within the household. "Never acknowledged" may need qualifying to "never legitimated or placed in the succession".
- Check: Kanahele, *Emma: Hawaii's Remarkable Queen* (1999); Forbes, *In Haste with Aloha* (2017), already in the CR-027 bibliography; probate files for Emma's estate.

**[HIST-U] Opposition figures (low to moderate confidence)**
- Historically, after 1885 the opposition was not a single Emma party. It ran through legislators and editors.
- Among them were G. W. Pilipō of North Kona, an Emma-aligned critic of Kalākaua who died in 1887; Joseph Nāwahī; John E. Bush; and, after his return from Italy in 1887, Robert W. Wilcox. There was also the Wilcox conspiracy of early 1888, which reportedly aimed to replace Kalākaua with Liliʻuokalani, a historical attempt to engineer the succession from outside the sovereign's will.
- Check: Kuykendall vol. 3 (1967); Osorio, *Dismembering Lāhui* (2002).

**[HIST-U] Organizations (moderate confidence)**
- The **Hawaiian League** formed early in 1887. It was a secret, largely haole reform body with the Honolulu Rifles behind it, and it forced the July 1887 constitution.
- **Hui Kālaiʻāina** (the Hawaiian Political Association) formed around 1887–88 to oppose that constitution and restore the 1864 order.
- **Hui Aloha ʻĀina** formed in 1893, with men's and women's branches, and ran the 1897 Kūʻē petitions.
- Check: Noenoe K. Silva, *Aloha Betrayed* (2004); Kuykendall vol. 3.

**[HIST-U] Instruments actually used**
- Petitions and memorials to the sovereign and the legislature. This practice was long established, from the 1845 petitions against foreign officials to the 1897 Kūʻē petitions.
- Mass meetings (*ʻaha*) at Honolulu.
- Hawaiian-language newspapers: *Ka Nupepa Kuokoa* (1861–1927, establishment and missionary-leaning); *Ka Leo o ka Lahui* (from 1889, Bush); *Ka Makaainana* (from 1887?, low confidence); *Ko Hawaii Pae Aina*.
- Elections and votes in the Legislative Assembly; lawsuits; and, at the extreme, armed conspiracies (1888 and 1889).
- Check: the Papakilo and Ulukau newspaper databases; Chronicling America; Silva (2004).

**[INF] Applicability boundary**
- The Hawaiian League, the Bayonet constitution and Hui Kālaiʻāina answered a historical 1887 that is **not** the fictional 1887. In the novel, Kalākaua is Victoria's minister and the hostile instrument is the Realm constitution.
- Historical bodies and titles are comparators for the *kinds* of instruments available: petitions, the press, ʻaha meetings, and legal arguments resting on the 1864 order. They are not evidence that those named bodies exist in the fiction.

## 6. Women and medicine

**[REPO] What canon fixes**
- Noe was born about 1860 in Lahaina, so she is a Hawaiian subject by birth (inference). She is unmarried in the read files.
- She qualified through the Polytechnic examination in 1884. In 1886 she became medical director of a health board created by Elias's decree (master §20, lines 472–474).
- Nothing in the read files says whether her office survives his death or can be removed by the Regent.

**[HIST-U] Legal capacity (moderate confidence)**
- An unmarried adult woman was a *feme sole*: she could own property, contract and sue in her own name. Coverture would not apply.
- Hawaiian women, chiefly women especially, held and litigated land throughout the Kingdom period. Mothers petitioning as guardians was ordinary practice.
- I recall a Hawaiian Married Women's Act of 1888, which bears only on married women.
- No rule I know barred a woman from petitioning in a guardianship matter; counsel would normally appear.
- Check: Session Laws 1888; *Compiled Laws* 1884, on husband and wife; Merry, *Colonizing Hawaiʻi* (2000), on gender and the Kingdom courts.

**[HIST-U] Divergence note, low priority**
- A Hawaiian Board of Health existed historically from the 1850s. As I recall it was reorganized in 1859 and ran Kalaupapa from 1865.
- The 1886 "establishment" in canon is therefore a divergence, or a refounding, that the read sections do not flag.
- Check: the Board of Health reports and Kuykendall.

**[INF] What constrains her**
- Her real constraints are political and practical, not a matter of legal capacity:
  - her salaried office depends on the regime she would be litigating against;
  - she is not of the high rank custom expects of anyone exercising regency-type power;
  - any judgment would face the enforcement problem already shown in 1876.

## 7. Non-Hawaiian analogues (all [HIST-U])

**1. Alice Green de Iturbide and Maximilian's adopted heir (Mexico, 1865–67).** Moderate confidence.
- Maximilian and Carlota, childless, took the infant Agustín de Iturbide y Green, a grandson of the former emperor, into the imperial household as a quasi-heir. A written agreement in September 1865 placed the boy with the Emperor and named Princess Josefa de Iturbide as his guardian.
- His American-born mother repudiated the bargain within weeks. She tried to take the child back, was escorted out of Mexico, and pressed her claim through the U.S. State Department, the press and appeals to Carlota and Napoleon III. The boy was restored to his parents after the empire collapsed.
- *Applicability:* this is the closest structural match: a written bargain, a birth mother reclaiming, and the ruler's household holding the child. Its limits: the parents were married, the boy was not the ruler's natural son, and the mother's leverage was foreign diplomatic pressure on a client empire.
- Check: *Papers Relating to Foreign Affairs* (FRUS) 1865–66, Seward's correspondence; Arnold Blumberg, *The Diplomacy of the Mexican Empire* (1971). C. M. Mayo's 2009 novel is fiction, so use only its cited sources.

**2. María Cristina, the regency and the *tutela* of Isabel II (Spain, 1840–41).** Moderate confidence.
- After María Cristina left the regency in 1840, the Cortes in 1841 separated the minor queen's **guardianship** (*tutela*), conferring it on Agustín Argüelles, from the regency, which went to Espartero.
- She protested from exile. Her partisans' October 1841 rising included an attempt to seize the child from the palace.
- *Applicability:* this shows guardianship of the person operating as a separate, contestable office alongside a regency, decided by the legislature. Its limits: she was the legitimate mother of a reigning minor, and she was herself the former regent.
- Check: the *Diario de Sesiones de Cortes* (1841); Isabel Burdiel, *Isabel II* (2010).

**3. Caroline of Brunswick and access to Princess Charlotte (Britain, 1812–14).** Moderate confidence.
- The Prince Regent restricted his estranged wife's access to their daughter, the heir presumptive. With Whig allies (Brougham), Caroline published a letter of complaint in the *Morning Chronicle* in 1813 and turned access to the heir into a public political cause.
- *Applicability:* this shows custody of an heir used as leverage through the press and an opposition faction. Its limits: the child was legitimate, the opponent was the father-regent, and no court proceeding decided custody.
- Check: Flora Fraser, *The Unruly Queen* (1996).

**Counter-analogue, from the Japanese constituency's frame:**
- In the Meiji imperial house, birth mothers of heirs, for example Yanagiwara Naruko, the mother of the future Taishō Emperor, had no legal standing. The heir was registered as the empress's son. The birth mother's family could gain rank, but she could not contest custody.
- This shows that the Japanese constituency would read Noe's legal position as weak.
- Check: Donald Keene, *Emperor of Japan* (2002).

The repository already uses Guangxu in 1875 as a comparator (CR-016 B, PROPOSED). In that case the birth parents, Prince Chun and his consort, were subordinated, and the prince withdrew from office. It is a counter-analogue in which the birth parent makes no move.

## 8. Which mechanisms are legally colorable for a fictional 1887–88 move by Noe, and which would be invention

**Would be invention, a new premise under R3:**
- **A regency held by Noe "as natural guardian."** No comparator I know of derives a public regency from private guardianship. A regency needs constitutional text, a will or a legislative election. The canonical sentence "could claim a regency" stays a *political* claim unless the author supplies a new legal basis. - **Any change to Noe's death or culpability.** The mystery is protected (AGENTS.md; R3).
