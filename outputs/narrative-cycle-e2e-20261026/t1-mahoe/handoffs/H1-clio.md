## Context (neutral)

In the fictional baseline, Elias Thorne dies in August 1887. Victoria Kamāmalu (historical person, here Queen of Hawaii since December 1872 under Article 22 of Lot's fictional 1864 constitution) becomes Regent of the new Realm of Pacifica under an 1887 constitution whose Hawaiian assent came from an illegally created appointed Territorial Council; the Hawaiian Supreme Court voided that Council's ordinances in 1876, and the judgment was never enforced. Julian (born 1886) is the biological son of Elias and Kaʻanoe (Noe) Ward, a Hawaiian-American physician of minor Maui chiefly descent; Victoria took him as her hānai son in 1886, with Elias's written consent, in a negotiated bargain with Noe. The storyline says that "as natural guardian" Noe "could claim a regency", and that Hawaiian loyalists (Emma's party; after 1885, Albert Kūnuiākea) court her. She dies in autumn 1888. The author wants her to be able to make a real move in the succession during 1887–88.

## Questions (answer what you can; say what cannot be established)

1. **Hānai versus legal adoption.** In Hawaiian Kingdom law and practice of the 1860s–80s, what was the legal status of a hānai child relative to the birth mother? Was there a statutory adoption procedure (e.g. the 1841/1850s civil code provisions, or the Act of 1876 on adoption if one existed)? Could a birth parent reclaim a hānai child, and are there documented disputes (customary or in court)? Does the answer change if the hānai was also written down with the father's consent?
2. **"Natural guardian."** Under Hawaiian Kingdom law of the period (and the common-law tradition it drew on), did a surviving unmarried mother of a child born outside marriage have guardianship of the child's person and estate? Who could appoint a guardian of a minor of rank, and which court (e.g. Supreme Court in probate/chambers)? Was guardianship of the person distinct from guardianship of the estate?
3. **Royal succession and heirs.** Under the 1864 Constitution (historical Article 22), how was an heir to the throne designated (sovereign's proclamation with consent of the Nobles?) and what happened if there was none? Historical precedents: Lot's failure to name an heir; Kalākaua's naming of Leleiōhoku and then Liliʻuokalani; could an adopted/hānai child be proclaimed heir? (Note Liliʻuokalani's later hānai children and the 1890s treatment of Kaʻiulani.) What role did the House of Nobles play?
4. **Regency for a minor.** What did Hawaiian constitutions (1852, 1864, 1887 Bayonet) provide for a regency or guardian for a minor sovereign or heir? Who had standing?
5. **Loyalist politics 1885–1888.** Historically, what were Albert Kūnuiākea's claims and reputation; who were the Emma-party leaders after Emma's death (1885); what organizations existed (Hui Kālaiʻāina formed 1888; Hui Aloha ʻĀina later; the 1887 Hawaiian League on the other side)? What instruments did Hawaiian political actors of the time actually use: memorials/petitions, newspapers (e.g. *Ka Nupepa Kuokoa*, *Ka Leo o ka Lahui* later), court suits, ʻaha meetings?
6. **Women and medicine.** Any historical constraints relevant to a Hawaiian woman physician bringing a guardianship petition in her own name in 1887–88 (coverture does not apply to an unmarried woman; property and capacity to sue)?
7. **Analogues.** Briefly, two or three non-Hawaiian 19th-century cases where a birth mother of a ruler's illegitimate or adopted heir contested guardianship or used custody of the child as leverage against a regent (state the applicability boundary of each).
