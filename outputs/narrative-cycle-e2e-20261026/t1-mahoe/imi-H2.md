## 1. Baseline diagnosis

### What the baseline already does well (a change must keep it)

1. **Her agency is real up to 1886, and it is institutional, not dynastic.** In MS 472–476 she sets her own course step by step: she nurses in 1881, passes the Polytechnic examination, uses figures to win the minister-level attention ministers can't get, gets a funded board with quarantine powers, and builds registers. The hānai bargain (MS 476; F1 508) is a genuine two-sided negotiation, and both women set a price. The trajectory (MS 850) is right to say these "give her agency independent of the men and dynasty", and the bible protects "motives independent of the murder rumor" (CB 87). Any succession move must sit on top of the health work, not replace it.
2. **The symmetry of the two women.** "One controls access to Elias's body, the other to the state" (MS 476; CB 81). This structure carries a lot of weight, and a 1887–88 move should continue it after the body is gone.
3. **The registers' long arc.** Honest figures (MS 474) pass into the Office (MS 511), become Victoria's legitimacy (MS 513), are tabulated by Hollerith (MS 522, PROPOSED), are found by Julian (MS 515) and are betrayed by the Altai falsification (MS 515; CB 106). This is the main thing Noe does for the novel's moral design, and it depends on her being *honest and unpolitical about the numbers*. The POV table says she cannot see "how her rivals will use her honest figures after her death" (CB 668). A move should keep that blind spot, so the registers stay outside whatever bargain she strikes.
4. **The death's ambiguity is spread across several parties.** Victoria, Silas's faction (with Abigail and Silas suspecting each other) and loyalists who need a martyr all have a motive (MS 511; F1 512). The bible insists this is "real uncertainty rather than making Victoria guilty by implication" (CB 83).
5. **Dying while fighting the outbreak** (MS 511) is already an act of agency. She dies on duty, not as a victim waiting in a household.

### Where the text lets her down (the author's sense is accurate)

The passivity is specific. It sits in the political succession plot between August 1887 and autumn 1888, not in her character as a whole.

- **MS 509 (§23, first paragraph) is all potential and other people's verbs.** "As natural guardian she *could* claim a regency; Hawaiian loyalists … *begin to court* her." Then the loyalists "*also look to*" Albert. The paragraph closes on a summary ("Her rivalry with Victoria concerns the boy and Hawaii's future") that the text never acts out after Elias dies. A modal ("could claim") that is never resolved reads as an abandoned set-up, not as restraint.
- **MS 511 opens with her death.** Between 509 and 511 a year passes in the chronology, and Noe makes no choice in it. The reader gets an offer, a rival candidate, then a corpse.
- **The motives for her death are asserted, not earned.** MS 511 lists who had motives, but because Noe did nothing in 1887–88 to threaten or disappoint anyone, those motives are generic ("those who need a martyr"). The ambiguity is protected but thin: three parties with nominal motives is less suspenseful than three parties she has actually crossed.
- **The payoff in the trajectory has no set-up.** MS 850: in the sealed papers Julian finds "that Noe's death had more suspects than he knew." Nothing in §23 tells the reader what those papers could hold about Noe beyond the rumours already listed in 511. A 1887–88 move would give that discovery content without naming a culprit.
- **She disappears from the succession when she has the best access.** In §22 (MS 499–501) the Truce is negotiated in Hawaii in 1886–87 while she controls access to the dying founder (MS 476), yet she does not figure in it. This is a set-up gap rather than part of the 1887–88 window. It matters because it makes her absence after August 1887 look like a pattern.
- **One structural point the author's note runs together.** The note says she "could claim the regency as natural guardian", but the text makes clear that Victoria is not that kind of regent. Her authority comes from the constitution's regency article (MS 483) and the Truce (MS 499–501), and she governs "as Regent of a Realm … rather than as guardian obliged to surrender office on the boy's majority" (MS 520). Noe's guardianship therefore has little purchase on the Realm regency. Where it does have purchase is the boy's person and his Hawaiian standing. Hawaiian loyalists reject the 1887 constitution because Hawaii's assent came from the illegal Territorial Council (MS 485). The ground on which Noe is strong is therefore Hawaiian law and Hawaiian legitimacy, not the Realm. This shapes everything in §2.

**Smallest repair (whatever direction is chosen):** replace the unresolved "could claim" in MS 509 with an action and its consequence before MS 511, and let MS 850's sealed-papers line point back to it.

## 2. Direction assessment

### A. Considered refusal

- **Desire:** consistent. Her reasons (the health work, the bargain, the boy's safety) come straight from CB 87.
- **Conflict:** weak with Victoria, who loses nothing. It is moderate with the loyalists: her first patrons were loyalists (MS 472), so refusing them costs her something real.
- **Consequence:** thin in 1914 unless the refusal is tied to something Julian later finds.
- **Ambiguity:** safe, perhaps strengthening. A woman who refused the loyalists is more useful to them dead than alive, which sharpens the "martyr" motive without making it obvious.
- **Form:** fits easily, two or three sentences.
- **Cost:** the author asked for "a real move in the succession", and a refusal on its own risks reading as passivity with a reason attached.
- **Verdict:** a good *first beat*, not a whole answer.

### B. Claim to the regency

- **Desire:** off-target. Ruling the Realm is not one of her desires in CB 87 or F1.
- **Conflict:** loud but misdirected. She would be pressing a guardian's claim against a constitutional regency (MS 483, 520) and a three-faction Truce signed while Elias lived (MS 501). Victoria, Silas, Kiyoshi and Soberanes all have reasons to refuse, so the outcome is predetermined.
- **Consequence:** she must lose. That makes her the "loser" the brief warns of and contradicts her clear-eyed bargaining in 1886 (MS 474–476).
- **Ambiguity:** worst of the six. A public contest for the regency, followed within months by death, points the reader at Victoria, which CB 83 forbids by implication.
- **Crowding:** it competes with Donner (October 1887, MS 505) and the Truce for the same months and the same stage.
- **Verdict:** weakest. Its only virtue is that it literally cashes the "could claim" line, and that line is better rewritten.

### C. Guardian's bargain over the Hawaiian succession

- **Desire:** it serves her stated hope directly: "her son will sit on a restored Hawaiian throne" (MS 476; CB 87). It also turns the original hānai rationale, that a hānai son of the Hawaiian royal house can become heir (F1 508), into an enforceable question.
- **Conflict:** real, with Victoria keeping her own purposes and ways to refuse. Choosing the Hawaiian Supreme Court is pointed: it is the court whose 1876 judgment Victoria let stand unenforced (MS 432). The petition makes her either honour a Hawaiian court she once failed or fail it a second time in public. Victoria can resist on the written hānai consent (MS 476), by stalling, or by her command of the state. Her concession then becomes a choice she makes, not a defeat.
- **Consequence:** the strongest of the six. In 1914 the ʻAha Aliʻi "will proclaim only Julian" (MS 632) and proclaims him heir by Hawaiian forms (MS 634). Loyalists dispute whether a hānai son may continue the line, and Noe's Maui descent strengthens his claim (MS 638). An 1888 undertaking gives these approved 1914 events a root in her action. It also gives the sealed papers (MS 850) something Noe-specific to hold.
- **Ambiguity:** there is a risk if the sequence is written as "she forces the Queen's hand, then dies." Repair: make the undertaking **survive her**, written and witnessed, so her death releases Victoria from nothing. That lowers Victoria's motive. Meanwhile loyalists who see their courted figurehead making terms with the Regent, and factions for whom a Hawaiian-forms succession disturbs the Truce balance, each gain a motive. The suspect field widens, as MS 850 requires, instead of narrowing to one person.
- **Form:** fits. One causal paragraph in §23 and one clause each in §27 and MS 850.
- **Cost to Victoria:** if the undertaking becomes the *sole* cause of 1914, her approved reason there ("as she did not ask them in 1874", MS 634) and her lifelong reckoning with the lease (MS 838) are reduced to keeping a debt. Repair: keep the undertaking **narrower** than what Victoria later gives. For example (author choice), Noe obtains only that the Hawaiian heirship will be proclaimed by Hawaiian chiefs and not settled by the Realm constitution or the Territorial Council. The plebiscite remains Victoria's own, larger act of conscience. She then exceeds the promise rather than merely paying it, which fits her "need to outdo" Noe (F1 514–522).
- **Routes:** whether a Hawaiian court in 1887–88 would hear a guardian's petition over a hānai child, and whether "natural guardian" is a Kingdom-law concept, go to **Clio**. Whether the ʻAha Aliʻi (or what body) exists to receive an undertaking in 1888 goes to **Aumakua** and the author. I have seen the ʻAha Aliʻi only from 1914 (MS 632–634).

### D. Bringing the loyalists to her son

- **Desire:** it serves the Hawaiian-throne hope and uses material the text has already set up but not used: the courtship (MS 509), Albert as the existing alternative (MS 509; CB 887–890) and Noe's Maui line (MS 638).
- **Conflict:** strong with the loyalists and with Albert's partisans, who keep their purposes. Albert is a squanderer but a Kamehameha by common knowledge, and his backers can refuse. It is weak with Victoria unless the "conditions" bite. On its own, D can make Noe the person who delivers loyalist chiefs to Victoria's chosen heir, an unpaid agent of the Regent's succession. That cuts against "agency independent of the men and dynasty" (MS 850).
- **Consequence:** good, and it is compatible with 1914 if handled precisely. Loyalists can accept Julian as Hawaiian heir and still campaign against *union* with the Realm crown (MS 634), and still argue about hānai continuity (MS 638). Partial success is enough.
- **Ambiguity:** balanced. Albert's partisans gain a motive. Victoria gains a mixed one: Noe helps her heir but also builds a Hawaiian base around the boy that Victoria does not control.
- **Form:** fits.
- **Cost:** it can crowd the loyalist strand into a recruitment drive. Keep it to chiefs, not a movement.

### E. Reclaiming the child

- **Desire:** it serves a maternal desire the bible does not give her and works *against* her stated one. Half of Julian's 1914 claim is "his adoption by a Kamehameha queen" (MS 638), and revoking the hānai would destroy the throne she bargained for.
- **Conflict:** maximal, but the 1886 bargain "stands as written", so she must fail. That makes her both a loser and inconsistent with the clear-eyed woman of MS 474–476.
- **Ambiguity:** points hard at Victoria.
- **Verdict:** reject as a direction. As a *temptation she weighs and rejects* inside A or C, one clause could give the interior cost real force.

### F. Alliance with a Truce faction

- **Desire:** weak fit. Silas's American officers are later nativists (MS 614), and the naval party is Japanese. Soberanes's Polytechnic is the only natural affinity (it examined her, and the Office inherits her registers), but Soberanes "chooses institutions over a strongman" (MS 501) and would plausibly refuse to reopen the Truce for her. That is good characterization for him but a dead end for her.
- **Conflict:** with Victoria, yes, but on the Realm stage, where Noe is weakest.
- **Consequence:** pulls her into Donner and the Truce, which already carry a heavy load (MS 505, 530), and away from Hawaii.
- **Ambiguity:** risky. An alliance with Silas's side tilts the Silas/Abigail suspicion (MS 511) from mutual suspicion toward the obvious. An alliance with the Polytechnic edges toward explaining the registers' missing month, which is part of what is protected (R3).
- **Verdict:** costly. Its attractive element (an institutional ally for the registers) collides with the protected mystery.

## 3. Judgment

**One combination is clearly strongest: C with D as its leverage, opened by A's refusal.** In sequence: she refuses to be the loyalists' figurehead (A). She turns their courtship into a constituency for the boy rather than for Albert (D). She uses that constituency, together with her standing as natural guardian before a Hawaiian court, to extract from Victoria a narrow, written undertaking that Julian's Hawaiian heirship will be settled by Hawaiian forms (C).

This is the strongest option literarily because it is the only one that:

- acts on her own stated desire (CB 87);
- fights on the ground where her standing is real (Hawaiian law and legitimacy, MS 485) rather than on the Realm stage, where the text has already settled the outcome (MS 483, 501, 520);
- gives Victoria a choice with a cost rather than a defeat;
- feeds approved 1914 events (MS 632, 634, 638) and the sealed-papers discovery (MS 850);
- *widens* the suspect field.

C alone is the next best. D alone risks making Noe Victoria's helper. A alone is too small. B, E and F each cost more than they give.

**What I do not judge to be settled:** whether the undertaking is made privately, held among the regency's sealed papers, or witnessed publicly by chiefs. Private custody serves MS 850 (Julian finds it). Public witness serves the 1914 ʻAha Aliʻi beat (MS 632). These are genuinely different books, and I find them incomparable on literary grounds. That is an author choice.

**Conditions any version must meet:**

- keep the registers out of the bargain, preserving her blind spot (CB 668);
- make the undertaking survive her, so her death frees no single party;
- keep the undertaking narrower than Victoria's 1914 plebiscite;
- leave the health work as the frame of her last year, so she still dies fighting the outbreak;
- add nothing to §22's Donner material.

**Illustrative only (author choice, not canon, label PROPOSED if adopted):** *She will not be the loyalists' queen-mother, but she will take their chiefs. Through the winter of 1887–88 she brings some of them from Albert to the boy of Kahekili's line. With them behind her, and a guardian's petition drafted for the court whose judgment the Queen let die, she makes Victoria put in writing that the boy's Hawaiian heirship will be proclaimed by Hawaiian chiefs and not by the Council. Then she goes back to the typhoid wards.*

**Routing:** legal plausibility of the guardian's petition and Kingdom guardianship law goes to Clio. The existence and status in 1888 of any chiefly body that could witness or receive the undertaking, and the integration mismatch that F1 508 still speaks of "a hānai son of the Kuhina Nui" where Victoria is Queen from 1872 (historical attachment, not rewritten), go to Aumakua.
