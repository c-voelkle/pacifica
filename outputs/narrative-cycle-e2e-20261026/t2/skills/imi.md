## Summary judgment

The aim is right, and the choice of move is well matched to Noe. As written, though, the move does not yet *work* as drama. It succeeds at once, costs nothing, meets no refusal and changes nothing that follows. It is also framed with knowledge Noe cannot have. Separately, one silent edit tilts the protected mystery of her death towards Victoria. That edit should come out whatever happens to the move.

I recommend keeping the idea and redrafting ¶2, using one of the options in §3 below. Restore the baseline wording everywhere else unless the author intends those changes as separate decisions.

---

## 1. Strengths to keep

- **The kind of move fits her character.** Noe asks for guardianship of the heir's *Hawaiian education*, not the regency. That is the right scale. The bible's Noe "obtains an institution for the work, not merely a place near a ruler" (character_bible.md, CT-JULIAN-MOTHER). Her 1886 hānai price was "a continuing place in his life and Hawaiian upbringing" (§20). Turning that private bargain into a public claim reads as her own idea, not one the plot hands her. It also gives her a plausible alternative she *rejects*: the loyalists want her as regent or as a figure beside Albert, and she chooses a narrower lever she can hold. The candidate should say this outright (see §2.1).
- **Placement.** Putting the move in January 1888, before the autumn death, gives the death paragraph something to cut off. In the baseline Noe is courted and then dies. In the candidate she acts and then dies, which is the stronger shape.
- **"Noe does not wait to be courted."** This is a good hinge sentence. It turns the inherited passive construction around in five words.
- **Paragraphs 3–5 keep the baseline's ambivalence.** That includes "Care, rivalry with a dead woman and possible guilt remain inseparable to the observer" and the Julian/registers coda. The certificate clause is the one exception (§2.4).

## 2. Material weaknesses (most consequential first)

### 2.1 The move succeeds without opposition, cost or consequence
*Location: ¶2, "the Chamber grants the petition by a large majority. Victoria, who admires her courage, accepts every condition at once."*

**Effect on a reader.** The section's only new act of agency turns into a declaration that everyone complies. Three problems follow:
- **Victoria loses her own position.** §20 and the bible define the two women by "respect and fear". Instant, admiring acceptance turns that into deference. Victoria is a skilled bargainer elsewhere (the 1873 mutiny, Puʻuloa, the 1888 restriction "presented as Silas's price") and here has no counter-move.
- **The loyalists disappear.** ¶1 has them courting her as regent and looking to Albert. ¶2 never says how they take her choosing a lesser title from the institutions of the 1887 constitution, which they reject (§21: "Hawaiian loyalists reject it"). Going to the Chamber is a costly choice *for her* with the people who are her first patrons (Queen's Hospital, §20). The candidate leaves out the most interesting result of her own act.
- **Nothing later depends on it.** The guardianship is granted in January, and by autumn she is dead. ¶3–¶5 never mention it again. Who holds it afterwards? Does it lapse, pass to Victoria, or become a loyalist grievance? ¶5's "Julian grows up speaking Hawaiian with Victoria" quietly absorbs it. The move currently has no weight in what follows.

**Smallest repair:** keep the petition, but let it cost something and leave a trace. See the options in §3.

### 2.2 Noe is given knowledge from after her lifetime (R4), and the stated foresight is garbled
*Location: ¶2, "she knows that her district registers will one day decide which territories enter the Gulf and how the county league tables rank them."*

**Effect.** This is the author's later irony placed inside the character's head. The Gulf Republic does not exist until 1899–1900 (§25), and its admission tests belong to the 1900s–1920s. The league tables come from the Office's later practice. A woman in January 1888 can know that numbers moved Elias, and that an Office of Public Measures is being chartered under the new constitution. She cannot know the Gulf test. The prophecy makes her a mouthpiece for the state's endgame and spends the irony the baseline keeps for its closing lines ("later carried into county league tables and the Gulf admission test"; "his later falsification in 1921 corrupts an inheritance…"). It also makes her *less* her own person: she becomes the founder of the measures state by intention, not a physician fighting deaths she can count.

The phrase "which territories enter the Gulf" also inverts the relationship. In the canon the Gulf Republic seeks admission to Pacifica, so no territory "enters the Gulf". I flag this as a clarity error, not a style preference.

**Repair:** replace the sentence with what she can reckon in 1888 (see the sample in §3). Leave the long consequence to ¶4–¶5.

### 2.3 Point of view: the inner certainty clashes with the Chronicler's stated limits
*Location: ¶2, "Noe sees further than her rivals"; "Like Anne of Austria… she understands that…"*

**Effect.** Three paragraphs later the section rests on a narrator who "can prove none guilty". ¶2 meanwhile claims full access to Noe's strategic mind and sets her above her rivals. The two stances work against each other: the narrator knows exactly what Noe thinks but nothing about how she died. The bible asks for differentiated perception ("each person notices different evidence…"), and Noe's voice is "clinical observations, exact numbers, dry gallows humor". A Bourbon royal precedent is not her idiom. It sounds like the Chronicler's, Victoria's or Kalākaua's.

The analogy also sits better on the other side. Anne of Austria was the widowed queen who used the Parlement to break a regency council. That maps more closely onto Victoria's position than onto Noe's. As a comparison drawn *by the Chronicler*, or one used *against* Noe by Victoria's people, it could be sharp. As Noe's private conviction it is out of character, and it pre-empts the reader's judgment.

**Repair options (author's choice):**
- (a) Cut the analogy.
- (b) Give it to the Chronicler explicitly, as his comparison.
- (c) Give it to a court voice, so that the analogy becomes part of the rivalry.

Clio should check the accuracy of the analogy itself.

### 2.4 A silent edit tilts the protected mystery towards Victoria (R3)
*Location: ¶3, added clause: "who signs the certificate before he has seen the body, on instructions from the palace."*

**Effect.** This is the most serious change in the candidate, and it is not part of the stated aim. The bible says the approved event "preserves real uncertainty rather than making Victoria guilty by implication" (CT-JULIAN-MOTHER). In the baseline, Victoria's motive sits alongside the motives of Silas's faction and the loyalists. The new clause is not testimony or rumour. It is stated as narrative fact, and it attaches a specific irregular act to the palace alone. Combined with ¶2's suspiciously easy acceptance ("accepts every condition at once"), a reader is likely to read Victoria's generosity as a mask and the death as hers. The sentence "the Chronicler can prove none guilty" then reads as coyness, not uncertainty.

**Repair:** remove the clause. If the author wants an irregular certificate as a story element, make it *alleged*: a servant's account or a loyalist pamphlet that the Chronicler cannot authenticate. Balance it with comparably weighted evidence pointing elsewhere. That is an author decision. The Chronicler's authenticated testimony is OPEN in the bible.

### 2.5 Invented quantities and institutional facts stand in for drama
*Location: ¶2, "twenty-four-year-old", "by a large majority", "forty thousand Hawaiians"; "petitions the Chamber of Deputies"; ¶4, "Hollerith… in 1890."*

**Literary effect.** These details create a false precision that makes the success feel *certified*, not earned. "Forty thousand by the summer" is especially damaging. It turns Noe's work into the miraculous early success that §20 and the bible explicitly reject ("not a miraculous reversal of mortality in three years"; "limited and foundational… she does not cure the islands"). It also leaves nothing for Victoria's 1888–1892 completion to do.

Each of these is also a factual matter outside Imi's authority. I route them below and do not adjudicate them.

### 2.6 Albert's kinship is rewritten and becomes internally confusing
*Location: ¶1, "Emma's son Albert Kūnuiākea, by common knowledge a natural son of Kamehameha III."*

**Literary effect.** Within the one sentence, a reader now has Queen Emma as the mother of Kamehameha III's unacknowledged son. That is confusing, and it has nothing to do with Noe's agency. The approved text (CR-027 A16) calls him Emma's "cousin and heir", and the candidate also drops the `[APPROVED — CR-027]` label. Restore the baseline wording and label. Aumakua should handle the canon conflict.

## 3. Ways to make the move convincing (author choices, not corrections)

Each option keeps the petition idea and gives it friction and consequence. I have tried not to add new institutions. Anything marked *new premise* would need its own decision.

- **A. Enforce the 1886 bargain (fewest new premises).** Noe does not go to a chamber. She demands publicly that the Regent honour the written hānai terms: a place in Julian's life and a Hawaiian upbringing, now with her as its named guardian. Victoria agrees, but for a price. The price could be Noe's renunciation of any regency claim, or the registers passing to the new Office. **This is a new bargain term and needs the author's decision.** The loyalists call her concession a betrayal. The register transfer then gives the later "disappearance for a month" an extra ambiguity without resolving it.
- **B. Keep the Chamber, and let the institution answer back.** If the author wants a public parliamentary act, do not let it pass easily. Show who supports her (aliʻi life senators? Hawaiian deputies?) and who refuses: the loyalists boycott an 1887-constitution body, and Silas's people want no Hawaiian claim on the heir. Victoria then shapes the result. *Whether the Chamber, or the Senate, has any power over the heir's guardianship, and whether the Chamber sits in January 1888, is a new constitutional premise.* Aumakua and Clio should check it before drafting.
- **C. Act through the registers, not a title.** Noe's move is to publish district mortality figures that the loyalists, Victoria and Silas's people all have to answer. She uses her numbers as the leverage for the guardianship claim. This fits her voice best ("exact numbers… impatience with waste"). It also makes the later uses of her registers an echo of her own tactic, not of a prophecy.

Whichever option is chosen, add **one sentence of consequence after her death**: what becomes of the guardianship. It could lapse, pass to Victoria, or become a grievance the loyalists carry. **The outcome is an author choice.**

**Illustrative sample (option A, roughly the length of the current ¶2; not proposed canon):**

> Noe does not wait to be courted. In January 1888 she refuses the loyalists' regency and holds Victoria to the 1886 instrument instead: a place in the boy's life and a Hawaiian upbringing, with herself named to answer for it. Victoria agrees and names her price. The loyalists who wanted a regent call it a sale. Noe has counted the deaths in two districts and wants the vaccinators paid before the next outbreak, not a throne for a child of two.

The precise terms of the price are left blank on purpose. "Two districts" is a placeholder; Chryseis should check any figure.

## 4. Unchanged from the baseline (noted, not caused by the candidate)

- *"Caleb, the Donner telegraphist burned in his hut that January, and now Noe"* can be read as an appositive ("Caleb, the Donner telegraphist…"). In fact Caleb died by gunshot at Donner in October 1887 (§22), and the burned telegraphist is a separate victim. A semicolon or "and" would fix this. The candidate now also sets Noe's petition in "January" 1888, the same month as the telegraphist's death. Decide whether that coincidence is meant to be noticed. If so, someone in the story should notice it; if not, move one of the two dates.

## 6. Open questions for the author

1. Is the move meant to be a *public* act (Chamber or petition) or an act *within the bargain* (enforcing the 1886 hānai terms)? This decides how many new premises the section has to carry.
2. Do you want Victoria to pay a visible price for agreeing, or to extract one? Either way, I recommend that she should not simply concede.
3. What happens to the guardianship after Noe's death?
4. Were the changes to Albert's kinship, the certificate and Hollerith's date intended? If they were, each is a separate decision from the agency revision.

There is no overall score. The move can be kept. ¶2 needs redrafting before the candidate is an improvement on the baseline, and the ¶3 certificate clause is a regression whatever happens to the move.
