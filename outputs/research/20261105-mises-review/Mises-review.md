## What this note does

Mises is the hardest reader Pacifica's economics could meet. The design he spends the book attacking is the one Pacifica runs: a managed central bank, a state investment fund, chartered champions with state equity, and an adjustable international gold order. This note treats him as a hostile reviewer. For each objection it gives:
- what he claims;
- what in Pacifica it hits;
- whether canon already answers it, could answer it in a sentence, or needs a mechanism in the financial workbook or the planned dynamic layer.

The status labels used throughout:

| Label | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **HOLDS** | Canon already answers the objection on Mises's own terms. |
| **WORDING** | One sentence of canon answers it; the model is unchanged. |
| **MECHANISM** | The claim can't be called rigorous until the workbook or the dynamic layer computes it rather than assumes it. |
| **AUTHOR** | A real trade-off. Either answer is defensible, and Mises's side of it is material for the story. |

**Two cautions.**
- **Mises gives mechanisms and tests, not numbers.** He rejects mathematical economics (XVI §5, XXVI §6). Nothing here calibrates the workbook; for calibration see the Mankiw pairings at the end.
- **He concedes more than his reputation suggests**, and some of his concessions are Pacifica's best defence (part D). The most important is that state enterprises trading in a market "are integrated into a system of market economy. They are subject to the law of the market and have the opportunity of resorting to economic calculation" (XXVII §1). By his own definitions Pacifica is a hampered market economy, not socialism. His calculation argument therefore applies only where the state allocates capital without prices.

## Verdict

Pacifica's real economy holds up against Mises better than its money does.

- **Weakest: the monetary system of the 1920s.** Canon combines productivity-driven falling prices, "a Reserve Bank managing falling prices", a gold-exchange reserve centre, a Los Angeles boom and an adjustable order in 1932. That is the configuration Mises uses to explain the American boom of 1926–29 and the interwar collapse.
  - The workbook currently prints exactly the pattern he diagnoses. Its implied price index (nominal over real GDP) is **1.58 in 1922 and 1.60 in 1929**, flat, while real output grows **6.6% a year**. The author's direction, that technological deflation is a visible fact of the 1920s, does not yet show in the numbers.
  - The workbook also has no money stock and no fiduciary-media line, and it explicitly did not carry over foreign gold deposits (CR-020 D). It cannot yet answer him either way.
  - The decisive choice is one sentence of canon (MR-01).
- **Middle: the Development Fund.** The fixed $0.30 return is the one number in the model a careful reader can't accept. Mises's argument about the unseen alternative shows why: the model counts the Fund's gain but not what the same money would have done elsewhere (MR-07, MR-08).
- **Strongest: the champions, the Crown share, war finance and the 1932 rescues.** Canon already chose:
  - minority state stakes in private firms whose owners carry most of the losses;
  - war loans placed with savers;
  - rescues paid from reserves and borrowing rather than new notes.

  On Mises's own terms most of this holds. The rest is wording.

## A. Money, banking and the cycle

### MR-01: Falling prices, or managed ones? (AUTHOR + MECHANISM)

**Mises.**
- Price stability during a productivity boom is the mark of hidden credit expansion. "But there were some exceptional instances too in which the upward movement of prices was only slight. The most remarkable example was provided by the American boom of 1926–29" (XX §6).
- Had money grown less, "a tendency toward a drop in the prices of all commodities would have taken effect" (XX §6).
- A price fall that comes from more goods is not deflation in his sense, and it is not zero-sum. Such changes "are not merely transfers from some people to other people" (XVII §6).
- He calls a stable price level "an empty and contradictory notion" (XII §4). Index numbers say least in exactly the slow changes a productivity deflation produces.

**Pacifica.**
- The investment-share note's storyline sentence pulls two ways at once: "cheaper cars, power, aluminium and film every year, a Reserve Bank managing falling prices, savers who hold gold coupons because everything else gets cheaper."
  - If prices fall, the bank is not stabilizing them.
  - If the bank manages the fall by adding notes and credit to slow it, it is doing what Mises says made the American boom. Pacifica's productivity lead is much larger than America's, so the hidden expansion would be larger too.
- The workbook has already taken the second path by default. Its single general price index runs 1.00 in 1913, 1.58 in 1922 and 1.60 in 1929, against real growth of 6.6% a year in 1922–29.

**Recommendation (PROPOSED).** Canon should state the Reserve Bank's rule:
- The Bank keeps convertibility and watches its gold cover and the growth of its own notes and deposits.
- It does **not** target a price index.
- Manufactured goods, power and transport get cheaper year after year. Money wages hold or rise. Real wages rise from both sides.

This makes the gold-coupon saver coherent and makes falling prices part of daily life on the page. It also gives the 1920s a sharp contrast with the Union, whose central bank (CR-020 B13) can stabilize and boom. The gold-standard decades after 1873 are the historical precedent for falling prices alongside rising output (to verify with figures).

**The alternative.** If the Bank did stabilize, the Los Angeles boom would be largely Pacifica's own creation, and §34's milder slump would need the boom's size derived rather than asserted (MR-02).

**Decision (author, 5 November 2026).** "The notion that the reserve bank would fight against falling prices is exactly the problem i have been inchoately trying to push back against. Indeed Pacifica should not only embrace falling prices, but even strive to see them lowered where doing so can unlock more productivity. If goods become dirt cheap, they can in turn be used as cheap inputs for the next cycle. This is what I mean by state capitalism on the contemporary Chinese model."

The decision has two parts:
1. **The monetary rule (passive).** The Reserve Bank does not offset falling prices that come from more goods. This is Mises-compatible.
2. **The industrial aim (active).** The state works to make key inputs cheap: power, steel, aluminium, transport, chemicals and credit. Each cheap input lowers the costs of the next round of production.

**What part 2 needs in order to stay rigorous (PROPOSED).**
- **Two ways to make an input cheap.**
  - *Real cost reduction* (scale, learning, better methods) is a pure gain, and Mises would call it innovation, not intervention.
  - *Pricing below cost* (subsidy, below-market credit, suppressed deposit rates) moves the cost onto taxpayers or savers. That is China's financial repression, and Mises's "unseen" cost (MR-08).

  Pacifica should get most of its cheapness the first way. Where it uses the second, it should do so openly, as a published subsidy whose spillover the Office of Public Measures claims to measure.
- **Learning curves as the mechanism.** In the dynamic layer, model sector prices by experience: unit cost falls a fixed share with each doubling of cumulative output. The historical anchors are the Model T, Alcoa's aluminium and power-station costs (to verify). Lower downstream costs then follow from cheaper inputs. This is the second- and third-order machinery the dynamic layer was meant to have.
- **Consequences to carry.**
  - **Debt and interest.** Falling prices raise the real burden of existing debts: farm mortgages, development bonds, the standing debt. Nominal interest rates should run low, because lenders expect to be repaid in dearer money (Mises's negative price premium, XX §3).
  - **Margins and overcapacity.** Champions' profits depend on costs falling faster than prices. Otherwise there are price wars and overcapacity, China's *neijuan*. The Columbia's 1930–32 spill shows the risk.
  - **Gold.** Falling prices raise what gold buys, which encourages more gold mining. That is the gold standard's built-in brake, and it favours Pacifica as a producer.
  - **Partners and rivals.** Pacifica exports its falling prices. Cheap Pacifican steel, aluminium and cars in the Union and British markets bring accusations of dumping and calls for tariffs, much like today's complaints about Chinese overcapacity. This is material for the Union press and the confrontation dossier.

**Mechanism.** Add a money block to the Gold & money sheet:
- the money stock (gold coin, notes, deposits);
- fiduciary media (liabilities beyond gold held);
- a demand for money that grows with real output.

Derive the price level from these and test it against the current index. With real output growing about 5.8% a year in 1913–29, prices have to fall unless monetary gold or fiduciary media grow as fast. The lever "Pacifica suffers 60% of the US price fall" (CR-020 D) should become an output of this block, not an input. A Levers switch `rb_rule` (0 = let prices fall, 1 = stabilize the index) would show both worlds side by side.

### MR-02: What the 1927–28 lean actually does (WORDING + MECHANISM)

**Mises.**
- On the boom: "The banks believe that they have done all that is needed to stop “unsound” speculation when they lend on more onerous terms" (XX §6). The test is quantity: "one must look at the state of the supply of fiduciary media, not at the arithmetical state of interest rates" (XX §6).
- Margin rules are qualitative credit control. "Discrimination in lending is no substitute for checks placed on credit expansion" (XXXI §5).
- Without fresh expansion, "There is no means of avoiding the final collapse of a boom brought about by credit expansion" (XX §8).
- His own concession: "if the additional amount issued is not large, neither are the inevitable effects of the expansion" (XVII, note 17). The same note begins "The notion of “normal” credit expansion is absurd". He allows that a small expansion has small effects, not that it has none.

**Pacifica.** CR-020 B6 and storyline §34 describe the lean as higher rates and stock margins. On Mises's test that wording proves nothing.

**Where Pacifica's boom comes from.** The more interesting answer is gold flowing in rather than the Bank's own policy. Pacifica is the world's gold sink: about 22% of world monetary gold in 1925 (W3), and concession gold sold to the Reserve Bank against credit (CR-021 H). Mises notes that under gold "only a part of the additional gold immediately increased the supply offered on the loan market" (XX §8); the rest reached prices first.

Gold that arrives in San Francisco as bank deposits and foreign balances, rather than as mine output paid in wages, lands on the loan market first. That last step is an inference from his mechanism, not his words. It gives Pacifica a Misesian boom it did not choose.

**Recommendation (PROPOSED).** Reword the lean as quantitative:
- the Bank sterilizes the inflow, holding its notes and deposits still while gold arrives;
- it stops buying commercial paper;
- it lets its cover ratio rise;
- margin rules come second.

"A smaller boom and a smaller bust" then follows from his own concession: smaller expansion, smaller effects.

**Mechanism.** Add gold inflows and the Bank's sterilization to the money block (MR-01). In the dynamic layer, drive the boom's size from the growth of fiduciary media and size the 1930–32 bust from it.

### MR-03: The reserve centre (MECHANISM)

**Mises.**
- His most striking self-correction: "In dealing with the problems of the gold exchange standard all economists—including the author of this book—failed to realize the fact that it places in the hands of governments the power to manipulate their nations' currency easily" (XXXI §3).
- A bank that holds other central banks' reserves is limited only by what limits a single bank in an isolated system, and its partners become "satellites" (XVII §19; XXXI §3).
- Legal cover ratios restrain issue in normal times, but "The restriction will be withdrawn whenever a government deems that an emergency justifies resorting to extraordinary measures" (XVII §12).
- "There is but one means of keeping a local and national currency permanently at par with gold and foreign exchange: unconditional redemption" (XVII §16).

**Pacifica.** CR-021 H's ways to need less gold are Mises's list of warnings:
- partners holding their reserves in San Francisco;
- Japan's currency resting on Pacifican deposits (canon);
- gold coin withdrawn into the reserve;
- a legal cover that can be lowered.

The workbook's cover test does not see this. "Foreign gold deposits" were not carried over from Sol's model (CR-020 D), so the same gold may stand behind Pacifica's notes and behind its partners' currencies, and the check counts only the first.

**Recommendation.**
- **Mechanism.** Add foreign official deposits to the Reserve Bank's liabilities and run the cover test on notes plus those deposits. Add a Stress event: Britain draws its San Francisco balances in September 1931, the obvious run in canon.
- **Author's choice.** Partners' gold can be **earmarked** (held 100% and untouchable: Mises's money-certificates, no expansion, no earnings), or held **fractionally** (Pacifica earns on it and carries the run risk). The PROPOSED middle course: earmark partners' gold; treat dollar balances as fractional, with a published ratio.

**Story.** Mises's sentence was written in 1949. In a world where the reserve centre is Pacifica, the warning has a natural in-world speaker (part F).

### MR-04: Sterling's return in 1925 (AUTHOR)

**Mises.** Britain's two returns to the old parity, after the Napoleonic wars and after the war of 1914, are his central examples of deflation by policy. The second "was simply a display of inexcusable ignorance of economics as well as of monetary history" (XX §7). Raising the gold value of existing debts is debt aggravation, which favours bondholders over taxpayers (XXXI §2).

**Pacifica.** Pacifica backs sterling's return as the largest holder of monetary gold (W3), and storyline §34 keeps Britain's debt to Pacifica at its gold-dollar value after September 1931. If the return is at the pre-war parity, Mises supplies the motive the story can name: Pacifica is Britain's largest creditor, and an old-parity pound raises the value of what Britain owes it.

That gives the confrontation dossier an economic edge:
- London's deflation and unemployment of 1925–31 partly serve San Francisco's balance sheet;
- Pacifica's economists can see it.

**Choice.** Either keep the old parity and own the motive, or have Pacifica press for a lower parity. A lower parity would be kinder to Britain and is the course Mises would have approved, but it shrinks the debt's gold value and changes Britain's 1920s. Neither touches the 1932 ending.

### MR-05: The rescues of 1929–32 (HOLDS in part; WORDING)

**Mises.**
- The credit famine of a crisis is "caused not by contraction but by the abstention from further credit expansion" (XX §7).
- Rescues that check the fall in prices and wages prolong the slump: "the only means to shorten the period of bad business is to avoid any attempts to delay or to check the fall in prices and wage rates" (XX §7).
- His concessions matter here:
  - contraction "produces neither malinvestment nor overconsumption" (XX §7), so preventing a collapse of fiduciary media is the least objectionable kind of intervention;
  - a treasury that hoards boom-time surpluses "may to this extent weaken the boom created by credit expansion" (XXXI §5);
  - spending from taxes or borrowing "does not add anything" to total spending (XXXI §5).

**Pacifica.** Canon already chose most of his conditions:
- the $150m recapitalization and the rescues are paid from the Reserve Fund and borrowing, with no tax increase and no note issue named (CR-020 B6);
- the Reserve Fund is a hoarded boom surplus;
- the gold lent abroad is real gold above cover.

On his terms these are fiscal transfers, not credit expansion.

What Mises would still say is that the rescues keep boom-era positions alive, and that a milder slump carries unliquidated mistakes into the 1930s. That fits the novel: the Columbia's 1.2–4.6 TWh a year of spilled power in 1930–32 (CR-055) is the visible mistake, and the sequel can inherit the rest.

**Wording (PROPOSED).** The lender of last resort follows Bagehot's rule (*Lombard Street*, 1873, so period-correct): lend freely, at a penalty rate, against good security. Recapitalize solvent banks; wind up insolvent ones. Add one clause to §34. Mises's objection then narrows to the timing of the readjustment, a debate the novel can stage instead of losing.

### MR-06: The standing debt and the hydro refinancing (WORDING)

**Mises.**
- "The long-term public and semipublic credit is a foreign and disturbing element in the structure of a market society" (XII §5).
- When the treasury borrows from a bank that creates new money, "Legally the bank becomes the treasury's creditor. In fact the whole transaction amounts to fiat money inflation" (XX §7).
- If a bank lowers its rate without issuing new fiduciary media, it cannot cause a boom: "It merely makes a gift to the debtors" (XXXI §5, note).

**Pacifica.** The standing debt of 5% of GDP is there so the central bank has securities to work with, and "the Reserve Bank runs the 1931 rescue through these bonds" (OPEN-020-06). That reads as the Bank buying government bonds with new notes, which is the case Mises calls fiat inflation.

**Wording (PROPOSED).** The bonds are the Bank's **collateral** and the savers' safe asset. The Bank lends against them at its penalty rate and does not buy them outright beyond its cover. That keeps OPEN-020-06's realism (a central bank needs eligible security) without monetizing.

**The hydro refinancing holds without any subsidy.** Keawe's 30% cut in the power price (§34) needs no below-market rate. At 5%, stretching repayment from twelve years to thirty cuts annual debt service by **42%** (annuity factor 0.113 against 0.065). If capital charges are about seven tenths of a hydro board's power price, the price falls by about **30%** at an unchanged market rate: 27% at 6%, 32% at 4%.

Canon should say the Reserve Bank **arranges** the thirty-year bonds, placing them with savers and the savings bank at the market rate, rather than buying them. Mises then has nothing to object to but the maturity, and Keawe's figures, confirmed by the statistics bureau, are true in the strict sense.

## B. The Development Fund and capital

### MR-07: The fixed social return (MECHANISM)

**Mises.**
- Technology can say how to build a thing, but not whether it should be built: "Where there are no money prices, there are no such things as economic quantities" (XI §3).
- "Capital does not “beget” profit" (XV §9). A "normal rate of profit" is absurd, and profit comes only from foresight: "The only source from which an entrepreneur's profits stem is his ability to anticipate better than other people the future demand of the consumers" (XV §8).
- The only proof that capital was well placed is a surplus of proceeds over costs (XXVI §4).

**Pacifica.**
- CR-020 B8 gives every Fund dollar the same return. Each $1 invested raises next year's output by $0.30, capped at 1.2 points of growth a year.
- The Development stock is $17.7bn in 1929, about a quarter of GDP. Its return never falls as the stock grows, never depends on whether anyone uses what was built, and never includes a failure.
- CR-055 is the counter-example inside canon: the early Columbia earned nothing until its four purposes were found.

**Mechanism (dynamic layer).** Replace the constant with a return that:
1. is measured **over** the private return the same money would have earned (MR-08);
2. falls as the Development stock rises relative to GDP;
3. scales with utilization, which the energy workbook already computes for power (spill);
4. carries a write-off share for failed credits.

The Story case can still reach about two times US output per person, but by named projects that worked, not by a coefficient.

**In-world answer (WORDING, PROPOSED).** The Fund lends at market rates, publishes its accounts in the Office of Public Measures' tables, and writes off its losses in public. Where results can't be priced, as with the Polytechnic and the research institutes, it says so and books them as spending. Mises half-concedes this case: "It is very probable that technological progress would be seriously retarded if, for the inventor and for those who defray the expenses incurred by his experimentation, the results obtained were nothing but external economies" (XXIII §6). This fits the examination state's habit of published numbers.

### MR-08: What the money would have done elsewhere (MECHANISM)

**Mises.** "What the government spends more, the public spends less" (XXIII §6), whether the money comes "from the taxpayers' spending and investing power or from the loan market." The harm lies in the projects that never appear, "the possibilities that the government has not allowed to come into existence." In XXXVI §2: "one dollar cannot be spent twice".

**Pacifica.** The Fund draws on two sources:
- 70% of every budget surplus. On Mises's test this is taxation above what running the state needs (XXIX §2).
- Development bonds, which draw on the same savings private industry would borrow.

The model adds the Fund's output to the trend path. It does not subtract the return those taxes and savings would have earned in private hands. The investment account already fixes total investment by the `inv_share` path, so the Fund displaces private investment one for one in the account but not in output. That is an inconsistency, not just a Misesian complaint.

**Mechanism.** Net the Fund's return against the displaced private return. A private gross return of about 8–12% (to calibrate) leaves the Fund's net effect at roughly two thirds of its gross. Either show that the Story case still clears its targets, or raise the gross return only for projects that can be named (MR-07).

### MR-09: The Columbia built early (MECHANISM; HOLDS in part)

**Mises.**
- His own example is a power station: "If a hydroelectric power station is to be built, one must know whether or not this is the most economical way to produce the energy needed" (XXVI §1).
- Capital goods built for the wrong purpose become idle or useless (XVIII §5).
- Infrastructure built into empty country reverses cause and effect: "The plight of the farmers is not caused by the fact that they lack transportation facilities. The causation is the other way round" (XXIII §6).
- A substitute that is dearer or worse than the good it replaces wastes scarce factors (XXXIV §3). But he concedes "it is possible to improve the state of war preparedness by resorting to the production of substitutes such as synthetic rubber" (XXXIV §3). He also allows that defence industries "must be subsidized, and the subsidies granted are to be considered as any other armaments expenditure" (XXIX §2).

**Pacifica.**
- **What holds.** CR-055 already met the objection head-on. The second draft found the early river spilling 1–4 TWh a year, and the author's question ("Why build the dams if you don't need them?") is Mises's question.
- **What remains.** The four purposes that filled the river are state- or champion-led: the line, hydrogenation, synthetic rubber and electric pig iron. Mises would ask whether they pay at market prices or whether the state built the supply and then built the demand.

**Mechanism.** Add a unit-cost test to the energy and financial workbooks (CR-055 open item 6 already lists the financing side) for each purpose:
- hydrogenated motor fuel against Gulf crude at the compact price;
- synthetic rubber against natural rubber, year by year. Under Stevenson (1922–28) the comparison may favour synthetic; after 1928 natural rubber collapses (prices to verify);
- electric pig iron against coke iron on Pacifican coal;
- the line's delivered power against Sierra generation.

Any purpose that fails is booked as defence spending, on Mises's own concession. Rubber after 1928 is the likely case and, in the story, the honest one: a strategic reserve, not a business.

### MR-10: Who loses when the Fund is wrong? (WORDING)

**Mises.** "But the manager cannot be made answerable for the losses incurred. These losses are suffered by the owners of the capital employed" (XV §10). "One cannot play speculation and investment" (XXVI §5). "The capitalist system is not a managerial system; it is an entrepreneurial system" (XXVI §5).

**Pacifica.**
- **The champions hold on this point.** The firms are private, the Crown holds a minority stake (25% or 8%), and owners carry three quarters or more of any loss.
- **The golden share** blocks only sales to foreigners (B9). That is narrow enough to stay short of what Mises calls the Hindenburg pattern, where firms' managers become "subject to the government, not to the consumers' demand" (XXVII §2). Canon should keep it that narrow.
- **The Fund's "credit for new industries" is the exposed part.** Its officers lend public money they don't own.

**Wording (PROPOSED).** The Fund lends only alongside private capital that takes the first loss, and never for more than half of a venture. Its officers' careers are tied to the published write-off record (MR-07).

## C. Industry, labour and land

### MR-11: Charters, patents and the oil compact (AUTHOR)

**Mises.**
- "The great monopoly problem mankind has to face today is not an outgrowth of the operation of the market economy. It is a product of purposive action on the part of governments" (XVI §6).
- Charters given to selected applicants are licence monopolies. "The owner of a patent enjoys a legal monopoly which, other conditions being propitious, can be used for the attainment of monopoly prices" (XVI §6).
- Privileges wear off, so "the eagerness of the law's pet children to acquire privileges is insatiable" (XXIX §3).
- Commodity agreements between governments aim at "world-market monopoly prices". An incomplete monopoly fails when outsiders won't cut their own sales (XVI §6).
- He concedes one case: limited-space monopolies (rights of way, canals, streets) really can earn monopoly prices.

**Pacifica.**
- B9's principle, "The Crown is a partner where it gives something up", splits under this reading:
  - the canal, the trans-Pacific route and rights of way are genuinely scarce, which is Mises's concession;
  - charters and licensed Polytechnic patents are scarce because the law makes them so.
- The 1932 oil compact is now a price floor (decided 4 November). It is the commodity agreement he condemns, and the case where outsiders matter.

**For the author.** This is a trade-off, not an error. Two things follow:
- The insatiability line is a ready-made political engine for the 1920s: champions lobbying the Council for more, the Union press calling the Crown share a toll.
- Pacifica's answer to the Stevenson restriction (CR-055 purpose 3) is Mises's incomplete-monopoly argument used against Britain. It would be worth a sentence in the confrontation dossier.

### MR-12: Who saves 28% of GDP? (MECHANISM)

**Mises.** "Additional capital can be accumulated only by saving, i.e., a surplus of production over consumption" (XVIII §7). Saving always takes the form of goods produced and not consumed (XVIII §9).

**Pacifica.**
- The investment account (`finance/CR-052-workbook.md`) imposes the investment share: Story 27–29% in the 1920s, against Kuznets's 20.6% for the US in 1919–28.
- It reads household consumption as the remainder: 65% of GDP in 1929, 2.1 times the US per head.
- Nothing derives the saving that pays for it.

**A rough reading of 1929.**
- Gross domestic investment is 28% of GDP. The current account (net income from abroad, about $1.5bn, plus net exports) adds a point or two, so national saving is about 30% of GDP.
- The budget supplies under three points (a $1.9bn balance).
- Households and firms must save the rest, about 27% of GDP. That is above any interwar economy and comparable to postwar Japan (both to verify).

It may be right for a country this rich, with a state savings bank (OPEN-020-09) and champions that retain earnings. But it has to be derived, not assumed.

**Mechanism (dynamic layer).** A saving account by sector:
- government (the surplus);
- firms (the champions' retained earnings and the Crown's dividend policy);
- households (a saving rate, with the savings bank's deposits).

Investment is then saving plus net capital inflow, and the household saving rate is read off and judged. This is the identity the dynamic layer's prices and wages hang on, so it should come first in the build order.

### MR-13: Wages, prices and daily life (MECHANISM)

**Mises.**
- "There prevails an inevitable tendency for wage rates to reach the point at which they are equal to the price of the marginal product of the kind of labor in question" (XXI §3).
- "In the long run every increase in productivity benefits exclusively the workers and some groups of the owners of land and of capital goods" (XV §9).
- A charge on employers proportional to headcount lowers output and raises prices (XXIX §3).

**Pacifica.**
- The dynamic layer exists to show what Pacifica's investment means for prices, wages and life on the page.
- Mises gives its central rule: productivity reaches the worker through wages and through prices.
- Under MR-01's rule it reaches him both ways: money wages rise somewhat and goods get cheaper.
- The guest-worker levy (25% of wages, B11) is designed so that a guest worker is not cheaper than a resident. Mises would read it as a migration barrier that protects residents' wages (XXI §9) and costs output. Canon's answer, Singapore's, is that it buys social peace. That is a fair trade for the author to keep.

**Mechanism.**
- The real wage is labour's share times output per worker; labour's share is about 0.6–0.7, to calibrate (Mankiw ch. 3).
- The money wage is the real wage times the price level from MR-01.
- Sector prices fall faster where Pacifica's productivity lead is real (investment-share note, item 3, still OPEN).

The output is a table the storyline can quote: what a machinist's week buys in 1913, 1922 and 1929 (a car, a month's power, a cinema seat), against the Union's.

### MR-14: Land held through the state (AUTHOR)

**Mises.**
- "Taxes levied upon land reduce its market price to the extent of the discounted amount of their future burden" (XXII §5).
- "fears concerning the total or partial confiscation of the net yield of land tend to lower the prices of land" (XXII §5).
- Where the user doesn't own the land or expects to lose it, he ignores the future (XXII §2).
- A fee for an exemption is the price of a licence (XXIII §6).

**Pacifica.**
- **The public register holds.** CR-033's register, "the entry is the title", is exactly what Mises would want, since it lowers the risk that gets capitalized.
- **The homelands will behave as Mises predicts.** They are inalienable and leasable only through the Republic for a fee, so the fee is capitalized and investment by lessees depends on the length and security of the lease. Long, renewable leases with compensation for improvements answer him. Short ones produce the neglect he describes.
- **Leasehold premiums** (B7) are capitalized rent, which he treats as ordinary.

**For the author.** This shapes what the homelands look like by 1932: prosperous where leases run long, neglected where they don't. It is a choice of terms, not a flaw.

### MR-15: War finance (HOLDS)

**Mises.**
- "If the government had raised all the funds needed for the conduct of war by taxing the citizens and by borrowing from them, everybody would have been forced to cut down his consumption drastically" (XXXIV §2). It was inflationary finance that made war controls necessary.
- Loans between governments "are for the most part the price paid for military assistance in coming wars" (XVIII §4).

**Pacifica.**
- **The financing holds.** CR-025 raised the Entente programme from Treasury, Pacifican private and foreign subscriptions, with Pacifican savers taking the remainder, not the Reserve Bank. Canon could add one guard: the Reserve Bank did not discount war bonds freely (the American "borrow and buy" of 1917–19).
- **The credits are leverage, not capital.** Mises's second point fits canon's use of the war debts: they are spent in the war, and their return is political (the 1919 limits, Britain's dependence), as storyline §30–§37 already treats them.

## D. What Mises concedes (Pacifica's defences)

- **State firms in a market can calculate** (XXVII §1). The Crown share, the canal and the power estate are not socialism.
- **The state can be one actor among others** (XVIII §7, note 16), and a government that invests successfully "is in the same position as any other businessman" (XII §5).
- **Bureaucratic management is right where results can't be priced**, as with police, and also, in his reading, coercive government generally (XV §10).
- **Research may need protection.** Without it, technological progress would very probably be "seriously retarded" (XXIII §6).
- **Defence industries may be subsidized**, booked as armaments (XXIX §2). Synthetic rubber for war readiness is acceptable (XXXIV §3).
- **Small expansions have small effects** (XVII, note 17; XX §8). Contraction leaves no lasting scars (XX §7).
- **Hoarding treasury surpluses in a boom is genuinely contracyclical** (XXXI §5). That is a description of the Reserve Fund.
- **The 1931–38 devaluations reduced unemployment for a time** (XXXI §4). That is the release Thalassia gives Japan's silk districts.
- **Foreign capital let Latin America and Asia build ahead of their own saving** (XVIII §4). That is the champions' role abroad.
- **"The gold standard was an efficacious check upon credit expansion"** (XX §8). Pacifica's gold is its discipline, as long as the cover test is honest (MR-03).

### MR-03 follow-up: the ratios and the guard against the government (PROPOSED)

**Ratios.**

| Liability | Minimum gold cover | Rest held in | Precedent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Partners' gold reserves | 100%, earmarked | none | Mises's money-certificates |
| Partners' official dollar balances | **50%** | prime commercial bills under 90 days | above the domestic ratio, because foreign official money is what runs (Britain, September 1931) |
| Pacifica's notes | **40%** | prime commercial bills | Federal Reserve Act 1913 (40% gold against notes) |
| Banks' deposits at the Reserve Bank | **35%** | prime commercial bills | Federal Reserve Act 1913 (35% against deposits) |

**Government paper never counts as cover**, Pacifican or foreign. The money block (part E) will show whether 50% on the partners' balances is affordable from Pacifica's gold. If it is not, the balances Pacifica accepts should be smaller; the ratio should not be lower.

**The guard.** Mises's warning is that cover laws are lifted "whenever a government deems that an emergency justifies" it (XVII §12). Peel's Act of 1844 was suspended in 1847, 1857 and 1866. No rule binds a determined government. Pacifica can make breaking the rule costly, slow and public:
1. **The ratios are in a treaty, not a statute.** The reserve compact with the partners fixes them. The partners' own money is at stake, and each holds a veto. The Council of State cannot lower them alone.
2. **The Treasury cannot borrow from the Bank.** The only exception is advances within the fiscal year, repaid by its end. The Bank's holdings of government paper are capped at its own capital, and the standing debt serves only as collateral (MR-06).
3. **A shortfall tightens automatically.** If cover falls below a floor, the discount rate rises by a fixed step each week until cover is restored. This is the Reichsbank's 1875 tax on uncovered notes, redirected. The Reichsbank's tax went to the Treasury, which would give the government a stake in over-issue. Here the penalty is a dearer rate, so the government never gains from a breach.
4. **A weekly return.** The Bank of England has published one since 1844. Pacifica's is printed by the Office of Public Measures and cabled to every partner capital.
5. **Unconditional convertibility.** Anyone can redeem at par, and partners can withdraw earmarked gold without notice. "There is but one means of keeping a local and national currency permanently at par … unconditional redemption" (XVII §16). Suspending convertibility is a default under the compact.
6. **Separation.** The Reserve Bank, the Treasury and the Fund (MR-08) are three institutions with three boards. The Bank never lends to the Fund or buys its paper. Its governors serve long terms and can be removed only for cause.

**The real guard is interest.** Pacifica's power rests on the dollar being the safest money. Storyline §34 says "the Union's savers learn which flag is safe", and the investors who hold Pacifican paper become a constituency for accepting the order. A government that diluted the cover would sell the order for a season's projects. The temptation comes in 1931–32, when the rescues and the Columbia's idle power both argue for printing, and Pacifica refuses. That refusal is a scene (OPEN).

### MR-08 follow-up: the Fund as a sovereign wealth fund (PROPOSED; changes CR-020 B8)

**Sources: rents, not taxes on work.**
- gold royalties (the Superintendency of 1852);
- oil royalties;
- canal and concession fees;
- lease premiums on public land;
- the Crown's dividends (see MR-11).

These are the Norwegian and Singaporean patterns: petroleum revenue for Norway, land sales and reserves for Singapore. Ordinary budget surpluses go to lower taxes and the Reserve Fund, not to the Fund. B8's "70% of every budget surplus" and the standing-debt bonds leave its sources.

**Investment: market returns, much of it abroad.**
- A diversified portfolio of bonds and equities, with a large share abroad, in the creditor position Pacifica already has. Norway invests its fund wholly abroad, so that it neither overheats the home economy nor becomes a political lender.
- At home, an arm on Temasek's lines holds commercial stakes on market terms. It invests only when a firm wants capital, and never with board control.

**Spending rule.**
- Each year the budget receives a fixed share of the Fund's value, earmarked for infrastructure, the Polytechnic and the research institutes. Propose 3–4% (MODEL). Norway's rule was 4% from 2001 and 3% from 2017; Singapore's allows up to half of expected long-term real returns (to verify).
- That transfer is what lets Pacificans pay lower taxes for better infrastructure.
- Infrastructure also earns its own way where it can: power at cost, tolls, and project bonds sold to savers (MR-06). Each project is judged by a published benefit–cost account.

**Performing well.**
- an independent board;
- a benchmark published in advance;
- annual returns at market value in the Office's tables;
- managers paid on excess over the benchmark across several years;
- losses written off in public (MR-07).

**Against Mises.** A fund financed from rents is not financed from taxes on work, and a portfolio invested at market returns passes his profit test (XXVI §4). What is left of his objection (MR-08) shrinks to the rents' alternative use: lower royalties would leave more in private miners' hands.

**Workbook.** This changes:
- the Development Fund's inflows (to rents);
- its return (the portfolio's market return, about 5–6% nominal, to calibrate);
- its output effect (only through the infrastructure the spending rule pays for, at MR-07's net return).

The Development stock ($17.7bn in 1929) and the tax burden both move, so it needs a rebuild and a re-test against the storyline's figures. Canon change: CR-020 B8 and B8's "What would reach China-like productivity" passage.

### MR-11 follow-up: a lighter Crown share and the SEZs (PROPOSED; changes CR-020 B9 and OPEN-020-10)

**Patents.**
- Polytechnic patents are licensed non-exclusively to any firm, on published modest royalties, with no equity condition.
- The state's return comes from the taxes and spillovers of the activity, not from the licence.
- Patents become abundant, not scarce. The Union's complaint becomes that Pacifica gives its inventions away, which is harder to campaign against.

**The Crown share.**
- Competitive industries: the 8% stake is dropped. The Temasek-like arm of the Fund (MR-08) may invest on commercial terms when a firm asks.
- The golden share stays only in a short published list of defence-critical firms (armour, propellants, the Works' suppliers), as a review of foreign takeovers.
- Chartered and concession industries: concessions are **auctioned**, and the Crown takes the winning fee, not equity. Competitive bidding gives the privilege its market price, which is the course Mises would accept (XVI §6).
- The Crown keeps equity only where the state owns the underlying asset: the canal and the trunk rights of way, the limited-space monopolies he concedes.

**SEZs.** China's zones worked because the rest of China was closed. Pacifica is already open, so at home a zone is about **speed and cheap inputs**, not opening:
- **Chartered industrial districts.** Candidates are the Columbia districts, the two leeward districts of the Thalassia Plan (B7) and the Los Angeles harbour. Each offers:
  - power at cost from the river;
  - serviced leasehold land;
  - licences within weeks;
  - a bonded customs area, so components enter free for re-export (Hong Kong's and Singapore's entrepôt pattern);
  - a Polytechnic training school;
  - a tax holiday for new plants (MODEL: five years), open to any firm that locates there, so it is a general rule, not a privilege for named favourites (XXIII §6).
- **Zones abroad, in the order.** Pacifican-run industrial parks in Korea, the Philippine crown, the Siberian Republic, the Gulf and China's treaty ports. Singapore's Suzhou Industrial Park (1994) is the later pattern. They carry the partners' catch-up (B14) and the champions' plants abroad, and they are where the anti-colonial undertow of the 1920s finds its target.

- The Crown's dividends ($170m in 1929 on the decided stakes) fall. Auction fees and the Fund's commercial returns replace part of them.
- The champions' attractiveness (talent, migration, B10) rises.

### MR-12 follow-up: saving incentives (PROPOSED)

1. **Falling prices are the first incentive.** Under MR-01 cash and gold-coupon bonds buy more each year, so even a low nominal rate is a positive real return.
2. **The savings bank** (OPEN-020-09, already in the narrative). Post-office branches everywhere; interest tax-free up to a ceiling. The precedents are Britain's Post Office Savings Bank (1861) and Japan's postal savings (1875).
3. **Small gold-coupon savings bonds** sold at post offices and workplaces, as the US War Savings Stamps of 1917–18 were sold.
4. **A provident fund at work (author's choice).** Employers and workers contribute to personal accounts, managed at market returns by the Fund's manager. Withdrawals are allowed for a leasehold home (MR-14) and for retirement. Singapore's CPF (1955) is the pattern; its money was managed by GIC.
   - It can be compulsory, as in Singapore, or voluntary with a tax deduction.
   - Compulsory saving is the most state-capitalist option here. It would also explain a private saving rate above any interwar economy's (MR-12).
5. **The same guard as MR-03 and MR-08.** Savings are invested at market returns against a published benchmark. They are never lent to the state's favourite projects below the market rate.
