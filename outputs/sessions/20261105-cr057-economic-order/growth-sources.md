# Growth design note: what the sources say (6 November 2026)

Four separate agents read the author's copies of the sources against the first draft of `finance/CR-057-growth-design-note.md`. Their quotations are summarized here. Page numbers are printed pages. The scripts are in `growth-scripts/`:

## Hall and Jones (1999), QJE 114: 83–116

- "Output per worker in the five countries in 1988 with the highest levels of output per worker was 31.7 times higher than output per worker in the five lowest countries … differences in capital intensity and human capital per worker contributed factors of 1.8 and 2.2, respectively … Productivity, however, contributed a factor of 8.3" (p. 92).
- α = ⅓ (p. 89).
- Human capital is years of schooling, at returns of 13.4%, 10.1% and 6.8% (p. 89).
- Social infrastructure: "the institutions and government policies that determine the economic environment within which individuals accumulate skills, and firms accumulate capital and produce output" (p. 84). It is "the primary, fundamental determinant of a country's long-run economic performance" (p. 95).
- "national boundaries do not limit the areas where ideas are applied" (p. 113).

## Wright (1936), JAS 3: 122–128

- The 80% curve (exponent 0.322) is the "factor by which the average labor cost in any quantity shall be multiplied in order to determine the average labor cost for a quantity of twice that number of airplanes" (pp. 124–125).
- Raw material follows a 95% curve, purchased material 88% and overhead 70% (p. 125).
- The whole airplane "will start out at eighty-three percent, then change to eighty-five percent, then change to eighty-seven percent, and finally reach ninety percent" (p. 126).
- The curve was observed up to about 150–200 units. On 100,000 units: "a figure so out of keeping with experience to date" (p. 127).
- The supplier "is the supplier to many airplane manufacturers and thus is constantly manufacturing in greater quantities" (p. 125).

## Jones and Williams (1998), QJE 113: 1119–1135

- "a conservative estimate of the social return of 30 percent" (p. 1121).
- "optimal R&D investment is at least two to four times actual investment" (abstract, p. 1119).
- The pooled regression gives 0.37, or 0.35 with controls; US industries, 1961–89 (p. 1131).
- The return is r̃ = λ·g/s, inversely proportional to research intensity (eq. 14, p. 1130). At the optimum it falls to the interest rate, about 7% (p. 1133).
- "Stepping on toes" duplication and "fishing out" (p. 1125).
- "estimated TFP growth accounted for by R&D is then r̃ R/Y" (p. 1128). "zero depreciation is somewhat standard" (fn 9, p. 1128).

## Hall, Mairesse and Mohnen (2010), CIRANO 2010s-02 / NBER w15622

- Abstract: "In general, the private returns to R&D are strongly positive and somewhat higher than those for ordinary capital, while the social returns are even higher, although variable and imprecisely measured in many cases."
- Private returns "more likely to be in the 20% to 30% range" (p. 22).
- Case studies: "the median social rate of return is about 56% against a median private rate of return of about 25%" (p. 25).
- Depreciation: "most researchers use the 15 per cent that Griliches had settled on" (p. 15). Estimates range from 10% to 36%.
- Output elasticity of R&D: "0.01 to 0.25 but centered on 0.08" (p. 21). Country-level elasticities are biased upward (p. 28).
- Lags: "a mean lag from 4 to 6 years". Adams finds 20 years for own R&D and 10–30 for basic research (p. 16).
- Leakage: "roughly a quarter of the benefits from R&D in G-7 countries accrues to their trading partners" (p. 30).
- Absorption: "It depends of course on the absorption capacity of the receiver" (p. 31).
- Publicly funded R&D shows lower returns (pp. 19, 32).
- "the measurement process is not a search for a 'scientific constant.'" (p. 3).

## Mowery and Rosenberg (1998), *Paths of Innovation*

- **Research scientists and engineers in manufacturing labs:** 2,775 in 1921, 6,274 in 1927, 10,918 in 1933, 27,777 in 1940 and 45,941 in 1946 (Table 1, pp. 21–22).
- **Per 1,000 production workers:**

| Industry | 1921 | 1927 | 1933 | 1940 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Chemicals | 5.2 | 6.52 | 12.81 | 27.81 |
| Electrical | 1.11 | 2.86 | 8.06 | 13.18 |

- **Federal spending:** 12–20% of US R&D in the 1930s; $74.1m in 1940 (p. 27).
- **In-house research and outside knowledge:** the aircraft industry's own R&D "has been essential to the absorption and application of technologies developed outside its boundaries" (p. 69). In-house and contract research "were complements rather than substitutes" (p. 13, n. 4).
- **Haber-Bosch:** "U.S. experts could not replicate the Haber-Bosch process" despite the 1918 expropriation of BASF's patents (p. 75).
- **Development:** "Roughly two thirds of U.S. R&D investment constitutes 'D'" (p. 173).
- **Electric power:**
  - coal per kWh fell from almost 7 lb in 1900 to under 0.9 lb by the 1960s (p. 117);
  - household power fell from 7.45 to 6.03 cents a kWh, 1920–30 (p. 105);
  - electric motors were 25.4% of mechanical horsepower in 1909, 55.0% in 1919 and 82.3% in 1929 (Table 9, p. 116).
- **Chemicals:** organic chemicals not made from coal tar fell from 43 to 13 cents a lb, 1921–39 (p. 80).
- **Aircraft:** cost per seat-mile was 2.63 cents (Ford Trimotor, 1928), 2.11 (Boeing 247, 1933) and 1.27 (DC-3, 1936) (Table 3, p. 63).

## Moser (2013), JEP 27(1): 23–44

- Without patent laws, innovation "focused on a narrow set of industries in which secrecy was effective" (p. 30).
- Patent laws "do not appear to be a necessary or sufficient condition for higher rates of innovation" (p. 33).
- "In countries with patent laws, the majority of innovations occur outside of the patent system" (p. 40). Shares patented: 19–20% of US chemical innovations (1893, 1915) and 44–49% in machinery (p. 31).
- Chemical Foundation licences from 1919: "a 20 percent increase in domestic patenting … with a lag of eight to nine years" (p. 38).
- Licensees "typically cannot access the uncodified knowledge" (p. 39).
- Patents are an effective protection only "for pharmaceuticals and chemical inventions" (p. 31).
- The pool's unequal fees "diverted the research investments of outside firms towards technologically inferior substitutes" (p. 37).

## Online abstracts

- Bom and Ligthart (2014), *J. Econ. Surveys* 28: 889–916: "a short-run output elasticity of public capital supplied at the central government level of 0.083, which increases to 0.122 in the long run"; for core regional infrastructure, "almost doubled".

- Lieberman (1984), *RAND J.* 15: 213–228: across 37 chemical products, learning is "a function of cumulated industry output and cumulated investment rather than calendar time".
