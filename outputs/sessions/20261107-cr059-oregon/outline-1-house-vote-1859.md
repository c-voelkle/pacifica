# CR-059 (outline) — Oregon and the Northwest: the admission that fails, the compact and the choice (1849–1866)

**Status:** Outline for the author, 6 November 2026. Not a CR draft yet. Every decision below is a proposal. Vote figures for the alternate roll call are PROPOSED. Motives given to historical persons must be checked against their speeches and letters before drafting (see Research tasks).

**Affects:** master storyline §§8–12 (new Oregon passages in §8, the §10 Whiteaker paragraph, §12's Oregon article); `historical_background.md` (the historical roll call and the divergence); `character_registry.json` (new historical persons); `commitments/data/ledger.toml` (the 1861 civil compact, the 1866 accession charter).

**Depends on:** canon as of `main` 221ed91: the sectional lock (CR-028), the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention and its twelve-year articles, the 1855 panic (CR-034), assisted passages from 1852 (CR-034), the Confederation of 1856, the Defense Law of 1855.

## What this CR does

On 12 February 1859 the House of Representatives passed the bill admitting Oregon by 114 to 103. In this timeline it fails, 106 to 109. This CR makes that result a **consequence of an independent California and of California's Oregon policy**, and puts the chain on the page.

Six votes change. Each has a named holder, a reason and a link back to California. Two more votes are simply missing, because California sends no one to Congress. Everything later in the CR follows from the failure:
- the deadlock of 1859–60;
- the Enabling Act of 1861 that the war overtakes;
- the occupation compact;
- the vote of 1866.

The irony the story can carry: the man who argues hardest in 1859 that Oregon must stay a territory to stay American is John Sherman. In 1867 he is the senator who helps cede it (CR-015 A5).

## Part 1 · The causal chain

### California's changed status: four structural facts by 1859

| # | Fact (canon) | What it changes about Oregon's admission |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | California never joins the Union (1846–48; sectional lock, CR-028) | Its two House members and two senators, all Democrats who historically voted for Oregon, are absent. |
| S2 | No free state is admitted in 1850 | Senate parity of fifteen to fifteen survives from 1850 until Minnesota in May 1858. Oregon would be the second free state in nine months, with Kansas waiting. |
| S3 | The Confederation of Pacifica (1856): a sovereign legislature, Hawaii's, votes itself into a Pacific union. Querétaro forbids California to join the United States but not to admit others (CR-039 comment) | The "Pacific republic" is no longer loose talk, as it was historically before 1860–61. It has a capital, a treasury, a conscript army (Defense Law, 1855) and a precedent for accession by a legislature's vote. |
| S4 | The United States' only sovereign Pacific harbours are in Oregon and Washington; its squadron provisions at Sausalito under a revocable privilege that expires with the convention in 1860 | The Columbia and Puget Sound are strategic in a way they never were historically. Who governs them matters. |

### California's Oregon policy, 1849–1859

This is the second half of the chain. It needs its own passage in §8, because canon has not written it yet.

**OR-P1 · Market.** California publishes its duties and wharf charges. It allows bonded onward shipment through San Francisco. Its public yards (Carquinez, Mare Island) buy Oregon lumber, spars and coal on published contracts. Oregon's flour and salmon sell in a foreign port, under a foreign tariff, for foreign coin.

**OR-P2 · Money.** Oregon circulates Californian gold coin. Southern Oregon's placer gold is assayed by the State Assay Office in San Francisco. There is no United States mint on the coast.

**OR-P3 · Credit.** In the Page, Bacon panic of February 1855, the Superintendency's credit carries sound Portland bills along with San Francisco's houses (an extension of CR-034). Portland's merchants owe their survival to Monterey's treasury, not to New York. Elias knows exactly what a foreign creditor can do to a debtor. He extends the credit anyway, and on commercial terms.

**OR-P4 · People.** From 1852 the Superintendency pays the passage of single women and whole families (CR-034), and Pacifica's family land law copies Oregon's Donation Land Claim Act without its racial bar (CR-015 I2). The donation claims close in 1855. Pacifica keeps paying. Emigrants who might have gone to the Willamette go to the Sacramento.

**OR-P5 · Restraint.** Elias forbids every political approach to Oregon: no agents, no subsidies to newspapers, no talk of association. Querétaro, the Buchanan–Vallejo Convention and the sectional lock all depend on Washington believing that California covets nothing north of the 42nd parallel.

**OR-P6 · The renewal note (January 1859).** The convention's commercial articles expire in 1860. The Confederal Council's chargé in Washington opens renewal talks with a note listing the produce to be traded on bonded terms and the port charges at Astoria and Portland. **[PROPOSED]** Elias knows that the House is about to debate Oregon. He could hold the note for a month, and he does not. Whether that was intent is left open on the page.

Under OR-P5 Pacifica does nothing *to* Oregon's admission. Under OR-P1–P4 it has done a great deal *about* Oregon. The House debate of 1859 is where the two meet.

### The chain, step by step

1. **S1** removes two Democratic votes: 114–103 becomes 112–103.
2. **S2** gives the balance argument, which the Deep South used historically, a reality it had lost in 1850. Two Mississippi Democrats change their votes (A).
3. **S3 + OR-P1–P3 + OR-P6** create the security argument: a state with Lane's legislature, Monterey's credit and a working precedent for leaving cannot be governed from Washington, but a territory can. John Sherman makes it, with his brother's letters from Pacifica and the renewal note in his hand. Three Ohio Republicans change their votes (B).
4. **OR-P4** gives a free-soil Republican a population argument of his own: Oregon is losing the race for settlers to a foreign republic and should be filled before it is admitted. One Massachusetts Republican changes his vote (C).
5. **S4** turns the debate's aftermath into appropriations for the Columbia and Puget Sound. Those works later become Casey's Port Townsend foothold in 1861 (canon §10).

## Part 2 · The vote of 12 February 1859

### Historical baseline (for `historical_background.md`)

The House passed S. 239 by **114 to 103** (House Journal vol. 55, p. 398; Voteview RH0350454; the Oregon History Project gives 114–108, a discrepancy to note).

| Party | Yea | Nay |
|---|---|---|
| Democrats | 98 | 20 |
| Republicans | 15 | 71 |
| Americans | 0 | 11 |
| Independent Democrats | 1 | 1 |

Free-state members split 65–73; slave-state members 49–30.

Schuyler Colfax wrote that the bill would have failed without the fifteen Republicans, himself among them (Bancroft). The Republican objections were:
- the exclusion of free Black people, which John A. Bingham and Henry L. Dawes attacked;
- alien suffrage;
- above all, Kansas: Congress had refused Kansas admission under the English bill until it reached the population ratio (about 93,000), and Oregon had about half that.

Some Southern members voted no because Oregon would be free, or for consistency with the English bill. Alexander Stephens managed the bill. In 1858 Lane had had Stephens hold back the committee report until the timing suited him (Bancroft).

The fifteen Republican yeas were Kilgore, Colfax, Case and Pettit (Indiana); Curtis (Iowa); Wood and Foster (Maine); Comins and Thayer (Massachusetts); Cragin (New Hampshire); Nichols, Horton and Leiter (Ohio); Kunkel (Pennsylvania); Billinghurst (Wisconsin). California's Charles L. Scott and Joseph C. McKibbin, both Democrats, voted yea.

### Alternate result: 106 yeas, 109 nays

| Change | Members | Historical | Here | Net |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absent | Scott, McKibbin (California) | Yea | not members | −2 yea |
| A · balance | L. Q. C. Lamar, William Barksdale (Mississippi, D) | Yea | Nay | −2 yea, +2 nay |
| B · security | Valentine B. Horton, Matthias H. Nichols, Benjamin F. Leiter (Ohio, R; all three leaving Congress in March) | Yea | Nay | −3 yea, +3 nay |
| C · population race | Eli Thayer (Massachusetts, R) | Yea | Nay | −1 yea, +1 nay |
| **Total** | | **114–103** | **106–109** | |

The margin of three is deliberate. A one-vote defeat invites the question why Stephens did not find one absentee. Colfax holds eleven of his fifteen.

### Who changed their mind and why

**A · Lamar and Barksdale: the balance.** Historically the balance of the sections died in 1850 with California, and the Deep South had nine years to accept it. Here it lived on. From 1850 to May 1858 the Senate stood at fifteen free states to fifteen slave, and Southern men called that the Compromise's real guarantee, the reward for having refused a free California. Minnesota broke it in May 1858. Oregon would make the gap two within nine months, with Kansas certain to follow.

Lamar and Barksdale voted for Oregon historically out of party loyalty to Lane and Stephens. Here they hear the argument the Alabama and South Carolina members were already making, now backed by a fact eight years old: the South kept its equality because California stayed out, and it should not give in nine months what California's independence preserved. The English bill gives them a respectable form: no state below the population ratio, whichever side it serves. **[PROPOSED]**

*Link to California: S1, S2.*

**B · Horton, Nichols and Leiter: the security argument.** Ohio's Republicans led the opposition historically: Bingham on the exclusion clause, Stanton and John Sherman against Lane's Lecompton record. Here Sherman adds an argument nobody could make in our history.

He moves a resolution calling for the correspondence with the Confederation. Buchanan, who signed the convention of 1848 as Secretary of State, transmits the renewal note. Sherman reads its schedule of Oregon produce and Astoria port charges to the House. He uses his brother William's letters from Monterey: a republic that drills every young man, owns the mint Oregon's coin comes from, carried Portland's banks in 1855 and in 1856 took a kingdom into its confederation by a vote of that kingdom's legislature.

His case:
- Admit Oregon now and you give a Lecompton delegate, who advised Oregon in 1858 to act as a state without waiting for Congress, a legislature, a governor and a militia, beside a foreign republic that holds its purse and has shown how a legislature can leave.
- A territory is governed from Washington. Its governor and judges are federal appointees, Congress may annul its laws, and its forts are federal ground.
- Keep Oregon a territory until it has a constitution Republicans can accept and an administration they trust. Republicans expect to win in 1860.

Horton, Nichols and Leiter had voted for Oregon because it was a free state. The security argument lets them vote with their delegation without voting against a free state. As members leaving Congress, they owe nothing to Oregon's Democrats. **[PROPOSED]**

*Link to California: S3, S4, OR-P1–P3, OR-P6, and William Sherman's presence in Pacifica (canon §8).*

**C · Thayer: the population race.** Eli Thayer built the Emigrant Aid Company to win Kansas by settlement. He voted for Oregon historically because it was free. Here he reads the Superintendency's passenger returns. Pacifica pays the passage of women and families and grants married couples land on Oregon's own terms without a colour bar, while Oregon's donation claims closed in 1855.

His argument: Oregon is half the size the English bill demands of Kansas because a foreign treasury is buying its emigrants. Admit it now and it enters as Lane's state of fifty thousand. Fill it first with free labour, then admit it. He proposes an Oregon emigrant society on the Kansas model, which never raises its capital. **[PROPOSED]**

*Link to California: OR-P4.*

### Arguments heard more than they were historically

| Argument | Historically | Here | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sectional balance | Residual after 1850 | Decisive for two votes | Parity survived until 1858 (S2) |
| A Pacific republic | Speculation, mostly in 1860–61 | Central | It exists and has an accession precedent (S3) |
| The ripe-fruit doctrine (Polk, Buchanan: California will come of itself) | The Union's working policy toward California | Discredited | The fruit fell the other way in 1856: Hawaii joined Monterey |
| Dependence on a foreign treasury | Absent; San Francisco was American | A named fear | OR-P1–P3, the renewal note |
| Population race | Kansas consistency only | Kansas consistency plus Pacifica's passages | OR-P4 |
| Partisan timing (wait for a Republican president) | Present | Stronger, because it now has a security argument as cover | B |

### Arguments heard less

**"Admit to bind."** Colfax and Stephens argue that refusal will push Oregon toward Monterey and that statehood is the surest tie. It is the right argument, and the story should let the reader see that in 1866. It loses in 1859 because its vehicle is Lane, the Lecompton man.

**Oregon's war claims.** Oregon's advocates promised that statehood would bring payment of the Indian war debts. That argument counts for less once the debate turns to loyalty.

## Part 3 · Draft storyline passage (§8, after the 1858 paragraph)

> **[PROPOSED — CR-059]** California's Oregon policy is commerce and nothing else. The Republic publishes its duties, carries Oregon's flour, salmon and lumber in bond through San Francisco and buys spars and coal for its yards at Carquinez and Mare Island on published contracts. Oregon pays its debts in Californian coin, and in the panic of 1855 the Superintendency's credit carries Portland's sound houses with San Francisco's. Elias forbids anything more: no agents, no newspapers, no word of association. The Republic's treaties with Washington rest on its coveting nothing north of the 42nd parallel. In 1852 it begins paying the passage of women and families and grants married couples land on terms copied from Oregon's donation law, without the colour bar. Oregon's donation claims close in 1855. The Superintendency keeps paying.
>
> In 1857 Oregon votes for a constitution that bans slavery and excludes free Black people, and in June 1858 it elects Whiteaker governor and a legislature that chooses Lane and Smith as its senators, in anticipation of admission. The Senate passes the bill in May. The House takes it up in February 1859, when the Republic's chargé has just opened talks to renew the commercial articles of 1848 with a note listing Oregon's produce and the port charges at Astoria and Portland. Elias could have held the note for a month and did not.
>
> John Sherman of Ohio moves for the correspondence, and Buchanan, who signed the convention of 1848, sends it. Sherman reads it to the House with his brother's letters from Monterey: a republic that drills every young man, coins Oregon's money, carried Portland's banks through the panic and in 1856 took a kingdom into its confederation by a vote of that kingdom's own legislature. Lane, Sherman says, advised Oregon to act as a state without waiting for Congress. Admit it now and he gets a legislature and a militia beside a foreign treasury that has shown how a legislature leaves. Keep it a territory, governed from Washington, until it has a constitution Congress can accept. Three of his Ohio colleagues who meant to vote for a free state vote with him. Eli Thayer, who won Kansas by emigration, argues that Oregon is small because a foreign treasury is buying its settlers, and that it should be filled before it is admitted. Lamar and Barksdale of Mississippi, who meant to stand by Lane, remember that the South held the Senate's balance for eight years because California stayed out, and will not give away the second free state in nine months.
>
> Without California's two members the bill would have passed by nine votes. It fails by three, 106 to 109. Colfax holds eleven of the fifteen Republicans who would have carried it and tells the House that it has just sent Oregon to Monterey. Stephens leaves Congress in March without the admission that was to have been his last achievement. Oregon keeps a governor-elect, a legislature and two senators-designate, none with an office.

## Part 4 · Other effects of the failed vote

**Congress, 1859–60**
- With the bill dead at the end of the 35th Congress, Republicans in the 36th offer admission only under a new constitution without the exclusion clause. The Democratic Senate refuses. **[PROPOSED]**
- The debate produces appropriations for fortifications and a depot at the Columbia bar and Port Townsend, because the Sausalito privilege is revocable and Puget Sound is the Union's only sovereign anchorage. These works give Casey his foothold in 1861 (canon §10). **[PROPOSED]**
- The renewal talks of 1859–60 keep Oregon in the papers. Buchanan's demand for a stronger Sausalito privilege is refused, and the Carson Convention of autumn 1860 renews only the commercial articles (canon CR-028).

**Oregon**
- The limbo of December 1858, when the state legislature's session was cancelled and the territorial legislature carried on (Hist.), becomes permanent.
- Bush's *Statesman* blames Lane's Lecompton record for the defeat. The Salem Clique's historical break with Lane in 1859–60 comes earlier and harder. **[PROPOSED]**
- Lane's delegate term ends in March 1859. He is still Breckinridge's running mate in 1860 (Hist.), now as the man whose state was refused.
- Oregon casts no electoral votes in 1860. Lincoln still wins comfortably without Oregon's three and California's four.
- The unpaid war claims stay a federal promise. Pacifica, which refused in 1851 to pay for volunteer expeditions against Native villages (CR-033), will not buy the scrip. Oregon's hope of payment therefore stays tied to Washington. **[PROPOSED]**

**Pacifica**
- Elias orders official silence. In Portland the merchants who owe their survival to 1855 note that Congress has named them as the reason.
- Victoria, newly married (November 1858), reads the debate as proof that commerce alone is a policy. In 1861 she will insist that the compact is honoured. **[PROPOSED]**

**The bookend**
- John Sherman's argument of 1859 is the one he must answer in 1867 (CR-D).
