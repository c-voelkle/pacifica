# CR-059 (outline, part 2) — The Oregon vote of October 1866: the question, the offers and the choice

**Status:** Outline for the author, 7 November 2026. It continues `CR-059-outline-oregon.md` (decision OR-7) and replaces plan v2's assumption that both offers repeal the exclusion clause. Everything here is PROPOSED unless marked Hist. Vote shares are MODEL.

**The hinge, in one sentence.** The Union can offer Oregon statehood only without its exclusion clause, because Senate Republicans will not drop the condition. Pacifica offers Oregon what Stephen Douglas promised and the Union took away: popular sovereignty over who may live in Oregon. A floor of confederal rights limits that power, and the floor costs Oregon things it also wanted.

I assume "the 1855 constitution" in your note means Oregon's constitution of 1857, drafted at Salem in August–September and ratified on 9 November 1857.

## 2 · The question: who frames it, and what it is

Three bodies frame the vote, each at a different level.

### Layer 1 · The treaty's Oregon article (late 1865): what the vote decides

McClellan's commissioners and Pacifica write the legal effect.

| Term | Who wanted it | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Oregon's cession takes effect only if a majority of votes cast approve accession** | Washington | A vote lets the Union lose Oregon without signing it away. Senators can tell their voters that Oregon left by its own choice. Elias wanted outright cession; his army holds Astoria. |
| **An absolute majority on a single question**, not a plurality among three options | Victoria and William Sherman, against Elias | Elias's first draft offers three options: accession, statehood, territory. Plurality would split the Union vote and could carry accession on 40 percent. Victoria: "An Oregon vote Washington can call contrived is worth less in the Senate than a narrow clean one." Elias's own count says he can win a majority, so he gives way. |
| **The electorate:** qualified electors under the Union's Oregon Enabling Act of 1861 (white male citizens, and white male aliens who have declared their intention, aged 21 and resident six months), resident before the armistice of spring 1865 and for a year before the vote; no one in Pacifican service | Compromise | Washington asked for the rolls of June 1861. Pacifica asked for every resident. The armistice date admits wartime migrants, including Union refugees, but not anyone Pacifica could ship in after the peace. |
| **Absent voters:** Oregonians serving with Union forces and displaced Unionists vote at Port Townsend or by sworn ballot, on the model of the absent-soldier provisions of 1864 | Washington | Equal standing for the loyal. |
| **A joint protocol:** county officials run the polls; garrisons confined to post on polling day; observers from both governments; a three-member returns commission (Union, Pacifican, and Matthew Deady presiding) publishes returns county by county | Both | — |
| **Both offers published in full** before the campaign | Both | — |
| **Either result:** residents' property and choice of citizenship protected; Columbia navigation guaranteed to Pacifica if Oregon stays, transit to the Union if it goes | Both | — |

### Layer 2 · The convention (July–August 1866): what the ballot says

The delegates elected in June 1861 under the Union's own Enabling Act reconvene at Salem, and the June 1866 election fills vacancies. Washington cannot call them Pacifica's creation. Deady presides, as he did in 1857.

The convention drafts **one constitution for the State of Oregon**, essentially the 1857 text, with two schedules: one for entry into the Union under the Enabling Act, one for accession under the Charter. Following 1857's precedent of submitting the slavery and exclusion articles separately, it submits **two questions**:

1. **"Shall the State of Oregon enter the Confederation of Pacifica on the terms of the Charter of Accession?"** — *For accession / For the United States.*
   - "For the United States" keeps Oregon a U.S. territory. Its constitution then goes to Congress under the Enabling Act.
2. **"Shall Article XVIII (the exclusion of free negroes and mulattoes), as limited by the Charter, form part of the Constitution?"** — *Yes / No.*

The printed ballot carries a note required by the convention's majority: **Article XVIII takes effect only if Oregon enters the Confederation, because the Enabling Act forbids it.** Lane's delegates insist on the separate article and the note. The Union Democrats cannot strike either without looking like Republicans. Bush wants the issue kept off the ballot and loses.

### Layer 3 · What the three outcomes really are

| Ballot | Result |
|---|---|
| Accession | Oregon becomes a member state under its entrenched charter when the treaty is exchanged in 1867. Article XVIII stands if approved, as limited. |
| United States, exclusion approved | Congress will reject a constitution with the clause, so Oregon **remains a territory** until it votes again without it. |
| United States, exclusion rejected | Oregon goes to Congress for statehood under the Enabling Act. |

**So is it Pacifica, the Union or remaining a territory?** All three outcomes exist, but the October ballot decides only between the two unions. "Remain a territory" is what a Union vote means for anyone who also wants exclusion, and the ballot makes that visible. For the Willamette Democrat that note is the whole campaign.

## 3 · Why the Union cannot match the offer

The Enabling Act of 2 March 1861 forbids slavery and exclusion (CR-059 part 1, OR-5). In spring 1866 the House, with a Democratic majority after McClellan's election (composition OPEN), passes a bill dropping the exclusion condition to win Oregon. **Senate Republicans kill it in committee.**

Three Republican motives, all continuous with the 1859 debate:

- **Principle.** Sumner, Wade and Bingham's old cause: no free state may exclude free Black people.
- **Party.** Oregon would bring two Democratic senators to a Senate where Republicans hold the line against McClellan.
- **Riddance.** Privately, some senators who voted against Oregon in 1859 would rather lose a Democratic exclusion state than admit one. John Sherman's argument of 1859, keep Oregon a territory because it cannot be trusted as a state, becomes "let Oregon choose." He votes to keep the condition.

So the Union's offer stands as written: statehood without the clause, a restoration programme, payment of the Indian war claims, the Homestead Act. Its weakness was chosen by its own Senate. The same calculation makes the cession easier to ratify in April 1867 (CR-D).

**A further charge.** The Thirteenth Amendment failed in this timeline (canon §12), so Kentucky and Delaware keep slavery inside the Union of 1866. Oregon's Democrats can say: the Union that asks us to admit free Black people still keeps slaves. (Check: the status of border-state slavery in 1866 is not fixed in canon.)

## 4 · The exclusion bargain and its limits

### Why Pacifica can offer it: the Hawaiian clause

The Articles of 1856 leave to the member states everything not given to the common institutions, including residence, land tenure and the franchise. **[PROPOSED]** Hawaii insisted on this in 1855–56. Victoria and Wyllie would not let Californian settlers buy their way across Hawaiian land the way Americans had bought their way into the kingdom's politics. Each member decides who may settle among its people.

Oregon's exclusion clause rests on the clause Victoria wrote to protect Hawaiians. She cannot strike Oregon's power without striking her own.

### What the confederal floor still does

The floor is negotiated in the convention's charter committee and settled in Hawaii's ratifying session (§5).

| Floor | Effect in Oregon | Canon basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Marriage by race or origin cannot be forbidden** | Voids Oregon's 1862 marriage ban. No 1866 Act | CR-015 I2 (APPROVED) |
| **No member state may impose a tax or civil disability on a citizen of another member state by race or origin** | Article XVIII cannot reach Black Californians or Hawaiians. It binds only Black people who are not Pacifican citizens: migrants from the Union and the Confederacy. The 1862 poll tax falls for confederal citizens | New, on the common-market principle |
| **No penal law touches a contract of employment** | Article XVIII's instruction to punish whoever "employs or harbors" an excluded person is struck. Removal by public officers remains | CR-038 (Hawaii's penal contracts) |
| **Property, contract and access to the courts are confederal rights in the common market** | The Chinese real estate and mining ban falls for Chinese Pacificans and for Chinese subjects under the China treaty | Common market; I3 |
| **Residents protected** | Everyone lawfully resident at accession may stay, whatever their color; the 1857 clause already exempted residents | Treaty |
| **Franchise is the member state's** | Oregon may still deny the vote to Black and Chinese men, as it decides | Residual powers |

What Oregon keeps in practice: a border against Black migrants from the United States and the Confederacy, and control of its own franchise. What it loses: its marriage ban, its poll tax on Pacifican citizens, its Chinese property and mining ban, and the penal clause against employers.

### Why Elias accepts

- Oregon is the price of the Columbia and of a Union gone from the Pacific. A clause that has never been enforced and touches 128 people, all of them protected, is cheap.
- Residence belongs to the members, and he cannot change that without reopening Hawaii's charter.
- He expects the member states' powers to wither under his county policy from 1865 (canon §12). He says so to no one.
- His own principle, that service and ability count more than race, gives way as it does with the fugitive agreement. The pattern is canon.

### Why Victoria signs

She has two levers. Hawaii must ratify the charter as a constitutional amendment, and she enforced the compact in 1861. She can block Oregon. She would then block the end of the Western war and prove that Hawaii's own residence power is a fraud.

She takes the limits instead. The exemption of member-state citizens, which covers her own subjects, is her doing. She knows that the people left unprotected are the ones with no state behind them. **[PROPOSED]** This is her bitterest compromise before 1887.

### Why the protections fall where they do

Each protection follows a constituency with leverage:
- **Chinese:** the China partnership and California's Chinese community.
- **Hawaiians:** Victoria.
- **Black Californians** (about four thousand): a petition. **[PROPOSED]** Mifflin Wistar Gibbs, who historically left San Francisco for Victoria in 1858 because of California's discriminatory laws, stays in a California that has none. He sends the Council a petition from the Colored Convention in 1866. It wins the member-citizen exemption its due prominence and nothing more.
- **Black Americans** outside Pacifica have no state behind them.

The book can let the reader see this without a narrator's verdict.

## 5 · Sequence, 1865–1866

| When | Event |
|---|---|
| Late 1865 | Western treaty signed with the Oregon article (CR-D) |
| Jan–Apr 1866 | House passes the amendment dropping the exclusion condition; Senate Republicans kill it. The Union's offer is fixed |
| Spring 1866 | Charter committee of the convention's leaders (Nesmith, Deady, a Portland merchant) negotiates with the Confederal government at Monterey |
| June 1866 | Oregon fills convention vacancies at its regular June election. The Confederal chambers pass the charter as an amendment by two-thirds, conditional on Oregon's vote |
| Summer 1866 | California's legislature ratifies. **Hawaii's legislature ratifies after Victoria wins the floor's limits.** Pacifica's offer is now entrenched law before Oregon votes, as the Union's Enabling Act is law |
| July–Aug 1866 | Convention at Salem frames the constitution, the two questions and the ballot note |
| Aug–Sep 1866 | Campaign |
| Monday 1 Oct 1866 | Polling (date PROPOSED) |
| Oct 1866 | Returns commission certifies county by county; returns, both offers and the opposition's published case go to Washington |

**What makes the charter different.** Because it is a constitutional amendment, it is a protected provision. The Providentor's cabinet cannot legislate around it under the Enabling Law (canon §12), and it cannot be amended without the consent of Oregon's own legislature. No U.S. state has that: the U.S. Constitution can be amended over a state's objection.

## 6 · The two offers side by side

| | The United States (Enabling Act as amended in 1864) | The Confederation of Pacifica (Charter of Accession) |
|---|---|---|
| Status | Territory now; statehood once Oregon adopts a constitution without exclusion | Member state at exchange in 1867, under an entrenched charter |
| Exclusion | Forbidden | Permitted as limited (§4) |
| Other race laws | Untouched: marriage ban, poll tax and Chinese ban stand | Marriage ban void; poll tax and Chinese ban void for Pacifican citizens and treaty subjects |
| Weight | Two senators and one representative among about twenty-five states | A Council delegation among five members (California, Hawaii, Oregon, Washington, Nevada; Deseret's terms are separate), Assembly seats by population, and a veto over its own charter |
| Money | Union war debt, internal revenue taxes, greenbacks as federal legal tender | Customs union with a defined revenue share; gold coin; no confederal direct tax |
| Trade | Behind Pacifica's tariff toward San Francisco, Hawaii, Japan and Idaho; the rest of the Union reachable only by sea via Panama | Inside the customs union; the Idaho and Montana trade stays domestic |
| Land | Public lands stay federal; the Homestead Act gives 160 acres free | **The ceded public domain passes to the State of Oregon** (an offer no Union state ever had); family grants on the CR-015 I2 model; further Native land only through the confederal Land Fund |
| Indian war claims | Paid at face, in greenbacks | Not paid in money (CR-033); volunteers offered land warrants on Oregon's own public land |
| Works | A restoration programme of courts, mail and a custom house at Astoria | Willamette Falls and Cascades locks, the Columbia bar, and the Oregon–Sacramento railway on Development Fund credit, with dates (MODEL) |
| Defense | No draft in peacetime; Oregon becomes the Union's Pacific outpost in any second war | The Defense Law applies, but for ten years Oregon's men serve in an Oregon regiment and the engineer corps at home; Oregon is no longer anyone's frontier |
| Religion | Protestant majority nation | Religious liberty; Catholic institutions protected; Deseret a fellow member |
| Executive | President, Congress | A Providentor for life since 1865, limited by the charter's protected provisions |
| Identity | The flag, kin, the men who served | The compact's five-year record |

## 7 · Who decides, and what moves them

The 1860 presidential split in Oregon was roughly Lincoln 36, Breckinridge 34, Douglas 28 percent (Hist., approximate; verify). That is the baseline electorate: about 15,000 voters in 1866 (MODEL).

### Portland and the Columbia: decisive in Multnomah and the river counties

*Leaders:* John C. Ainsworth and the Oregon Steam Navigation Company; William S. Ladd, banker (politics to check); the Failing brothers. Against them, Henry W. Corbett.

- **The Idaho trade (decisive).** Idaho is Pacifican under the treaty either way. If Oregon stays Union, a customs border runs between Portland and its hinterland at the Snake, and the OSN's upriver gold traffic becomes foreign trade.
- **The exclave.** A Union Oregon is bordered by Pacifica on three sides, with Vancouver across the Columbia in a foreign country. No railway can reach it except across Pacifica.
- **Credit and coin.** Portland's houses survived 1855 on Monterey's credit and already price in Pacifican gold.
- **Corbett.** A Republican who wants Oregon's Senate seat, which the Union option would give him; he campaigns for the United States. Ladd votes his ledger. The split between Portland's two great houses carries the argument into a family-scale scene.

### The Willamette's Union Democrats: the swing

*Leader:* James W. Nesmith. *Against him:* Asahel Bush and the *Statesman.*

- **Popular sovereignty (decisive for this group).** These are Douglas men. Douglas died in 1861 defending the doctrine that a people decides its own institutions. The Republican Senate has just told Oregon it may not decide who lives there. Pacifica's Hawaiian clause is Douglas's doctrine in someone else's constitution. Nesmith: "Monterey offers us what Illinois's own senator promised and Washington refused."
- **The record.** Five years of the compact, the relief-boat judgment, paid requisitions, Oregon's courts sitting.
- **The charter's entrenchment.** It cannot be changed without Oregon's own consent.
- **Taxes and the public domain.**
- **Against:** the flag; Bush's loyalty; the Providentor; Protestant suspicion of a Catholic-tolerant republic with a Mormon member state; the Defense Law.
- **Result:** the group splits, roughly six to four for accession (MODEL).

### The Lane Democrats: solid for accession, except in the mines

*Leader:* Joseph Lane, a liability.

- Hatred of Republican Washington; Pacifica as Richmond's partner; exclusion; no Union reconstruction.
- **The cross-pressure.** In Jackson and Josephine counties the floor protects Chinese Pacifican miners' claims, which the 1857 constitution's Chinese ban would have closed. Southern Oregon miners fear the Chinese far more than the 128 Black Oregonians. **Lane's own country splits against Pacifica**, while the Willamette, where exclusion is symbolic and the Chinese are few, leans toward it. This gives the vote its geography.

### French Prairie and the Catholic settlers: a small, specific gain

French-Canadian and Métis families in Marion County, Archbishop Blanchet's flock.

- Religious liberty and protected Catholic institutions in a country where Protestants blamed Catholics for the Whitman killings (Hist.).
- **The marriage floor protects their marriages.** The 1862 and 1866 laws threatened white–Native marriages.

### Republicans: nearly solid for the United States

- Loyalty; the men who served; the Homestead Act; the war claims.
- Some Portland Republicans vote their trade.

### The coalition (MODEL)

| Bloc | Approx. share of electorate | For accession | Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lane Democrats outside the mining counties | 26% | 90% | 23 |
| Lane Democrats in Jackson and Josephine | 8% | 45% | 4 |
| Union Democrats | 28% | 60% | 17 |
| Republicans | 34% | 15% (mostly Portland) | 5 |
| French Prairie and others | 4% | 75% | 3 |
| **Total** | | | **about 52%** |

**Result: accession by about 52 to 48 percent.** The exclusion article passes by about three to one, less than 1857's eight to one, because the Union side campaigns against it as the price of statehood. A clear but narrow margin, which the protocol makes credible.

**The decisive shift** is the Willamette Union Democrats in Marion, Linn and Lane counties: Nesmith's men. Their reasons are popular sovereignty and the compact's record, not exclusion alone. Exclusion is the occasion; self-government is the argument.

## 8 · The campaign: scenes

1. **Colfax comes west.** Historically Schuyler Colfax toured the Pacific coast in 1865. Here he comes in August 1866 to campaign for statehood. He is the man who told the House in 1859 that it had "sent Oregon to Monterey." His best argument is kinship and the flag. His hardest moment is defending the repeal of exclusion before audiences that voted for it eight to one, on behalf of a Senate he cannot promise to move.
2. **Pacifica sends no speakers.** Victoria's protocol: Monterey publishes its charter and its record and lets Oregonians argue. The asymmetry reads as confidence.
3. **Lane speaks.** The motif of 1859 returns: the right side tainted by Lane. Nesmith asks that Lane stay home. Elias refuses to silence anyone; the protocol protects speech. In August, at Roseburg, Lane praises Richmond. Bush prints the speech, and the Willamette wavers. Nesmith answers with the armistice record: Pacifica ended its military mission to Richmond when the war ended (CR-D), and Oregon's compact forbade its men and ports to serve Richmond. The cutoff, which was a diplomatic term, becomes a campaign argument.
4. **The petition.** Gibbs's petition reaches the Council in the week Hawaii ratifies. Victoria reads it into the record.
5. **The Hawaiian legislature.** Victoria wins the floor's limits and ratifies the charter, with the clause.
6. **The count.** Deady's commission publishes the returns county by county: Multnomah and the Willamette for accession, Jackson for the United States, Lane's Douglas County for accession by less than expected.

## 9 · Consequences beyond 1866

- **April 1867, the Senate (CR-D).** The riddance motive helps ratification. Sumner can say Oregon chose to leave in order to keep a clause the Union would not admit, and the treaty protects every Black resident and gives each a choice of U.S. citizenship. The Republicans who would not drop the condition vote to let Oregon go.
- **1871, the fugitive agreement (§13).** Its travel-paper controls are modelled on Oregon's limited clause. The exclusion binds Black people who are not Pacifican citizens. **Victoria's exemption of 1866 becomes the template for the border she opposes in 1871.** **[PROPOSED]**
- **Black Californians.** The Colored Convention's grievance has its first named object.
- **The Chinese.** Southern Oregon's anger at the floor becomes the first anti-Chinese movement inside Pacifica. CR-037's test begins here.
- **The clause's end.** When Elias's county policy hollows out the member states, or at the latest with the Constitution of the Realm of 1887, the member states' residence power goes. **Victoria, as Regent, ends what she conceded in 1866.** **[OPEN — later CR]**
