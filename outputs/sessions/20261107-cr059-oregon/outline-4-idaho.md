# CR-059 (outline, part 4) — How Idaho becomes Pacifican before Oregon votes, 1861–1866

**Status:** Outline for the author, 7 November 2026. Everything is PROPOSED unless marked Hist. or canon. Effects on the vote are MODEL.

**Why this part matters.** Part 2's strongest argument in Portland is that Idaho is Pacifican whatever Oregon decides. That argument needs a chain behind it. Canon has only CR-015 H4 (PROPOSED): Idaho joins in 1863, because "with the Northwest Pacifican, Idaho's only supply line runs through Pacifica", and the Missouri route to Fort Benton "is cut in wartime."

This part builds the chain in three layers:

| Layer | When | How |
|---|---|---|
| **In fact** | 1862–63 | The Union's interior force leaves; Pacifica supplies, polices and coins for the mines |
| **In the treaty** | late 1865 | Idaho's transfer is **unconditional**; only Oregon's depends on a vote |
| **By consent** | June 1866 | Idaho's own legislature, elected under U.S. law, calls a convention, and Idaho accepts its charter **four months before Oregon votes** |

**Dependency.** This material is CR-B's (the interior) in plan v2, but CR-059 cannot be integrated without it. **Recommendation:** CR-059 takes over "the interior gate" (Walla Walla 1862, Idaho 1862–66), and CR-B keeps Deseret, Nevada, Montana and Wyoming.

## 1 · The geography that decides it

In 1861–65, Idaho's mines can be reached four ways:

| Route | Who holds it |
|---|---|
| The Columbia: Oregon Steam Navigation Company steamers to Wallula, Umatilla Landing and Lewiston; the Umatilla–Boise wagon road | Pacifica, from autumn 1861 |
| Salt Lake: Mormon freighters and the Montana Trail | Pacifica's associate Deseret, from December 1862 (CR-B) |
| California: the Humboldt and Chico roads to the Owyhee | Pacifica |
| **The Missouri: steamboats to Fort Benton (head of navigation from 1860, Hist.), then the Mullan Road west, about 624 miles over the Bitterroots** | **The Union, if it can keep it open** |

Everything turns on whether the Union can hold the fourth route and the interior post at its western end.

## 2 · The chain

### 1860–61: gold on Nez Perce land

Gold is found at Pierce in 1860 and Lewiston is founded in 1861, both on the Nez Perce reservation (Hist.). When the war comes, the camps are cut off from federal authority.

Pacifica's first act in Idaho is not a conquest but a Native agreement. **The Lewiston townsite is leased from the Nez Perce through the Confederation** on the CR-033 A22 model: lease only through the Republic, with rent to the nation. **[PROPOSED]** Idaho's gateway town stands on a Nez Perce lease that the Nez Perce want kept. That is also why no U.S. commission assembles at Lapwai in 1863 (part 3).

### Autumn 1861 – summer 1862: the Union's interior force

Canon §10: in 1861 "Wright evacuates mobile troops and usable stores toward the upper Columbia," and the Union keeps "an interior concentration, giving later reinforcements somewhere to arrive." Here that concentration is **Fort Walla Walla**, the interior's gate.

- **The winter of 1861–62 is a hard one in the interior** (Hist.: Mullan's road crew's "another harsh winter"; heavy stock losses). Wright's force can draw supplies only from the Missouri over the Mullan Road, which is not finished until June 1862 (Hist.). Pacifican steamers feed the town of Walla Walla while the fort goes short.
- **Spring–summer 1862.** The federal attempt to recover the lower Columbia fails against the turret ship (canon §10). Wright's force was meant to be its upper jaw. It cannot descend the river past Pacifican-held The Dalles and Celilo.
- **The Missouri route closes for practical purposes.** Fort Benton is a seasonal riverhead 2,000 miles from St Louis. The Lakota wars of 1862–64 make the upper river dangerous (Hist.: Sully's campaigns of 1863–64). Halleck needs every regiment in the East.

### September 1862: the Walla Walla convention

Wright and the Pacifican commander sign an evacuation:
- the garrison marches east **with the honours of war, its arms and its colours**, by the Mullan Road to Fort Benton before the snow;
- Fort Walla Walla and its stores pass to Pacifica at valuation;
- the property of civilians and the Union's loyal residents is guaranteed;
- no one is required to swear allegiance.

**[PROPOSED]** It sets the pattern of orderly evacuation that Fort Union and Port Townsend repeat in 1867.

**John Mullan**, still a U.S. Army officer, guides the column east on the road he built. He resigns his commission in April 1863 (Hist.) and comes back to Walla Walla, where he has already invested in local ventures (Hist.). There he takes Pacifica's mail contract on his own road. **[PROPOSED]** The Union's road-builder becomes the operator of Pacifica's route to the Montana gold. His later stage and law career in California is to verify.

**The Native nations decide the road.** The Coeur d'Alene and Spokane remember Wright's campaign of 1858: the hangings at Hangman Creek and the slaughter of their horses (Hist.). They let civilians pass on the Mullan Road but refuse passage to any Union column coming west. **[PROPOSED]** Their compact with Pacifica (passage, trade, no troops) is the reason the Union cannot come back from the east.

### August 1862 onward: the Boise Basin rush inside Pacifica's economy

- Gold in the Boise Basin in August 1862 (Hist.). Bannock, later **Idaho City**, is founded in December 1862 and reaches about 7,000 people in 1864, **the largest town in the Northwest, bigger than Portland** (Hist.).
- The rush happens entirely within Pacifica's supply system: Oregon Steam Navigation Company freight, Willamette flour and beef, Salt Lake freighters, Owyhee roads from California.
- **A Pacifican assay office at Idaho City** coins the basin's dust at cost. The express that carries the gold to San Francisco is Wells Fargo, a Californian company. Idaho's money is Pacifican, and so, through Portland, is Oregon's (levers L1).
- **January 1863: Bear River** (canon). A Pacifican atrocity under a local commander secures the Snake road at a terrible price. It must stay on the page; it makes the Shoshone and Bannock agreements harder (CR-015 H6), and the Union cites it in 1890 (canon §20).
- **July 1863: Camp Boise.** Historically Fort Boise was founded by Union volunteers to protect the emigrant road. Here it is **a Pacifican post**, and the town of Boise grows beside it. The emigrants of 1863–64 come down the Snake under its protection, among them the Missouri refugees and draft evaders of lever L11. Many settle the Boise valley's "Dixie" (Hist.). Others go on to the Grande Ronde, Powder River and the Willamette, and they are Oregon electors in 1866.

### 1863–64: a Union government on paper

Congress organizes Idaho Territory on 4 March 1863 (Hist.) to keep its claim. Governor William Wallace can reach it only by Fort Benton and the Mullan Road. He arrives at Lewiston in 1863 with a small escort.

**Pacifica lets him govern what he can and does not arrest him.** **[PROPOSED]** This is Elias's patience. The Union's organic act gives Idaho elections run by local officials, and Pacifica wants those elections to happen, because the legislature they produce will be a body Washington created.

- The Boise Basin's Southern Democrats dominate the legislature (Hist.: Idaho's Democratic legislatures from 1864).
- In December 1864 the legislature moves the capital from Lewiston to Boise (Hist.). Here Boise is the town beneath the Pacifican fort.
- Governor **Caleb Lyon** (1864–66) is mostly absent. **An 1866 audit finds $46,418 of federal money meant for the Nez Perce missing** (Hist.). Here it is the money Congress appropriated for the 1863 treaty commission that never met. **[PROPOSED]** The Union's last act in Idaho is its own governor's theft from the Nez Perce, whom Pacifica has been paying.

### Spring 1865 – June 1866: Idaho's consent

- **Spring 1865, the armistice.** The line of control is frozen with Idaho behind Pacifican lines.
- **Late 1865, the Western treaty.** The Union has had no force west of Fort Benton since 1862. It keeps Fort Benton and the Missouri under the 111°W line (CR-015 H5), so **Idaho's transfer is unconditional.** Only Oregon has a statehood promise and an Enabling Act, so only Oregon's transfer is conditional.
- **January 1866.** The Idaho legislature, sitting at Boise under the U.S. organic act, passes a memorial calling a convention to accept the cession and draft an accession charter. **[PROPOSED]**
- **May 1866.** The convention meets at Boise. Idaho's charter has no exclusion clause, because Idaho never had one. The Chinese floor applies: Idaho's 1864 tax on Chinese miners (Hist.) gives way to Pacifica's uniform light licence (CR-033). Idaho accepts it for courts, coin and roads. This is the floor's first real test, and it happens in a mining country four months before southern Oregon's.
- **June 1866.** Idaho's voters approve the charter. Its electorate follows Idaho's own convention rules, so Californians resident in the camps vote. That is legitimate, because Idaho's transfer does not depend on the vote; this is Pacifica's own exercise in consent. The Confederal chambers, California and Hawaii ratify Idaho's charter in the same summer session as Oregon's (part 2, §5).

**Idaho is Pacifica's first accession by consent with a published charter.** Oregon votes with Idaho's result in front of it.

## 3 · How Idaho moves Oregon's vote

| Mechanism | Who it moves | How |
|---|---|---|
| **The customs map** (new lever L20) | Portland, the Oregon Steam Navigation Company, The Dalles, Umatilla Landing, Willamette millers and stockmen | In July 1866 the Confederal Treasury publishes, as the law requires, the schedule of frontier customs houses that would open on exchange **if Oregon votes for the United States**: at Umatilla Landing, the Snake ferries and the Columbia above The Dalles. Every word is true and lawful. Oregon's flour, beef and freight to Idaho would pay Pacifica's tariff. The treaty's transit clause covers through-shipment, not sale. Portland's Board of Trade prints the map |
| **The exclave made concrete** | Willamette Union Democrats | With Idaho, Washington and California Pacifican, a Union Oregon has no land route to the Union except across Pacifica. Idaho's unconditional article in the treaty is the proof |
| **The precedent** | Waverers | A legislature elected under U.S. law chose Pacifica, and Idaho's charter is a benchmark Oregon can read |
| **The theft** | Everyone | Lyon's missing Nez Perce money is reported in Oregon's papers in summer 1866: true news, placed well (L12) |
| **The refugees** | Eastern Oregon | The emigrants Pacifica protected on the Snake road settle the Grande Ronde and Powder River. Their trade is Idaho's, and they are electors |
| **The gold** | Portland's banks | Idaho's gold reaches Portland as Pacifican coin |

**Size of the effect (MODEL).** Idaho is worth about **4–6 points** to accession: 2–3 in Portland and on the river, 1–2 in eastern Oregon, about 1 among Willamette waverers.

**It is load-bearing.** If Idaho were Union, a Union Oregon would keep its hinterland and a land road east, the Union's statehood offer would be credible, and accession would probably lose, about 47–53. With Idaho Pacifican and levers L9–L11, accession wins by about 53–47.

## 4 · The Union's counter-moves, and why they fail

| Counter-move | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| Relieve Walla Walla from Fort Benton (1862) | Distance, snow, the Lakota on the Missouri; the Coeur d'Alene and Spokane refuse troops passage |
| Settle the interior with Union men: James L. Fisk's escorted wagon trains from Minnesota, 1862–66 (Hist.) | They reach the Montana camps east of the divide. They help keep Fort Benton and the Missouri Union (the 111°W line), not Idaho |
| A Nez Perce treaty in 1863 to take Lewiston | The commission never assembles; the Lewiston lease and Pacifica's annuities hold |
| Pay Idaho in greenbacks | A gold country refuses paper (Hist. on the Pacific coast) |
| A Union governor at Lewiston | He governs a dwindling Clearwater camp; the basin's legislature moves to Boise; Lyon discredits the office |

### Canon tensions

1. **CR-015 H4's date, "1863" (PROPOSED).** Kept as the date Pacifica's control is effective; consent follows in 1866. No conflict.
2. **Canon §10's "interior concentration."** Identified here as Fort Walla Walla, and ended by the September 1862 convention. Check that §10's 1862 recovery attempt and any later interior operations do not require it to survive. If they do, move the convention to 1863.
3. **The Missouri route "cut in wartime" (CR-015 H4).** Given a mechanism: distance, season, the Lakota wars and Halleck's priorities.
4. **Bear River (canon).** Kept, and placed in the chain as the price of the Snake road.
