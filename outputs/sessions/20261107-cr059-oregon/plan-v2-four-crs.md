# Pacifica's continental settlement, 1848–1867 — revised plan (v2)

**Planning draft for four CRs.** This revision of the working draft keeps its central idea: Pacifica gains the West through consent, local compacts and a paid cession rather than by conquest alone. It removes the blockers identified in review and strengthens the weak links. It is not storyline prose. Each CR below lists the decisions it would ask for, its expected canon tensions and what stays open. The final map is the one in the master storyline §12 and CR-015 H5. Only the route to it changes.

## Working principles

1. **No state is ever ceded.** Oregon, Nevada and Washington stay U.S. territories until the treaty transfers title.
2. **Consent before title.** Where an organized electorate exists, it approves its own terms of accession. The treaty supplies the U.S. title. Native title and political authority need separate agreements in every region.
3. **Emergency power does not annex.** Under Article XIV, Pacifica occupies, administers and makes compacts. Accession waits for the peace and for the founding states' constitutional procedure.
4. **The Union's grievance stays pointed at Richmond.** No treaty clause, disclosure or breach should give Washington a case against Pacifica that outweighs its claim to the South.
5. **Promises are made to be tested.** Every guarantee in the settlement should be one the story can later honour, strain or break with consequences.

## CR-A · Oregon and the Northwest, 1850–1866

**Scope.** Why Oregon is never admitted, how it is governed under occupation and why it votes for Pacifica. Washington Territory's parallel path. This is the CR with the most story in it and should go first.

### 1850–56 · The market

California publishes its duties and wharf charges, allows bonded onward shipment through San Francisco and uses its yards, docks and public stores to anchor orders for Oregon lumber, produce and coal. Private wholesalers finance crops against orders. As California becomes a wheat producer itself, Oregon's tie rests increasingly on timber, shipping and credit. In the Page, Bacon panic of 1855, the mint's bullion and the Superintendency's credit carry sound Portland cargo bills along with the San Francisco houses (an extension of canon's CR-034 passage). Portland's merchants learn that their credit comes from Monterey, not New York.

### 1857–60 · The admission that fails

Oregon's voters have rejected a convention three times (1854–56) on the argument that statehood costs more than it gives (Hist.). In 1857 they approve a convention and then a constitution that bans slavery and excludes free Black people, both by large majorities (Hist.). In June 1858 they elect John Whiteaker governor and a legislature, which chooses Joseph Lane and Delazon Smith as senators, all in anticipation of admission (Hist.). The Senate passes the bill in May 1858 (Hist.).

Without California in the Union the free states lead the slave states by only sixteen to fifteen, but the House vote turns on party, not section. Republicans object to the exclusion clause and to two more Lecompton Democrats. In February 1859 a few anti-Lecompton Democrats join them, and the bill fails by a handful of votes. **[PROPOSED]** The 36th Congress makes the deadlock structural: the Republican House offers admission only under a new constitution without the exclusion clause, and the Democratic Senate refuses to make Oregon pay that price.

Oregon is left with an elected governor, legislature and senators-designate who hold no office. Whiteaker's title, governor of a state that does not exist, gives him standing without authority, which is exactly what canon's §10 needs. Lane runs for vice president with Breckinridge in 1860 (Hist.), now as a man whose own state was refused.

### 1861 · The Enabling Act and the war

The departure of the Southern senators clears the way. On 2 March 1861 Congress passes an **Oregon Enabling Act** alongside the historical organic acts for Nevada, Colorado and Dakota: delegates to be elected on 3 June 1861, a convention at Salem in September, a constitution without slavery or exclusion, then a popular vote. **[PROPOSED]** The act is a standing statute. It remains federal law through the war and becomes the legal basis of the Union's offer in 1866.

The delegates are elected. The Hawaiian packet incident, Article XIV and the July offensive follow on canon's calendar. Marcus takes Astoria, Portland and Fort Vancouver; Olympia and Steilacoom fall in autumn; Casey holds Port Townsend. The Salem convention never meets. Oregon is occupied as a territory of the United States, which it remains in law until 1867.

The occupation matters politically because of who Oregon's voters are. Many are Southern-born Democrats who hate Republican Washington more than they love slavery, and Pacifica is Richmond's partner. Commercial Portland depends on Monterey's credit. Union Democrats such as Asahel Bush and James Nesmith (Hist. persons) and the small Republican party oppose the occupation outright. Canon's three factions (Unionists, commercial accommodationists, Southern sympathizers) become the electorate of 1866.

### Autumn 1861 · The civil compact

Lincoln's territorial governor (name OPEN) withdraws with the federal administration to Port Townsend. County commissioners, probate judges and the members of the 1858 legislature negotiate a civil compact with the occupation command, with Whiteaker as their chair. He concedes nothing; he has no allegiance to transfer.

The compact provides:
- Oregon's courts and elected county governments continue.
- The army holds named positions, pays for its supplies and submits requisition disputes to civilian courts.
- No one is required to swear allegiance.
- Mail, property and trade continue across the lines where commanders can secure them.
- Oregon's men, ports and revenues are not used for any military purpose outside Oregon, including aid to Richmond.
- Sovereignty is left to a treaty and to Oregon's own consent.

The Native nations of the Willamette, the coast and the Columbia negotiate separately over land, passage, fishing and security.

The Puget Sound counties sign a parallel compact. **[PROPOSED]** Matthew Deady, a Southern-born Democrat who presided over the 1857 convention, stays at his post as a territorial justice and becomes the compact's court.

### December 1861 · The relief-boat judgment

The Willamette floods (Hist.). Steamboat crews and county officials lead the relief. A Pacifican officer requisitions a relief steamer for the garrison. Deady orders its release under the compact. The command refuses. Victoria makes compliance with the compact a condition of Hawaii's assent to renewing the Protectorship in 1862, and the officer is overruled. California is in its own flood emergency, so the visible act is that Oregon's boats and stores stay in Oregon's hands. **[PROPOSED]** This is Victoria's lever over the Protector and Oregon's first evidence that a Pacifican court order can bind a Pacifican army.

### 1862–64 · Holding the compact

The Columbia recovery attempt fails (canon §10). The compact survives because both factions in Oregon have reasons to keep it: Unionists keep their courts and presses, accommodationists their trade. In 1864 Congress amends the Enabling Act with a restoration programme: land grants, federal courts, mail and a place in the reconstruction appropriations. **[PROPOSED]** The offer is real and Oregon knows it.

### 1865–66 · Two offers

The armistice freezes the lines. The Western treaty, signed in late 1865 (CR-D), transfers Oregon only if Oregon votes for it. Pacifica drafts an accession charter:
- an elected legislature and courts;
- representation in the confederal institutions;
- a defined share of the customs;
- limits on confederal military authority in civilian affairs;
- a choice of citizenship, protection of property and equal civil protection.

Equal civil protection repeals the exclusion clause. So does the Union's Enabling Act. **Both offers require it**, so the issue that sank admission in 1859 decides nothing in 1866. The vote turns on four questions:

1. **Cost.** A state shares the Union's war taxes and debt. A Pacifican state shares the customs.
2. **Richmond.** The Union means reconstruction under a Republican Congress. Pacifica means a partner of the Confederacy that has just ended its military mission there.
3. **The record.** Five years of the compact, the relief-boat judgment and paid requisitions.
4. **Portland's credit.** Monterey's money is already in its wharves.

### October 1866 · Oregon chooses

The delegates elected in June 1861 under the Union's own Enabling Act are reconvened, with vacancies filled by fresh election. **[PROPOSED]** Washington cannot call them Pacifica's creation. The convention publishes both complete offers. County officials run the vote under a joint armistice protocol. Opposition papers campaign freely. Displaced voters and Oregonians serving in Union units vote by a procedure modelled on the absent-soldier provisions of the 1864 enabling acts.

Elias wants a margin he can show the Senate. Victoria and Sherman insist that the protocol be followed exactly, because an Oregon vote that Washington can call coerced is worth less in the Senate than a narrow clean one. **[PROPOSED]** This is the scene in which Elias is tempted and constrained.

Nesmith backs accession after the armistice cutoff, carrying Union Democrats with him. Lane's followers vote against Republican Washington. Portland and the lower Columbia vote their credit. Bush, the Republicans and part of the Willamette vote for statehood. **Oregon chooses Pacifica** by a clear but not overwhelming majority (margin OPEN). The returns, both offers and the opposition's published case go to Washington before the winter.

**Washington Territory.** Congress never offered it statehood, so it gets no plebiscite. An elected territorial convention approves its own accession charter in 1866. The reason Oregon alone votes on two offers is stated: only Oregon held a federal promise.

### Seed for after 1867

Oregon's charter protects its legislature against cabinet legislation under the Enabling Law. From 1868 Elias's water, railway and land grants go to Oregon's counties over the legislature's head, as canon §12 describes for all member states. Oregon's courts are the first to test whether the charter means what it says. **[OPEN — later CR]**

### Expected canon tensions

1. §10's Whiteaker line: keep the sentence, reframe Whiteaker as governor-elect of a state never admitted. Fact change: Oregon is never a U.S. state.
2. CR-015 A6's "Oregon's politics favorable" is preserved and given a mechanism.

### Open for CR-A

The territorial governor's name; the county or issue that supplies the decisive shift; the margin; Deady's role (PROPOSED); the Native nations party to the Northwest agreements.

## CR-B · The interior: Deseret, Washoe, Idaho, Montana and the divide, 1862–1867

**Scope.** Mostly repairs. It gives Pacifica a lawful wartime presence in the interior and reconciles the Deseret and Nevada dates with Article XIV and the Washoe sequence.

### Deseret · association first, accession after title

In January 1862 the Deseret constitutional convention elects Brigham Young governor of a state Washington never recognized (Hist.), with a Pacifican envoy quietly present (CR-015 H2). The Morrill Act of July 1862 is the breaking point. Pacifica's troops arrive on the Salt Lake road in October 1862 to guard the mail and telegraph, as the Third California Infantry did in history (Hist.: Camp Douglas). In December 1862 Pacifica and the State of Deseret sign a **Compact of Association**:

| Pacifica gives | Deseret gives |
|---|---|
| Protection from federal enforcement | Supplies and repair stations |
| Published terms of passage on its roads | Secure passage for the interior campaigns |
| Continuation of Deseret's courts and church-property arrangements | Lot Smith's cavalry and the Nauvoo Legion as militia guarding the road |
| A wheat market toward the Sierra railway | — |

It is an Article XIV agreement about passage, supply and defense, not an annexation. Full accession waits for the treaty and for the founding states' constitutional procedure.

Brigham Young surrenders John D. Lee in 1863 as the price. Pacifica tries and hangs him for Mountain Meadows (APPROVED canon, kept). Bear River, in January 1863, is committed by the Pacifican commander of the road garrison (canon) and poisons the later agreements with the Shoshone. Washakie's Eastern Shoshone negotiate their own passage and land terms (CR-015 H6). Deseret's accession charter, protecting member-state marriage and church-property law under the confederal floor, is approved by its legislature in 1866 and takes effect when the treaty is exchanged in 1867.

### Washoe and Nevada · the dead letter

The Union keeps a territorial government at Carson under Governor James Nye, with a garrison at Fort Churchill (Hist. persons and places). It is supplied only through Pacifican country. In December 1863 a territorial convention drafts a constitution that taxes the mines, and voters reject it in January 1864 (Hist.).

In spring 1864 Pacifica retakes Washoe. Fort Churchill's garrison, unsupplied, is allowed to withdraw east with its arms. Washoe's county officials sign a civil compact on the Oregon model, and Pacifica publishes a mining tax schedule lighter than the rejected constitution's, with recognition of valid titles and liens. The Carson Convention's property guarantees to American owners stay in force (canon CR-039 keeps them through the peace).

Congress's Nevada Enabling Act of March 1864 becomes a dead letter: no second convention can meet under federal authority, and there is no October 1864 admission. An elected Washoe convention approves Nevada's accession charter in 1866.

### Idaho and western Montana · supply and courts

Idaho's goldfields (Pierce 1860, Boise Basin 1862) are supplied from Walla Walla and Portland, and western Montana's camps (Bannack 1862, Alder Gulch 1863, Last Chance Gulch 1864) by the Montana Trail from Salt Lake and the Mullan Road (Hist.). With the Northwest and Deseret in Pacifican hands, Lincoln's territorial governors of Idaho (1863) and Montana (1864) govern little beyond their own offices.

Pacifica's supply compacts with the mining districts offer freight, specie, assay and licensed courts. The Montana vigilantes of 1863–64 and the hanging of Henry Plummer (Hist.) set Pacifica's offer of courts against the miners' own extralegal justice, the same choice Elias faced in San Francisco in 1851 and 1856. **[PROPOSED]** Fort Benton stays a Union river post and civilian trade runs both ways, which makes the 111°W line easier to defend in Washington.

### Southwest Wyoming and the Western Slope

Fort Bridger, the Green River crossing and South Pass are tied to Salt Lake by post supplies and stage contracts, with passage agreements negotiated with the Eastern Shoshone. The Western Slope and the Parks are Ute country with almost no settlers; Pacifica's claim rests on the treaty and on a Ute agreement, not on a local compact. The San Luis Valley belongs to CR-C.

### Expected canon tensions

1. CR-015 H2: Deseret's approved accession entry date (late 1862–63) becomes association in December 1862 and accession on exchange in 1867. The 1862–63 date survives as the date of the bargain.
2. CR-015 H4: the approved October 1864 Nevada admission is retired. Its line "signs it away months later" goes.
3. §11: "Deseret accedes as a confederate state in late 1862–1863" and "Washington still admits Nevada" are revised.

### Open for CR-B

Who commands the Salt Lake garrison; the Washoe tax schedule; Idaho's and Montana's Native agreements (Nez Perce, Bannock, Blackfeet across the line); the Ute agreement's terms and date.

## CR-C · The Southwest: the Rio Grande, Santa Fe and Fort Union, 1862–1867

**Scope.** How Pacifica holds New Mexico without a truce, and how the Rio Grande towns come to accept it.

### 1862 · The column and the gap

Sibley's Texans advance up the Rio Grande, take Albuquerque and Santa Fe and lose their trains at Glorieta in March 1862 (Hist.). They retreat having lived off the country. Canby's regulars and the Colorado volunteers have saved Fort Union, but the territory's stores are gone, the Colorado men go home and Washington is now at war on the Columbia.

The Pacifican column marches from Fort Yuma up the Gila to Tucson and reaches the Rio Grande in summer 1862, as Carleton's did (Hist.). It drives the last Texans from Mesilla in a brief clash; Henry Pruett dies there (CR-022, kept). Richmond renounces its Arizona claim for credit and the Sunset route (canon).

The column then moves into the gap the Texans left. **It pays for its forage in gold** where Sibley's men took it. **[PROPOSED]** That is the column's best argument in the Rio Grande towns. It reaches Albuquerque and Santa Fe in autumn 1862.

### The frozen front

Halleck has two choices: reinforce New Mexico over seven hundred miles of the Santa Fe Trail, or hold Fort Union as the bastion covering Colorado and Kansas. He chooses Fort Union. Governor Henry Connelly (Hist.) withdraws to Las Vegas with the territorial administration and keeps claiming the whole territory for the Union.

Glorieta Pass, between Santa Fe and Fort Union, becomes the front: a narrow, defensible defile that neither side can force cheaply. Both sides are at war; neither has the supply to attack in winter. Raids, patrols and civilian traders cross the line. **[PROPOSED]** Pacifica has to feed Santa Fe through the winter of 1862–63 over a staged road from Tucson. That is the logistics test the plan must pass.

### 1863 · The Rio Grande compact

With Connelly gone, Pacifica bargains with the members of the territorial legislature, county probate judges and town councils. **[PROPOSED]** Miguel Antonio Otero, the former territorial delegate with Southern sympathies (Hist. person), is a candidate leader on the Hispano side.

The compact offers:
- elected local government;
- bilingual courts;
- adjudication of Spanish and Mexican land grants on California's model (CR-033);
- continuity of the 1850 territorial institutions.

Its price comes later: the founding law forbids debt peonage and the captive trade. The Pueblos bargain separately for their lands and their governments. Passage and security agreements with the Navajo and Apache are separate.

### 1865–66 · The settlements inside the line

- The Navajo homeland treaty takes the place of the Long Walk (APPROVED canon, CR-015 H6).
- The Apache peace of 1865 pairs homelands and paid scouts with prosecution of settler massacres, alongside the Mexican convention on scalp bounties.
- Pacifica abolishes peonage and the captive trade with compensation by 1866, and meets the short Hispano revolt (APPROVED canon). The compact is tested by the promise it made least willingly.

### 1865–67 · Fort Union

The armistice keeps Glorieta as the line. The treaty gives Fort Union's garrison a withdrawal timetable after exchange, protection for its evacuation and continued civilian freight on the Santa Fe Trail. The final line is 37°N to 103°W and south to Texas, with Raton Pass and Fort Union as Pacifica's eastern bastion (canon). The Union has no land border with Mexico (CR-058).

From 1865 Pacifica recognizes and lends to Juárez on the Sonora customs. Rifles go south through Paso del Norte from Pacifican New Mexico (CR-058), at a crossing and inspection point agreed with Mexico that does not depend on Confederate El Paso.

### Alternative kept for the CR: the Gadsden split

California buys the western half of the Gadsden strip, through Tucson, in 1853–54; Washington buys the Mesilla half and gets a transit right across California's segment. This gives the 1862 column a Californian base at Tucson, but it adds a prewar treaty, a new border and a Southern railway quarrel. **Recommendation: drop it.**

### Expected canon tensions

1. §10's "A Pacifican California column establishes control of Arizona and New Mexico in 1862" (PROPOSED) becomes control of the Rio Grande to Santa Fe in 1862, with Fort Union Union-held until 1867.

### Open for CR-C

The column's strength and its winter supply audit; whether Fort Union is besieged or only watched; the compact's Hispano signatories; the Paso del Norte crossing.

## CR-D · The peace, Alaska and the Senate, 1864–1867

**Scope.** The instruments that convert occupation and consent into title. Their order matters more than their detail.

### 1864–65 · Keep McClellan's conditions intact

Marcus returns home in late 1863 (canon). Logan's reduced mission and Pacifican credit through Matamoros continue to the armistice; Mobile holds and Farragut dies in February 1865 (canon). Nothing in this plan weakens the conditions that elect McClellan.

### Spring 1865 · The armistice

McClellan signs separate armistices with Richmond and Pacifica in March–June 1865 (canon). The Pacifica–Union armistice includes one public term on the South: while the Union–Confederate armistice holds, Pacifica sends no military mission and no arms to Richmond. **[PROPOSED]** Consuls at Matamoros and New Orleans can see whether it is kept. Nobody needs Pacifica's accounts.

Logan stays in Louisiana as a private engineer, which canon §13 already suggests. Pacifica's commercial treaty with Richmond in spring 1865 (canon) is not a military commitment. In 1870 Pacifica sends a squadron and mines under Logan, after the Union attacks (canon).

### Late 1865 · The treaty is signed

The Western treaty is signed in late 1865 (place and signatories OPEN), carrying:
- the CR-015 H5 line;
- the evacuation of Port Townsend and Fort Union;
- the $40 million gold vendor credit;
- guaranteed commercial access to the Columbia and Puget Sound;
- named civilian routes from Denver and Fort Benton;
- the Pacific article (CR-029);
- the end of the 1848 and 1860 conventions, with Carson's property guarantees kept (CR-039);
- a **mutual waiver of war claims** on the 1848 model, which keeps Farragut, Hampton Roads and the Columbia out of the treaty.

**The Oregon article takes effect only if Oregon votes for it.** If Oregon chooses statehood, the treaty still transfers the rest, with port access and evacuation terms written for either result. McClellan holds the treaty until the vote. **[PROPOSED]** This explains why canon's 1865 peace waits until 1867 for ratification.

Each resident of a ceded district may choose U.S. or Pacifican citizenship and is guaranteed property, access to the courts and civil protection without distinction of color. The treaty says nothing about fugitives. Native title and authority are left to separate agreements. The treaty neither recognizes Richmond nor disposes of the Union's claim to the South.

### December 1866 – March 1867 · Alaska

By 1866 Russia has its reasons to sell: the Russian-American Company's weakness and Alaska's indefensibility against Britain (Hist.). In this timeline the United States is about to lose its last Pacific port. Stoeckl reports that the Union could neither use nor defend the colony. Pacifica's envoy in St Petersburg offers $7.2 million in its own minted gold, payable at handover, with orderly terms for the company's people and property.

Pacifica has refused British protection since 1846, so Gorchakov can sell to it without strengthening Britain. At the special meeting of 16 December 1866 (Old Style), Alexander II decides to sell to Pacifica. The treaty is signed in March 1867 (exact date OPEN; Russian instruments carry Julian dates). Handover follows at Sitka in October 1867. The Aleut, Tlingit and other nations' lands and rights need their own arrangements. CR-015 A8 ("the Union cannot bid") is preserved and given its mechanism.

### April 1867 · The Senate

McClellan sends the treaty with Oregon's certified returns, the published opposition case, the Washington Territory, Nevada and Deseret charters, a Navy estimate of what recovering the Columbia would cost and a Treasury statement of Pacifica's payments. The first instalment is deposited in gold at the New York Subtreasury before the vote. **[PROPOSED]**

The coalition rests on four questions, each answered by senators who can bring colleagues:

| Question | Senators | Answer they need |
|---|---|---|
| Can the Union afford the alternative? | Grimes (Naval Affairs) | The Navy's own estimate of a Columbia campaign against the cost of guaranteed access |
| Is the money real? | Fessenden, John Sherman | Gold on deposit, a schedule and the Alaska obligation on the same statement |
| Can Union commerce reach the Pacific? | Harlan, Ramsey; Morgan for New York | Named routes, published rates and an arbitration process |
| Is anyone abandoned? | Sumner, Reverdy Johnson | Oregon's free vote, citizenship choice, civil protection without distinction of color, the South's question untouched |

**Why Republicans vote for a Democrat's treaty.** The West is already lost in fact. Ratification ends the Western war, puts $40 million in gold toward resumption, and leaves the claim to the South, which is the Radicals' real cause, entirely open. A rejected treaty leaves the armistice in a Democratic president's hands and the army in his command.

Democrats vote for peace. Wade, Chandler and Morton lead the opposition. If the Foreign Relations committee matches its historical membership, Cameron and Patterson are the remaining members to win. The count waits for the rebuilt 1866 Senate, including Tennessee's status and the absence of California, Oregon and Nevada. The target is two-thirds of senators present with a margin.

The Senate consents in April 1867, during the historical special session. Congress passes the implementing and appropriation measures. Ratifications are exchanged in summer 1867 against the first payment, and the survey, evacuation, archives and claims schedules begin.

## Dependencies and order

**CR-A first.** It decides the facts the others rest on: Oregon was never a state, and the treaty carries an Oregon condition.

**CR-B and CR-C next,** in either order. They share only the consent principle and the Native-agreements pattern.

**CR-D last.** It collects the charters and returns the other three produce, and carries the workbook and ledger changes.

Each CR needs the repository's `## Fact changes` and `## Canon tensions` sections, a `cr_facts.py preview` run and the fresh-context review before adoption.
