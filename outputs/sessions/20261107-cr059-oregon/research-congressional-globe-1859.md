### Hughes, 10 February (pamphlet pp. 3–15)

- He had himself "conveyed to this House from a sick room". As a defeated member he speaks "as a voice from the spirit land".
- Oregon would be "a second Pacific star".
- He says he understood, when he voted for the English bill's ratio, that Oregon's pending application "was not to be affected". He quotes Buchanan's message to the same effect.
- He praises Lane's Lecompton loyalty: "who stood forth more nobly … than the gallant gentleman who sits upon this floor as the delegate from Oregon?"
- He quotes Lane's own Kansas speech: "Every southern member of the House is ready to vote for the admission of Minnesota and Oregon without slavery" (Globe, vol. 36, part 2, p. 1396).
- **The case for admitting to bind:** "It is our obvious policy, to build up at once, powerful States on the Pacific. Their growth should be stimulated, their governments strengthened, and their affections drawn to us by every legitimate means." Admission is "one step in a policy demanded by our security as a nation". The same passage calls for seizing "the gateways of the Isthmus, and the island that commands the gulf".

**For CR-059.** This is the "admit to bind" argument already on the historical record, and it arrives wrapped in praise of Lane's Lecompton loyalty: the draft's taint, in the advocate's own words. In this timeline Oregon would be the *first* Pacific star.

### John B. Clark of Missouri, 11 February (about pp. 968–971)

Speaking "as a member of … the Committee on Territories", he explains "the reasons which governed my vote in committee".
- **The compact.** The organic act of 1848 extended the Ordinance of 1787, so Oregon is entitled to admission at sixty thousand. He cites Tennessee as the precedent, with Madison, Macon and Gallatin.
- **Good faith.** Admission is no "party question". It would be "extreme bad faith to Oregon to invite her to make a constitution regardless of population" and then refuse her. "Oregon comes here as Kansas came."
- **The English bill.** It operates on Kansas's future action, not on Oregon's past.
- **The franchise and exclusion.** Alien suffrage is a state matter; Illinois's first constitution allowed it. Free-Black exclusion stands on *Dred Scott*.
- **Population.** He claims more than 100,000, citing Grow's statement of two years earlier, which Grow had taken from the Delegate from Oregon. An exchange follows on whether the Delegate is still believed. Lane's credibility is tested on the floor, historically.
- A sentence from Clark's column reads: "so long as I am a member upon this floor, I will never vote to admit any Territory as a State after this, that has not a population equal to that required for a Representative". The attribution is likely but not certain.

### Thayer, 11 February (pp. 975–977)

- He answers his colleague Dawes, who could not see "how an honest Representative of the State of Massachusetts can vote for the admission of Oregon".
- "I shall vote for the admission of the State of Oregon without hesitation, without reluctance, and without reserve."
- He notes that ten Republican senators voted for admission on 19 May 1858 and six against.
- Oregonians are "the pilgrims of the Pacific coast". They will "contaminate this Confederacy" just as much "as their mountain streams will contaminate the Pacific ocean". In "the sturdy virtues of honesty, of fidelity, of industry, and of endurance, they are above the average".
- He rebukes "the gentleman from Maine", who had called the pioneers "interlopers, runaways, and outlaws".
- **The key line:** "Is it better that they should remain under the tuition of this Federal Government—a non-resident Government—or that they should govern themselves? … I have no faith in that kind of government being exercised over Anglo-Saxons anywhere."

**For CR-059.** The draft's turn for Thayer is ruled out. He is better used as he was: the loudest voice for admission, whose argument against "a non-resident Government" becomes Oregon's own case in 1866.

### Stephens, 12 February (pamphlet, 8 pp.)

- **The compact.** The 1787 Ordinance is extended by the organic act; Madison, Macon and Gallatin on Tennessee. "It is the simple, naked question of fulfilling obligations."
- **Population.** He estimates more than 100,000 from Oregon's personal property of $22,000,000.
- **Exclusion.** Kansas's Topeka constitution also excluded free Black people.
- **Partisan motive.** He says "I will never do wrong that right may afterwards come from it."
- **To the slaveholding members:** their opposition comes from "a reluctance … to increase the number of what are called free States". But "that balance is already gone—lost by causes beyond your or my control. There is no prospect of its ever being regained … let us look to the security which rests upon principle, rather than upon numbers. The citadel of our defense is principle … Let us, therefore, do justice, though the heavens fall."
- **Growth.** Material progress, the telegraph, "over a hundred million human beings within the present boundaries", "perhaps double the number of States … should the Union last". The Union is a machinery of independent states, illustrated by Ezekiel's vision of the wheels. The galleries applaud.
- **Precedents:** Florida and Texas were admitted without a census. "So was California."
- **His close:** "Put on no riders … If you cannot vote for the bill, vote against it just as it stands."

**For CR-059.**
- In this timeline Stephens's central claim to the Southern members, that the balance is already gone, is false. Without California the balance is still even, and Barksdale knows it. This is the causal hinge of H3, now in Stephens's own terms.
- His California precedent disappears.

### John Sherman

- He appears once in the Feb 12 issue (p. 968) as a member of the naval investigating committee, answering a question about the *New York Times*.
- He did not speak on Oregon and did not vote. Historically his naval committee seat is a ready reason for his interest in Sausalito and the Pacific squadron.

## Consequences for CR-059 (applied in the draft)

1. **The six changed votes are re-chosen among members silent in the historical debate.** Barksdale and McRae (Mississippi); Horton and Leiter (Ohio, both leaving in March); Wood and Foster (Maine). The total stays 106 to 109, and Colfax still holds eleven of fifteen Republicans.
2. **Lamar, Nichols and Thayer** keep their historical yeas and speeches.
3. **Hughes** is added as a voice of "admit to bind".
4. **Millson's and Hill's arguments** are the historical form of the ratio argument that Barksdale and McRae adopt.
5. **A proposed campaign scene for 1866:** the Oregon papers reprint Thayer's "non-resident Government".
