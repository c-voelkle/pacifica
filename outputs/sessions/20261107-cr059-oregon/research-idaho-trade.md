## 1. The river company

**Traffic, 1861–1864** (Irene Lincoln Poppleton, "Oregon's First Monopoly—The O.S.N. Co.", *OHQ* 9 (1908); her footnote cites the *Pacific Monthly*, Jan.–June 1907). The table gives the company's traffic, not Idaho's alone; the Oregon Encyclopedia describes the same totals as traffic upriver from Celilo.

| Year | Passengers | Tons of freight |
|---|---|---|
| 1861 | 10,500 | 6,290 |
| 1862 | 24,500 | 14,550 |
| 1863 | 22,000 | 17,646 |
| 1864 | 36,000 | 21,834 |

Poppleton attributes part of the 1862 jump to the absorption of the Willamette boats. The four years together (93,000 passengers, 60,320 tons) match the Oregon Encyclopedia's "nearly 100,000 passengers and 60,000 tons", which it places in about two years after 1862; the four-year reading is the right one.

**Rates** (P. W. Gillette, "A Brief History of the Oregon Steam Navigation Company", *OHQ* 5 (1904), a participant's memoir, undated but describing the rush years): Portland–The Dalles $40 a ton; The Dalles–Celilo portage $15; The Dalles–Wallula $55; Portland–Lewiston $120 a ton (measurement ton of 40 cubic feet). Passage Portland–Lewiston $60. A Lewiston merchant's case of shovels in 1862 paid $1 a shovel in freight.

**Earnings.** Nearly 50 per cent return to investors in 1862 (Oregon Encyclopedia, after Johansen). Tickets sold at The Dalles for up-trips alone ran $1,000–11,000 a sailing in spring 1862; one May up-trip of the *Tenino* took over $18,000 (Poppleton, from the company's Dalles books). Capital re-registered at $2,000,000 in October 1862. Earnings fell in 1866 and 1867 (Oregon Encyclopedia).

**Gold down the river.** Wells Fargo's treasure shipments from Portland: 1864 $6.2 million, 1865 $5.8 million, 1866 $5.4 million, 1867 $4.0 million (Poppleton, citing the *Overland Monthly*, July 1868; also Raymond 1870, Oregon section, who notes these include Idaho gold reaching the Columbia). The Oregonian reported $3 million of dust reaching Portland in 1861, mostly from the Clearwater and Salmon River (quoted in L. M. Scott, "The Pioneer Stimulus of Gold", *OHQ* 18 (1917), p. 165).

**Scale against Oregon's own trade.** Portland's merchandise exports in 1866, at wholesale prices, were $457,967 (flour, bacon, apples, wool and the rest; Poppleton, after the *Overland Monthly*). The treasure passing through Portland was more than ten times Oregon's own exported produce.

## 2. Idaho's gold

**Idaho Territory, all districts** (Waldemar Lindgren, "The Mining Districts of the Idaho Basin and the Boise Ridge", USGS 18th Annual Report, part 3 (1898), p. 652; checked against the scan):

| Year | Value | Lindgren's basis |
|---|---|---|
| 1863 | $7,000,000 | his estimate; reports lacking |
| 1864 | $6,470,100 | Browne's 1867 report, Wells Fargo data, estimates added |
| 1865 | $6,581,400 | same |
| 1866 | $8,023,700 | same (Eleventh Census gives $17 million, "authority … unknown") |
| 1867 | $6,500,000 | Mint reports |

Lindgren calls the often-quoted $40 million for the Basin's first six years extravagant; the territory's whole output for those years scarcely exceeded $41 million.

**The Boise Basin** (Lindgren p. 655, his "probable production", checked against the scan): 1863 $3.0 million; 1864 $4.0 million; 1865 $5.0 million; 1866 $5.0 million; 1867 $4.3 million (the last from W. A. Atlee, Wells Fargo's agent at Boise, p. 654). Hailey, a pioneer, put 1863 at about $5 million (*History of Idaho*, 1910, p. 64).

**People.** Idaho's official census of August 1863: 32,342 (Bancroft, fn. 16). Basin in summer 1863: 15,000–20,000 (Hailey p. 62); Idaho City about 6,000 in autumn 1863 (Bancroft).

**Not found:** annual figures for Owyhee or the northern camps; Browne's 1868 Idaho chapter; Raymond's 1869–1870 Idaho chapters.

## 3. Who supplied the mines

No source found gives tonnage shares by route. The sources support a ranking:

- **The Columbia (Portland → Umatilla Landing or Wallula → Boise; Lewiston for the north)** carried most of the Boise Basin's supply in every year 1862–1866. Umatilla was the chief outfitting point for the Basin from 1863 (James W. Watt, *Washington Historical Quarterly*). In July–August 1863 ten or more pack trains a day reached the Basin, with half as many wagons (Bancroft); one tally gives about 290 short tons arriving every thirteen days (Bancroft fn. 17). San Francisco's hold on the Basin ran through Portland (Trimble, *Mining Advance into the Inland Empire*, 1914, p. 116).
- **Salt Lake** sent mainly flour, which in 1863 undersold Willamette flour (Hailey p. 62; Bancroft). Freight Salt Lake–Basin 20–33 cents a pound in 1863 (J. G. Martin's letter, *Galena Daily Advertiser*, 8 February 1864).
- **California (Chico, Red Bluff, the Humboldt roads)** took the Owyhee trade: by 1865 the California routes hauled most of it (Idaho State Historical Society Reference Series 427). In 1866 a rate war reached the Basin: the Central Pacific and the California Navigation Company carried freight free to Chico landing, wagons ran to Ruby City and Boise for 11–12 cents a pound, Sacramento merchants offered a bonus for the first 100-ton train to Owyhee, and the OSN cut its rates (Bancroft).
- **The Missouri and Fort Benton** served Montana, not Boise or Owyhee.

**Freight rates, Umatilla–Boise:** packing 40–80 cents a pound at first, then 16–25 (1863; Watt; Hailey p. 63); wagons 10–12 cents, falling to 6–8 (1864; Hailey p. 99); 5–6 cents in summer and autumn 1866 (Hailey p. 140). Camp prices: flour 40 cents a pound at Idaho City in December 1863 (Martin), $1 a pound in the winter of 1864–65 before the flour riot (Bancroft).

## 4. What it means for CR-059 (MODEL)

1. **Idaho was Portland's largest business, by a wide margin.** Idaho's $6.5–8 million a year of gold against Oregon's own exports of under half a million in 1866; the river company's traffic more than tripling 1861–64 and returning nearly 50 per cent to its investors in 1862. Portland, the river company and The Dalles had more at stake in Idaho than in anything Oregon produced. This supports the CR's claim that the customs map (I11) moves Portland, and its 4–6 points are now grounded in the scale of the trade rather than assumed. Converting it into votes remains MODEL.
2. **1866 is the right year for the threat.** Historically, in the summer of 1866, California's railway, river and teamster interests were already underbidding Portland for the Owyhee and Boise trade, and the river company's earnings were falling. In this timeline those California routes, and Salt Lake's, are Pacifica's. The customs map lands in the same summer as California's rate war: a Union Oregon would face a tariff line and a cheaper rival at once. Proposed for I11 and the §12 text.
3. **The trade was declining by 1866.** The Basin peaked in 1865–66; Portland's treasure fell from $6.2 million (1864) to $5.4 million (1866). The argument in 1866 is about keeping a shrinking trade, not winning a growing one, which suits fear better than hope: Portland votes to avoid losing what is left.
4. **Salt Lake's flour** is a second Pacifican supply line (Deseret), so the Basin can be fed without Oregon. That is the exclave argument in one commodity.
