## 1. The river company

**Traffic, 1861–1864** (Irene Lincoln Poppleton, "Oregon's First Monopoly—The O.S.N. Co.", *OHQ* 9 (1908); her footnote cites the *Pacific Monthly*, Jan.–June 1907; Throckmorton, p. 253, prints the same table and traces it, through Johansen's dissertation, to J. Ross Browne's *Mineral Resources* (1868), p. 579, who had it from the company's secretary). The table gives the company's traffic, not Idaho's alone; the Oregon Encyclopedia describes the same totals as traffic upriver from Celilo.

| Year | Passengers | Tons of freight |
|---|---|---|
| 1861 | 10,500 | 6,290 |
| 1862 | 24,500 | 14,550 |
| 1863 | 22,000 | 17,646 |
| 1864 | 36,000 | 21,834 |

Poppleton attributes part of the 1862 jump to the absorption of the Willamette boats. The four years together (93,000 passengers, 60,320 tons) match the Oregon Encyclopedia's "nearly 100,000 passengers and 60,000 tons", which it places in about two years after 1862; the four-year reading is the right one.

**Rates** (P. W. Gillette, "A Brief History of the Oregon Steam Navigation Company", *OHQ* 5 (1904), a participant's memoir, undated but describing the rush years): Portland–The Dalles $40 a ton; The Dalles–Celilo portage $15; The Dalles–Wallula $55; Portland–Lewiston $120 a ton (measurement ton of 40 cubic feet). Passage Portland–Lewiston $60. A Lewiston merchant's case of shovels in 1862 paid $1 a shovel in freight.

**Earnings.** A return of 48 per cent on the original shares in the company's first year, 1861 (Johansen p. 182; Throckmorton p. 251); the Oregon Encyclopedia's "nearly 50 percent … in 1862" misdates it. Yearly figures in section 6. Tickets sold at The Dalles for up-trips alone ran $1,000–11,000 a sailing in spring 1862; one May up-trip of the *Tenino* took over $18,000 (Poppleton, from the company's Dalles books). Capital re-registered at $2,000,000 in October 1862. Earnings fell in 1866 and 1867 (Oregon Encyclopedia).

**Gold down the river.** Wells Fargo's treasure shipments from Portland: 1864 $6.2 million, 1865 $5.8 million, 1866 $5.4 million, 1867 $4.0 million (Poppleton, citing the *Overland Monthly*, July 1868; also Raymond 1870, Oregon section, who notes these include Idaho gold reaching the Columbia). The Oregonian reported $3 million of dust reaching Portland in 1861, mostly from the Clearwater and Salmon River (quoted in L. M. Scott, "The Pioneer Stimulus of Gold", *OHQ* 18 (1917), p. 165).

**Scale against Oregon's own trade.** Portland's merchandise exports in 1866, at wholesale prices, were $457,967 (flour, bacon, apples, wool and the rest; Poppleton, after the *Overland Monthly*). The treasure passing through Portland was more than ten times Oregon's own exported produce.

## 3. Who supplied the mines

No source found gives tonnage shares by route. The sources support a ranking:

- **The Columbia (Portland → Umatilla Landing or Wallula → Boise; Lewiston for the north)** carried most of the Boise Basin's supply in every year 1862–1866. Umatilla was the chief outfitting point for the Basin from 1863 (James W. Watt, *Washington Historical Quarterly*). In July–August 1863 ten or more pack trains a day reached the Basin, with half as many wagons (Bancroft); one tally gives about 290 short tons arriving every thirteen days (Bancroft fn. 17). San Francisco's hold on the Basin ran through Portland (Trimble, *Mining Advance into the Inland Empire*, 1914, p. 116). **Correction (Throckmorton):** the Columbia's lead held for 1862–64 only. The Idaho and California Wagon Road Company's first train left Sacramento for Boise in May 1864. By October 1865 three-fourths of the trains entering Idaho came from California (*Idaho World*, 14 October 1865, quoted by Trimble pp. 115–116; Throckmorton p. 262), and much of the Basin's gold went out overland to San Francisco by the Humboldt route (Throckmorton p. 300). Throckmorton's verdict: Portland "failed to command the trade of southern Idaho" (p. 262). Trains are not tons, and Umatilla stayed the Basin's main landing (Hailey), so the share by weight is still open.
- **Salt Lake** sent mainly flour, which in 1863 undersold Willamette flour (Hailey p. 62; Bancroft). Freight Salt Lake–Basin 20–33 cents a pound in 1863 (J. G. Martin's letter, *Galena Daily Advertiser*, 8 February 1864).
- **California (Chico, Red Bluff, the Humboldt roads)** took the Owyhee trade: by 1865 the California routes hauled most of it (Idaho State Historical Society Reference Series 427). In 1866 a rate war reached the Basin: the Central Pacific and the California Navigation Company carried freight free to Chico landing, wagons ran to Ruby City and Boise for 11–12 cents a pound, Sacramento merchants offered a bonus for the first 100-ton train to Owyhee, and the OSN cut its rates (Bancroft).
- **The Missouri and Fort Benton** served Montana, not Boise or Owyhee.

**Freight rates, Umatilla–Boise:** packing 40–80 cents a pound at first, then 16–25 (1863; Watt; Hailey p. 63); wagons 10–12 cents, falling to 6–8 (1864; Hailey p. 99); 5–6 cents in summer and autumn 1866 (Hailey p. 140). Camp prices: flour 40 cents a pound at Idaho City in December 1863 (Martin), $1 a pound in the winter of 1864–65 before the flour riot (Bancroft).

## 4. What it means for CR-059 (MODEL)

1. **Idaho was Portland's largest business, by a wide margin.** Idaho's $6.5–8 million a year of gold against Oregon's own exports of under half a million in 1866; the river company's traffic more than tripling 1861–64 and returning 48 per cent to its investors in its first year. Portland, the river company and The Dalles had more at stake in Idaho than in anything Oregon produced. This supports the CR's claim that the customs map (I11) moves Portland, and its 4–6 points are now grounded in the scale of the trade rather than assumed. Converting it into votes remains MODEL.
2. **1866 is the right year for the threat.** Historically, in the summer of 1866, California's railway, river and teamster interests were already underbidding Portland for the Owyhee and Boise trade, and the river company's earnings were falling. In this timeline those California routes, and Salt Lake's, are Pacifica's. The customs map lands in the same summer as California's rate war: a Union Oregon would face a tariff line and a cheaper rival at once. Proposed for I11 and the §12 text.
3. **The trade was declining by 1866.** The Basin peaked in 1865–66; Portland's treasure fell from $6.2 million (1864) to $5.4 million (1866). The argument in 1866 is about keeping a shrinking trade, not winning a growing one, which suits fear better than hope: Portland votes to avoid losing what is left.
4. **Salt Lake's flour** is a second Pacifican supply line (Deseret), so the Basin can be fed without Oregon. That is the exclave argument in one commodity.

## 6. From Johansen (1941) and Throckmorton (1961)

### The river company's money, year by year

| Year | What the sources say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1861 | Capital raised from $172,500 to $690,000 in June and $996,000 in September instead of a dividend; dividends worth 48% of the original shares by year's end | Johansen p. 182; Throckmorton p. 251 |
| 1862–63 | No dividends: the Cascades portages bought for $200,000, the Dalles–Celilo railway built for close to $1 million, $200,000 borrowed. Rechartered in Oregon in December 1862 at $2 million | Johansen pp. 182, 184 |
| 1863 | Earnings $1,300,000 (Johansen, from Reed's statement in the Bancroft Library); Throckmorton gives $1,500,000 | Johansen p. 184; Throckmorton p. 251 |
| 1864 | Dividends of 12% paid May–December | Johansen p. 184; Throckmorton p. 251 |
| 1865 | Eighteen vessels, averaging 160 tons; real estate "rising two Million dollars" (Bowles) | Johansen pp. 182–183 |
| 1866 | Ladd reports business "very quiet", and in January 1867 that 1866 had fallen off (the printed figure, "$300", is a misprint). Johansen reads these reports as part of a squeeze on the dissenting shareholders (below) | Johansen p. 187 |
| 1867 | Seven owners left by December; the four directors declare a dividend of 36.84% at Christmas | Johansen p. 187; Throckmorton p. 271 |

Running costs: Portland to Wallula meant ten to twelve handlings of every cargo, the boats came back down empty, and without opposition the company put its overhead at half of each trip's income (Johansen p. 183).

**Who took the retail price.** Johansen's calculation for Boise in 1863: flour cost 4 cents a pound in Portland, 7 cents to Wallula by the company, 2 cents on to Walla Walla and 25 cents by pack train to Boise, and sold for at least 60 cents. The company took 11⅔ per cent of the retail price, the packers 41⅔ and the Boise merchants 36⅔. For molasses the company's share was 4⅓ per cent, for beans 4 (Johansen p. 182; Throckmorton pp. 260–261). The company's grip was on the river, not on the price in the camps.

**Rates and the rate war.** Spring 1862: Portland to The Dalles $25 a ton, Wallula $80, Lewiston $125; passage up to $68 in gold to Lewiston (Throckmorton p. 252, against Gillette's $40, $55 and $120). The People's Transportation Company and Corbett's Merchants Line cut the rates in 1862–63 to $5 to The Dalles and $25 to Lewiston. When Reed's "Opposition Settled" came in June 1863 the Lewiston rate went straight back to $95 (Throckmorton p. 254). One Lewiston merchant had landed flour at $24 a barrel and watched it sell for $9.

**The squeeze of 1866.** In May 1866 Daniel Bradford, writing from Massachusetts, urged his partners to milk the company and trade its stock on inside knowledge (Johansen pp. 186–187). Ainsworth, Reed, Ladd and Thompson took the advice and turned it on him: gloomy reports, stock offered low and bought back (Throckmorton p. 271). By December 1867 they had the company, by the end of 1868 all of it, and they paid themselves the 36.84 per cent dividend. The 1866 "decline" is partly theirs.

**The reach for Montana.** Reed in July 1865: leave "no stone unturned" for the Boise and Owyhee trade (Throckmorton p. 270). Portland and San Francisco merchants put $11,225 into a road along Clark's Fork. In March 1866 Reed wrote from Washington that "St. Louis is wide awake" (p. 271). The *Mary Moody*, built on Lake Pend Oreille from whipsawed lumber with an old Columbia steamer's engines, ran from June 1866 at a cent a pound (p. 272). In 1866 Walla Walla outfitted 2,000 miners for Montana and sent 6,000 mules of freight. Even so, thirty-one steamers landed 4,441 tons at Fort Benton, about half of Montana's freight (pp. 273–274). In the same years Reed got three bills through Congress over Washington Territory's legislature, which would be "reminded that there is a 'power above them'" (Reed to Ainsworth, 29 June 1866; Johansen p. 185).

**Owyhee.** Ainsworth and the company backed one of the two groups fighting over the War Eagle lode; New York capital backed the other (Throckmorton p. 265).

### Idaho's output, a higher series

August C. Bolino, "The Role of Mining in the Economic Development of Idaho Territory", *OHQ* 59 (1958), via Throckmorton pp. 275–276, gold and silver: 1862 $5.0 million; 1863 $7.45m; 1864 $9.02m; 1865 $12.91m; 1866 $10.0m; 1867 $7.39m; 1868 $3.03m; 1869 $1.61m. The years 1862–67 total $51.77 million, against Lindgren's $41.6 million for 1863–68. Both series put the peak at 1865–66 and the fall after 1866. Gold dust assayed at Portland in 1863: $4,505,731 (Throckmorton p. 300).

### Portland's trade

- Portland had 2,917 people in 1860, 4,057 in 1862 (Throckmorton p. 258) and about 7,000 in 1865, when Samuel Bowles called it "half rival to San Francisco itself" (pp. 262–263).
- Portland's merchants bought in New York to keep the Idaho trade from going straight to San Francisco. Mining tools still had to come from San Francisco: no picks sold but Californian ones (p. 261). Corbett had gold pans made in the East and packed in square boxes so San Francisco's dealers would not guess (p. 291).
- In December 1865 the *Oregonian* found only twenty-three kegs of eightpenny nails in all of Portland. An Idaho buyer who could not fill his order went on to San Francisco (p. 262).
- Boise's market was overstocked by autumn 1863. Its merchants failed in numbers in 1865, the year Idaho City burned (p. 263).

### Gold against greenbacks (Throckmorton ch. XIII)

- In 1862 The Dalles pledged to trade only with men "patriotic enough" to take the government's paper at par (pp. 279–280). In January 1863 Portland's merchants agreed to take greenbacks only at San Francisco's market rate (p. 280).
- Debtors who paid in greenbacks at par were blacklisted, and from 1865 they were named in paid notices in the newspapers (p. 281). Ladd told tenants who paid rent in greenbacks to pay in gold or leave (p. 280).
- In October 1864 Oregon's specific contracts law made contracts payable in coin, and the legislature made taxes payable in coin "and not otherwise" (pp. 281–282).
- After the war a slump hit the coast. Failing's profit for 1865 was $2,900 (p. 289). In August 1865 Bowles found greenbacks at about 75 cents in coin. He also reported that, outside San Francisco and "especially in Oregon and Nevada", opinion was turning toward the national currency, because the East was booming and the coast was dull (p. 290).

### Corbett

- On the Union's 1862 victory in Oregon he wrote: "we did not know 'Secesh' was quite so dead" (p. 293 n.).
- In 1866 he bought the Sacramento–Portland stage line and its mail contract (bid $179,000; equipment $94,000), meaning to make it "a permanent Oregon Institution", and advertised "Through in Six Days to Portland". He gave it up on taking his Senate seat in 1867 (pp. 296–297).
- He was among the incorporators of the Northern Pacific in 1864, with Failing and Ainsworth (p. 312).

### Conflicts

- Wells Fargo's shipments from Portland: Throckmorton p. 300 gives $6.2 million for 1865, Poppleton $6.2 million for 1864 and $5.8 million for 1865.
- The river company's 1863 earnings: $1.3 million (Johansen) against $1.5 million (Throckmorton).
- The 1862 rates: Throckmorton against Gillette, as above.

### What it means for CR-059 (MODEL)

1. **The Idaho threat was real before Pacifica.** By 1865 California's wagon roads had most of the trains into southern Idaho and much of the Basin's gold. Here those roads are Pacifican, so in 1866 Portland is already losing Idaho to Pacifica's carriers, and the customs map adds a tariff line to a contest Portland is losing anyway. This strengthens Part V's case.
2. **Coin, the slump and the debtor (decided by the author, 7 November).** The historical slump of 1865–66 is softened here, not erased: less New York currency credit, cheaper credit from Pacifica's banks, the Siskiyou railway's gold wages in the south; Idaho's decline and Montana's loss stand. The argument for paper money shrinks to a debtor's windfall: greenbacks at about 71 cents in gold in 1866 (Throckmorton p. 273) would let a debtor without a gold clause repay at 71 cents and cost Portland's lenders 29 per cent. Written into CR-059 Part V ("The softened slump").
3. **The river company's insiders profit from the uncertainty.** Their squeeze of 1866 happens in the summer of the vote. If the company's board has Pacifican partners (L3), the Christmas dividend of 1867 is what the vote was worth to them, and it is something for Bush to use.

## 7. Set pieces suggested by the sources (PROPOSED, for the author; none adopted)

1. **The ship's ton.** The company charged by measurement, forty cubic feet to the ton, and a wagon was measured with its tongue raised. A small field gun was once hitched to a pair of mules so that its measurement "came out right" (Johansen p. 181). Here the gun is the occupation command's, shipped to The Dalles in 1861–62. It is the first look at the river monopoly that will carry Oregon's vote.
2. **The drays on Front Street (spring 1862).** Drays and trucks stood in line day and night for weeks to reach the docks. A San Francisco merchant's goods lay in Portland all summer because no one held his place (Gillette; Throckmorton p. 258). Seen by a Pacifican customs officer, this is the boom under occupation.
3. **Eph Day, the purser.** At a board meeting Captain Lyles moves to sack the best purser, because his 150-ton boat keeps bringing in cash for 250–300 tons (Throckmorton p. 252). A board scene that introduces Ainsworth's table and its Pacifican partners (L3).
4. **The greenbacker notices.** A Linn County farmer pays his Portland account in Union paper at par and is named in the *Oregonian*. Bush reprints the notice as proof that Portland wants Pacifica to keep the valley in coin debt. Nesmith has to answer for the merchants. It gives the campaign a class edge, debtor against creditor, that cuts against accession.
5. **The Christmas dividend.** May 1866: Bradford's letter arrives from Massachusetts. July: the customs map is published, and the company's stock is talked down. October: Oregon votes. Christmas 1867: the four declare 36.84 per cent. One question carries it all: did they know, or did they make it happen? Elias's part is the author's choice.
6. **The Speaker on the river (summer 1865).** Schuyler Colfax's western tour with Bowles reached Portland in 1865, historically. Here it comes weeks after the armistice. The Union's most prominent visitor sees a town "half rival to San Francisco itself" under Pacifica's peace, and goes home to the House that brings the amendment of 1866 (V7). This ties to open decision 30.
7. **Corbett's coaches.** The Union party's leader buys the Sacramento–Portland stage line in 1866 and advertises "Through in Six Days to Portland", from Pacifican Sacramento. He campaigns for the flag while his coaches carry the Republic's mail. This ties to open decision 31.
8. **Idaho City on a Sunday (1865).** Five to seven thousand people on weekdays, ten to fifteen thousand on Sundays. Houses stand propped on timbers over the diggings, and a church is rented as a courtroom (Throckmorton p. 264). A place for a confederal court to sit, or for Idaho's June vote of 1866.
9. **The *Mary Moody* (spring 1866).** A steamer built from whipsawed lumber on a mountain lake, with an old engine hauled in over the snow, to beat St. Louis to Montana. A Portland bet on reaching the Union's own territory. It is a handoff to CR-B.
10. **"Opposition Settled" (June 1863).** The rate war ends, the Lewiston rate jumps from $25 to $95 overnight, and a merchant who landed flour at $24 a barrel sells it for $9. It shows, in the occupation years, why the river men fear any line drawn across their trade.
