# Civil War chapters — cross-reference of canon and the four planned CRs against Boatner, Hattaway–Jones and Hess

**Date:** 8 November 2026. **Scope:** master storyline Part II (§§9–12) as on `main` (after CR-058-R1), CR-015 sections A, F and H, CR-059 (draft PR #69) and `plan-v2-four-crs.md` (CR-A Oregon, CR-B the interior, CR-C the Southwest, CR-D the peace). **Sources:** Mark M. Boatner III, *The Civil War Dictionary*; Herman Hattaway and Archer Jones, *How the North Won* (cited H&J, printed pages); Earl J. Hess, *Civil War Logistics* (cited by chapter). Each book was read in a separate pass against one claim list; the quotations below were spot-checked in the extracted text. Where I add something from outside the three books I say so.

**What the three books can and cannot check.** They cover the Gulf, the Mississippi, Shiloh, Atlanta, the 1864 election, Union and Confederate logistics and the Sibley–Carleton campaign well. They say nothing about Oregon's politics, Nevada, Deseret, Alaska, the Senate of 1867 or the Official Records vol. 50 material that CR-059 rests on, so CR-A and most of CR-B and CR-D are outside their reach; the notes on those CRs below are limited to the military dates they do touch.

---

## 1. Hard blockers

These are places where canon or the plan states, as history or as mechanism, something these sources show cannot stand as written.

### B1. The Union's Gulf operations of 1862–64 exist only because New Orleans fell — and in canon it does not.

Canon (§11, CR-015 F2) keeps New Orleans Confederate from April 1862 and then carries as plain **Hist.**: the occupation of Galveston (Oct 1862), the repulse at Sabine Pass (8 Sept 1863) and the Rio Grande expedition that takes Brownsville (Nov 1863) "to cut the Matamoros trade", retaken by the Confederates in July 1864.

- Boatner: "Franklin left New Orleans on 5 Sept. with 4,000 men of his XIX Corps (Gulf)" for Sabine Pass; "On 26 Oct. 3,500 men of the XIII Corps, under N. J. T. Dana, left New Orleans with an escort of three warships" for the Rio Grande; the XIII Corps had been "transferred to the Dept. of the Gulf" on 28 July 1863 after Vicksburg; Baton Rouge was occupied 12 May 1862, after New Orleans. Banks's stated purpose was "to discourage any idea the French might have of invading the state from Mexico", not the Matamoros trade.
- Hess (ch. 5): the Rio Grande force was supplied from New Orleans and evacuated in May 1864; the whole Gulf enclave lived on a 2,000-mile coastal supply line at about $300 a man.
- H&J (p. 432): Banks "chose to go by sea" to Texas with the corps Grant sent him.

Without a Union New Orleans there is no Department of the Gulf as a field army, no XIX Corps in Louisiana, no Baton Rouge, and the XIII Corps cannot reach the Gulf at all (the river below Vicksburg is closed at Port Hudson, and canon says so). Galveston is the one survivor: Boatner has it "captured without resistance 5 Oct. '62 by a naval force under Comdr. W. B. Renshaw", so it can stay as a blockading squadron's raid, garrisoned from the fleet rather than from New Orleans. Sabine Pass and the Brownsville expedition must go or be re-mounted from Pensacola with troops the East can spare. The same gap swallows two canon sentences that depend on it: "Union pressure on Port Hudson from the north stops when Chickamauga draws Grant's forces" is the only Union pressure Port Hudson ever faces here (there is no Banks to the south), and the 1871 narrative's "Pensacola and Ship Island give Grant's 1870 attack its geography" is fine.

**Fix:** a Gulf CR (see §4, CR-E) that (a) strikes the two expeditions as Hist., (b) decides the Union's real counter to the Matamoros channel — the blockade off Brazos Santiago, which the Navy kept anyway, with or without a small landing from Pensacola — and (c) disposes of Butler's army (next item).

### B2. Butler's 15,000 at Ship Island are unaccounted for.

Boatner: Ship Island occupied 3 Dec 1861; "during Feb. and Mar. '62 Butler used the island as a staging area for his 15,000 army troops." In canon Farragut fails on 24 April and the Confederates still "burn and abandon" Pensacola on 9 May (Boatner), which the Union occupies. That leaves the largest Union field force in the Gulf idle at Ship Island and Pensacola in summer 1862, fifty miles from a Mobile that Bragg had stripped for Shiloh (Boatner: ~10,000 of the Pensacola army went to Corinth; H&J p. 157: Davis "ordered the abandonment of Pensacola and Mobile" and Bragg declined only on Mobile). Hess (ch. 4, ch. 8) adds why this matters to the Confederacy: until the Selma–Meridian link closed in December 1862 "the only west-east line" ran through Mobile with a bay crossing, Bragg's army crossed Mobile Bay by boat on its way to Chattanooga in July 1862, and Bragg wrote that "If Mobile fell, there would be no west-east link at all."

Canon's "No further Union attempt on New Orleans occurs before 1870" is a decision, but it does not cover Mobile in 1862, and a 15,000-man army with Farragut's surviving squadron beside it is the obvious instrument. The Gulf CR must say what happens to Butler's force (recalled east after the Peninsula, as the XIX Corps division was in 1864; or landed at Pensacola and held there; or expended against Mobile and repulsed, which would double the Mobile legend) and why Mobile survives 1862.

### B3. Farragut in February 1865 and the fleet he would need.

Canon (F4, APPROVED): Farragut leads the February 1865 attack and is killed. Two facts stand against it.

- Boatner (FARRAGUT, p. 276): he "returned in Dec. '64 to N.Y.C., this time in failing health", was made Vice Admiral on 23 Dec 1864, and David D. Porter had held the North Atlantic Squadron since October 1864 and fought Fort Fisher. The ill health is not a consequence of the August 1864 battle, which canon removes; it is a year in the Gulf.
- Hess (ch. 5, ch. 8): the winter of 1864–65 "occupied all the ocean steam vessels of the country" and "exhausted the accumulated stock of coal at Fort Monroe, City Point, and Annapolis" — for Fort Fisher (Boatner: "nearly 60 vessels and 627 guns" plus the transports for 8,000 men, 12–15 Jan 1865) and Schofield's XXIII Corps by sea. H&J (p. 660): shipping Sherman's 60,000 to Virginia would have taken two months, so he marched.

Canon's Mobile attack is a second seaborne armoured expedition in the same six weeks, against a fort line that Boatner's own account says needed the siege train and 5,500 troops in August 1864. The two cannot both be mounted from the Union's ocean fleet in January–February 1865. The CR must choose: either Fort Fisher does not happen in this timeline (Wilmington stays open, which a surviving Confederacy needs anyway), or Mobile is attacked earlier (autumn 1864, before the election, which changes the election beat), or the February attack is a smaller, naval-only affair and the Dauphin Island landing goes. Farragut's presence then needs one sentence: Welles keeps him in the Gulf because Mobile is still untaken, or he insists on returning for the one operation he has waited a year for. Keeping him there in failing health is in character, but it must be stated, since the historical record has him in New York.

### B4. CR-C's winter at Santa Fe is at the edge of what wagons can do, and the plan has no numbers.

The plan has a Pacifican column reach the Rio Grande in summer 1862, take Albuquerque and Santa Fe in autumn, hold a frozen front at Glorieta against a Union-held Fort Union, and "feed Santa Fe through the winter of 1862–63 over a staged road from Tucson", with the comment "That is the logistics test the plan must pass." The sources give the test.

- Hess (ch. 6): a six-mule wagon carries at most 2,674 lb (practically 1,500–2,500, 1,000 on bad roads); an animal eats 26 lb a day against a man's 3; a loaded train makes about 15 miles a day, 26 on the open frontier. Contemporary estimates of the supply radius from a depot: Cox 12 miles, Van Vliet 20, Sherman "50 miles was too far", optimists 100.
- H&J (fn. 14, p. 78): "carrying fodder reduced the practical radius of wagon supply to less than 100 miles. An army 100 miles from rail or water transportation would find its wagons unable to deliver as much as one ounce of cargo unless fodder could be supplied along the route. An army far smaller than Sherman's could have received essentials by wagon because the animals of a smaller number of wagons could be fed along the route."
- Hess (ch. 6) on the very route the Union used: in FY1862–63 Fort Leavenworth shipped more than 6 million lb to Fort Union over 735 miles and 662,720 lb to Salt Lake over 1,837 miles — "regular supply shipments for small garrisons. Feeding and supporting a huge army of hungry soldiers concentrated in one place was an entirely different matter."
- Boatner (NEW MEXICO AND ARIZONA OPERATIONS): Sibley's 2,600 took Santa Fe on 23 March 1862 after the Federals emptied the Albuquerque depot (1 March) and took "120 wagonloads" out of Santa Fe (4 March); he withdrew "being unable to resupply his forces"; Carleton's California Column left southern California on 13 April 1862 and reached Santa Fe on 20 September — five months for eleven infantry companies, two of cavalry and two batteries, marching in detachments.

So the plan's shape is right (Carleton did exactly this march, and the Glorieta line is where two wagon radii meet), but only for a force of garrison size — on the order of 1,500–2,000 men with few animals, paying gold for Rio Grande grain and meat, with ammunition, clothing and specie coming over the Tucson road a few wagons at a time. A "column" that can also besiege Fort Union, which is fed by six million pounds a year from Leavenworth, is not available. The Union side has the same ceiling, which is why the front freezes. CR-C should state the column's strength, its animals, the local purchase mechanism and the Tucson–Mesilla–Santa Fe staging before the Glorieta front is written; the plan's note that Sibley's men took forage where the column pays gold is the right argument, and Boatner's "120 wagonloads" is the measure of what one depot held.

## 2. Constraints that need a written mechanism (not blockers)

**C1. Shiloh, 5 April.** H&J (pp. 163–164) and Boatner agree on the delay: the plan was to attack on 4 April; the army had barely left Corinth by nightfall on the 3rd; the 4th went slowly; the 5th was spent deploying in fog, ready only at 4 p.m.; Beauregard wanted to withdraw. A 5 April attack therefore needs the march of 4 April to go as planned — one good day from green troops on two roads over 25 miles, which is a stretch but not a violation. The harder constraint is Buell: H&J (p. 164) have his army "camping at Savannah" on 5 April, and Boatner has Nelson's lead brigade across in time to fight on the 6th. Moving the battle a day earlier buys hours, not a day, against Buell; what it does buy is Lew Wallace and Nelson arriving into a battle already a day old. Canon's modest outcome (Grant holds the landing, no second-day counterattack, Beauregard withdraws as Buell closes) is consistent with that. The "diverted reinforcements" lever is sound: Boatner confirms the 11th, 13th, 15th and 16th Iowa all fought at Shiloh, the 15th and 16th under colonels commissioned on 22 February and 15 March 1862 — raw regiments that canon sends to Panama instead. One name still has to be chosen: the commander of the green division at Shiloh Church (Sherman's, with Hildebrand, Buckland, McDowell and Stuart as its brigadiers); canon already marks this OPEN.

**C2. Port Hudson and the Vicksburg escape.** Boatner: "On 18 May Johnston had warned Pemberton of the danger of becoming besieged in Vicksburg and had advised the city be abandoned so that the army might be saved. After a council of war, however, Pemberton elected to defend Vicksburg." Canon's "Richmond authorizes preservation of the mobile army" has a clean historical hinge there. Port Hudson is actually stronger than canon says: with no Banks there is no siege from the south at all (Boatner: Banks took it "only after the fall of Vicksburg had rendered it untenable"). What canon must then explain is why Grant, with about 70,000 men free on 4 July (Boatner: 71,000 at the siege), does not take it from the north in July–August before Chickamauga; H&J (pp. 430–431) show Halleck's instinct was exactly "to clean up a little". The escaped Confederate army in Mississippi is the answer available, and should be written as the reason.

**C3. The corridor's value is a crossing, not a supply line.** Canon F3 makes the New Orleans–Port Hudson corridor "why the Confederacy survives as a coherent state." H&J (pp. 421–422, following Goff) hold that the trans-Mississippi link "was not a vital blow in terms of supply management, for the two sections of the Confederacy had long since ceased to rely on each other" and call the Mississippi conquest "almost a white elephant" except psychologically; Hess (ch. 9) shows that after July 1863 only cattle swum across and "individuals and small groups" got over. Canon's corridor does restore something real that history lacked — a secure crossing below Port Hudson for what Pacifica lands at Matamoros — but Hess's wagon numbers mean that what crosses is specie, rifles, machine tools, mines and men (Logan's mission), never bulk. CR-015 F1's "credit and materials" should be narrowed to that, and F3's sentence reframed from logistics to the political and financial fact of an unbroken state.

**C4. Atlanta "held into November" needs a different mechanism than Johnston defending the city.** H&J (pp. 606, 630, 688): Johnston "had not been equal to the task of stopping the more powerful Union army"; he could not have held Atlanta by defence; what might have forestalled its fall was a flank position on the Blue Mountain–Selma railroad — and "Grant's explicit adoption of the strategy of exhaustion through raids ... would have had equal effect without Atlanta's fall had Johnston based himself in Alabama. Sherman could have begun his raid at any time." That is the opening canon needs: with Sherman in Pacifica the western commander is Thomas, of whom Grant wrote that he "would never do to conduct one of your campaigns" (H&J p. 661), and the strategy of raids, which H&J say "distinctly belonged to Grant" but which only Sherman, Baldy Smith and Sheridan understood, has no western executor. Hess (Preface) supplies the other half: the single-track line "could not support further operations south of Atlanta"; a Thomas tied to the railroad does not cut loose. Rewrite §11's "Johnston ... has held Atlanta past the election" as a stalemate on the Chattahoochee and the Alabama flank, not a defence of the city; and give Davis a reason not to remove him (H&J p. 604: he was removed "by not revealing his plans"; here the slower campaign has not yet forced the question).

**C5. The 1864 election leans on the wrong battle.** Canon weighs Atlanta and Mobile. H&J (pp. 624–625) give Mobile Bay no political weight at all ("mattered little"), credit Atlanta with changing the climate, and add that it was "reinforced by Sheridan's victories over Early" (Winchester 19 Sept, Fisher's Hill 22 Sept, Cedar Creek 19 Oct). Canon leaves the Valley campaign untouched, so Lincoln still gets three victories in the six weeks before the vote. Boatner's Lincoln entry says the same more bluntly: "the military successes of Grant and Sherman swung sentiment to him." The election CR (or CR-D) must decide the Valley: either Sheridan's victories happen and are not enough against a stalled West and an untaken Mobile, or Grant, with no Atlanta pressure to exploit, never gives Sheridan the Valley army. H&J also offer a usable beat canon has not touched: on 2 September 1864 Greeley and two other editors wrote to the governors to shelve Lincoln, and the radicals were planning a late-September convention to replace him — a movement that Atlanta's fall killed and that here could run.

**C6. The Union expedition to Puget Sound (§10) has no ships.** Hess records no wartime troop movement across Panama, only prewar fares ($150 an enlisted man, $225 an officer) and freight going round the Horn; the Union's whole ocean fleet (Hess ch. 5: 719 craft on an average day in FY1864–65) was on the Atlantic and Gulf. The Pacific Mail's steamers ran Panama–San Francisco and San Francisco is Pacifican. The Iowa regiments can be taken off the Shiloh roster (C1), but canon needs a line on what carries them from Panama to the Sound and where they coal (Acapulco is neutral Mexico; the Panama-side Pacific Mail ships are Union property caught on the right side of the rupture, which is the mirror of the Sausalito storehouse). The alternative is British charters out of Victoria. This is a sentence, not a CR, but without it §10's expedition is unsupported.

**C7. Hampton Roads: canon's caution is right, and can be made precise.** H&J (pp. 128–133, 178) and Boatner agree: Virginia drew 22 feet, made 4 knots, took thirty minutes to turn, could not go to sea, and "remained permanently in her first port"; she "exercised a powerful effect on McClellan's strategic thinking" until Norfolk fell about 9 May; Totten told the War Board Fort Monroe was "as inaccessible to the Merrimack as if it were in the Moon", while Meigs said "If she gets out, there is nothing in this country that can beat her." Boatner: because of her "the Federal Navy could not protect the transports for a movement up the James" — which was true with Monitor present too. So the marginal change without Monitor is the anchorage at Hampton Roads, not the campaign: McClellan still goes by the York (blocked by batteries until Yorktown falls, as historically), the James stays closed until Norfolk falls, and the transports lie under Fort Monroe and the Rip Raps. One sentence in §11 settles it, and the rest of the Eastern war can stay historical.

## 3. Confirmed or strengthened by the sources

- Pensacola: Confederates "burned and abandoned" the mainland on 9 May 1862; Fort Pickens held throughout; "Pensacola became headquarters for the West Gulf Squadron" (Boatner). Canon's basing is right. Boatner also notes Steele marched 13,000 from Pensacola on Mobile in March 1865, a model for the February attack's land element.
- New Orleans failure mechanics: Boatner has the chain-and-hulk barrier "partially destroyed" by high water in late February and again on 11 April, the *Louisiana* "unpowered", the mortars doing "little damage", and no ship lost in the passage itself. Canon's "maintained obstruction" must therefore survive the high water of 11 April — a repair job for Logan's mission, which is exactly what canon gives him. The army–navy quarrel over where to moor the *Louisiana* is in the standard literature but not in these three books.
- Mobile 1864: Boatner confirms the *Tennessee* was "under-powered", the *Tecumseh* was mined at 7:45, and Granger's 5,500 came from the XIII Corps — which in canon does not exist in the Gulf; the delay also had a historical cause canon can borrow: "Diversion of land forces to take part in Banks's Red River Campaign had delayed Farragut's proposed operations against Mobile." The steering-chain and port-shutter faults are not in Boatner (they are in the battle literature); the "defective fuzes" he does record are a third fault Logan could fix.
- Mobile as the Confederacy's hinge (Hess ch. 4): "If Mobile fell, there would be no west-east link at all" — a line for the storyline.
- Sibley–Carleton chronology, Baylor's proclamation (1 Aug 1861, capital Mesilla), Valverde (21 Feb 1862), Glorieta (26–28 March, "indecisive", Slough fighting against Canby's orders), Carleton's dates — all consistent with CR-C's spine. Boatner notes Baylor was "removed by Davis" (1862), so the Texans at Mesilla in July 1862 were Sibley's rear guard, not Baylor's; CR-022's "Baylor's Texans" should become "the Arizona Confederates" or name Steele (the Steele attribution is mine, not Boatner's).
- Connor: colonel of the 3rd California 4 Sept 1861, Bear River 29 Jan 1863, brigadier only on 30 March 1863 (Boatner) — canon's "local commander" is right.
- Pacific commands (Boatner): Sumner held the Department of the Pacific 25 April–October 1861, Wright from then to 1 July 1864; the District of Oregon was Cady's from 23 Oct 1861 to 7 April 1862 and Alvord's thereafter; Casey was a brigadier in the East from 31 Aug 1861. Canon's Wright in Oregon and Casey at Port Townsend are divergences, not errors, but Casey's needs a sentence (he stays on the Sound because the war is there), and CR-059's Alvord references should not place him before April 1862.
- Halleck (H&J pp. 54–55): San Francisco lawyer, railroad president and California militia major general in 1861, regular major general in August 1861, general in chief 11 July 1862. CR-059's use of him as general in chief refusing coin in March 1863 is consistent. Canon should say in one line why he is in the Union at all when Sherman is not — he was in California with Sherman — because a reader of the character bible will ask.
- The Union's dependence on rivers (H&J pp. 58, 684; Hess Preface) supports canon's shape of the Western war: the advance stops where the Tennessee stops, the Mississippi "completely secure" under gunboats, nothing off the water.
- Overland casualties (>60,000 in six weeks), Early at Fort Stevens 11–12 July, Hampton Roads conference 3 Feb 1865 — as canon.

## 4. Additional CRs to plan

The four planned CRs are about the West and the peace. The blockers above all sit in the Gulf and the Union's war, which none of the four covers. I would add three, and one amendment.

**CR-E — The Gulf without New Orleans, 1862–1865.** Disposes of B1–B3 and C2–C3: Butler's 15,000; Baton Rouge Confederate; no Department of the Gulf field army; Galveston kept as a naval raid; Sabine Pass and Brownsville struck or re-mounted from Pensacola; the Union's real counter to Matamoros (the blockade off Brazos Santiago); why Mobile survives 1862; Grant and Port Hudson in July–September 1863; what crosses the river at the corridor (specie, arms, tools, men — not bulk); the Union's broadside-ironclad programme 1862–64 (how many *New Ironsides* types replace sixty monitors, since the February 1865 attack needs more than the one that existed); the Fort Fisher–Mobile shipping conflict and the sequence chosen; Farragut's presence and health; the February 1865 attack's size. This CR also owns MIL-01's "Pensacola basing and February 1865 broadside-armor attack" audit.

**CR-F — The Union's war without Sherman, 1862–1864.** Owns §11's standing OPEN ("Specific Union command functions must be reassigned because Sherman is Pacifican"), and uses H&J's judgments: the Shiloh Church division's commander; who takes the Army of the Tennessee (McPherson) and the Military Division in 1863–64 (Thomas, with his limits); no Meridian raid; a methodical Atlanta campaign that stalls on the Chattahoochee and the Alabama flank (C4); Davis not removing Johnston; the A. J. Smith divisions that history lent to the Red River staying with the western army (a small plus for the Union, since there is no Red River campaign); Halleck's survival in Union service explained. One sentence on Hampton Roads and the Peninsula (C7) can live here.

**CR-G — The election of 1864 and the lame-duck winter.** Owns C5: Sheridan's Valley campaign (happens or not; enough or not); the 2 September editors' letters and the radicals' September convention as a live movement; the October state elections; the soldier vote; the Greeley thread that canon already uses for 1872. It should also hold the "last winter offensive" pairing (Petersburg and Mobile), because B3 may move Mobile's date.

**Amendment to §10 / CR-A:** the Pacific sealift sentence (C6) — the Pacific Mail ships caught at Panama, or British charters from Victoria — and Casey's decision to stay.

**Inside CR-C:** the force-size and winter-supply table (B4), the column's commander (Carleton himself is a choice to make: he was a regular stationed in California in 1861), and the Mesilla attribution.

## 6. Not checkable from these three books

Oregon's House vote and Enabling Act, the Deseret convention and Compact, Nye and Fort Churchill, the Nevada constitutions, the Alaska sale, the 1866–67 Senate, the Matamoros cotton road's capacity, McClellan's 1853 Cascades survey, the Pacific Squadron and *Lancaster*, Lot Smith's company, Camp Douglas, the Morrill Act. For the Southwest and the interior the next sources to pull are Alvin Josephy, *The Civil War in the American West*; Donald Frazier, *Blood and Treasure* (Sibley); Aurora Hunt, *The Army of the Pacific* and *Major General James Henry Carleton*; Ray Colton, *The Civil War in the Western Territories*; for the Gulf, Chester Hearn, *The Capture of New Orleans, 1862* (the *Louisiana* placement quarrel) and Jack Friend, *West Wind, Flood Tide* (Mobile Bay, the *Tennessee*'s faults); for the Matamoros trade, James Irby, *Backdoor at Bagdad*. These are from my own knowledge, not from the three books.
