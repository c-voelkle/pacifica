## 1. Lincoln's exact texts (spot-check before prose drafting)

**Finding:** Checked against documentary editions other than Basler:
- **First inaugural (G1):** "If by the mere force of numbers a majority should deprive a minority of any clearly written constitutional right", it might morally justify revolution, and certainly would if the right were vital. Matches the CR.
- **12 January 1848 (AG2, M3):** "Any people anywhere, being inclined and having the power, have the right to rise up, and shake off the existing government, and form a new one that suits them better." Papers of Abraham Lincoln prints it from Lincoln's manuscript; the *Globe* Appendix, 93–95, follows it closely.
- **Lincoln to Seward, 1 April 1861 (K14):** "if this must be done, I must do it" (see item 10).
- **Blockade proclamation (K16):** see item 11.
- **4 July 1861 message (Part A, A3b, S24): disagrees.** The published question reads: "are all the laws, *but one*, to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated?" The CR, following Masters 415, has "whether all the laws but one should go unexecuted, and that one be observed, though the Union go to pieces". "That one be observed" and "though the Union go to pieces" are not in the published text. Masters seems to paraphrase inside quotation marks.
- "whether strictly legal or not" is in the published message (drafter's knowledge; the excerpt fetched did not include that sentence, so confirm in Basler 4:429).
**Source:** usinfo.org text of the first inaugural (https://usinfo.org/PUBS/LivingDoc_e/firstinaugural.htm); Papers of Abraham Lincoln D200444 (https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/documents/D200444); Teaching American History, 4 July message excerpt (https://teachingamericanhistory.org/library/document/message-to-congress-in-special-session/).
**Verdict:** partly. Not checked: the Lyceum address, Peoria, the 1858 debates, the "If all earthly power" passage, the 1842 temperance address.
**Draft change needed:** S24, Part A (row with Masters 415) and A3b should quote the published question, e.g. whether all the laws "but one" were "to go unexecuted, and the government itself go to pieces, lest that one be violated". A3b's sentence can stay as a question; only the quoted words change.

## 2. The Ashmun amendment (M3)

**Finding:** Ashmun moved to add that the war was "unnecessarily and unconstitutionally begun by the President of the United States" to the resolution of thanks to General Taylor, introduced by John W. Houston on 3 January 1848. The House adopted it with Lincoln voting yea. The tally differs between the sources: 82 to 81 in the House *Journal*, 85 to 81 in the *Globe*. The editors cite *Cong. Globe*, 30th Cong., 1st Sess., 59, 95, 320.
**Source:** Papers of Abraham Lincoln, Lincoln to Herndon, 1 Feb 1848, note 4 (https://papersofabrahamlincoln.org/documents/D200453).
**Verdict:** answered. The date is 3 January 1848 (the editors place it in the 3 January proceedings; the Globe's p. 95 is that day).
**Draft change needed:** if the Chronicler gives the tally, use "by a majority of one, or of four, as the *Journal* or the *Globe* has it", or pick 85–81 and footnote the *Journal*'s 82. M3's date range can start at "3 January 1848" for the vote, with the spot resolutions of 22 December before it.

## 4. The December 1862 annual message (M10)

**Finding:** Message of 1 December 1862. The proposed amendment's first article compensates, in interest-bearing United States bonds, every state that abolishes slavery "before the first day of January, A.D. 1900", paid in instalments or at once depending on gradual or immediate abolition; a state that reintroduces slavery repays. A separate article lets Congress fund colonization of free people who consent.
**Source:** Teaching American History, Annual Message 1862 (https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/annual-message-to-congress-1862/).
**Verdict:** agrees.
**Draft change needed:** drop "(Hist.; to verify)" in M10. The Chronicler's "eleven years before" holds if the Confederacy's last slaves are freed in 1889 (1900 − 1889 = 11). Note the bonds: "by the method he had proposed" is accurate if Pacifica's money buys out owners by instalments.

## 6. Lincoln's wish to see the Pacific coast (L4)

**Finding:** Colfax's account of 14 April 1865 has Lincoln ask about Colfax's trip west, promise to telegraph him at San Francisco and send the miners a message that he would promote their interests. That is about Colfax's journey, not Lincoln's own. The wish to travel to California after the presidency comes from Mary Lincoln's later recollection of their last carriage ride, which also names Europe and Palestine. Bowles, who wrote *Across the Continent* (1865), was in Colfax's party.
**Source:** Lincoln Lore no. 1295, 1 Feb 1954 (https://friendsofthelincolncollection.org/lore-archive/ll_1954-02-01_01); Abraham Lincoln's Classroom, "Abraham Lincoln and California", citing W. H. Smith, *Schuyler Colfax*, 207–8, and Shutes, *Lincoln and California*, 180–81 (https://www.abrahamlincolnsclassroom.org/abraham-lincoln-state-by-state/abraham-lincoln-and-california/index.html); Mental Floss (weak) on Mary Lincoln's letter (https://mentalfloss.com/article/53409/abraham-lincolns-retirement-plans).
**Verdict:** partly. The wish is real but its witness is Mary Lincoln, not Colfax.
**Draft change needed:** L4: "Historically, on the afternoon of 14 April 1865, Lincoln spoke to his wife of going to California after his term (her later letter), and that evening sent the miners a message by Colfax." The letter's recipient and date are still to confirm (probably her letter to F. B. Carpenter, late 1865).

## 9. Nietzsche, "Homer's Contest" (Part A)

**Finding:** Part A quotes no Nietzsche and uses only the idea: ostracism removes the man who has no rival so that the contest can go on (Heraclitus on the Ephesians' banishment of Hermodorus). A close reading of the essay confirms that is Nietzsche's frame: he judges ostracism by the health of the city's competition, not the exile's fate. The Cambridge translation is Carol Diethe's (in the *Genealogy* volume, 1994).
**Source:** T. H. Wilson, "Homer on Competition", *Minerva* 9 (2005) (https://minerva.mic.ul.ie/vol9/Homer.html).
**Verdict:** agrees in substance. Kaufmann's wording not checked.
**Draft change needed:** none. If the Chronicler or *On the Public Arts* ever quotes the essay, check the words against Diethe's or Kaufmann's text then.

## 10. Seward's memorandum of 1 April 1861 (K14)

**Finding:** Seward would "demand explanations from Spain and France, categorically, at once"; "seek explanations from Great Britain and Russia"; send agents into Canada, Mexico and Central America; and, if Spain and France did not satisfy, "convene Congress and declare war against them". Lincoln's reply the same day: "if this must be done, *I* must do it."
**Source:** jfepperson.org transcription, from the Lincoln Papers at the Library of Congress (mal0866000, mal0860800) (https://www.jfepperson.org/SumterSewardLincoln0401.htm).
**Verdict:** answered. K14's parenthesis has it backwards: Spain and France were to be *demanded* of categorically and threatened with war; Britain and Russia only *asked*.
**Draft change needed:** K14: "Hist.: explanations demanded categorically of Spain and France, sought of Britain and Russia; war on Spain and France if unsatisfied". Drop "to verify" on Lincoln's reply. Masters naming only Britain and Russia is an omission, not an error.

## 11. The blockade proclamation of 19 April 1861 (K16)

**Finding:** The proclamation says anyone who, under the seceding states' authority or any other pretence, "shall molest a vessel of the United States or the persons or cargo on board of her" will be "held amenable to the laws of the United States for the prevention and punishment of piracy". Timing west: the Sumter dispatch written in St. Louis on 14 April was telegraphed to Fort Kearny, carried by Pony Express and reached Fort Churchill on 24 April, then went by telegraph to California the same day (about ten days). A 19 April text on the same route reaches San Francisco about 29 April to 1 May.
**Source:** American Presidency Project (https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/proclamation-81-declaring-blockade-ports-rebellious-states); Anne T. Kent California Room, "Pony Express speeds news" (https://medium.com/anne-t-kent-california-room-community-newsletter/pony-express-speeds-news-of-lincolns-election-civil-war-to-marin-c66a03c2d2ba).
**Verdict:** partly. The clause and the timing agree with K16. The Pacific squadron's orders (*Official Records of the Navies*, ser. I, vol. 1) not checked.
**Draft change needed:** drop "to verify" on the piracy clause; K16's "about the first of May" holds. Note: the clause covers molesting US vessels, so the Union captain's reading it as licence to take an agent from a neutral packet is a stretch, which suits canon's "the officer exceeds his authority".

## 12. Edward D. Baker (K13, K20)

**Finding:** Baker was killed at Ball's Bluff on 21 October 1861 while a senator from Oregon and a colonel. Lincoln heard the news at McClellan's headquarters; John Hay recorded his grief, and another account has him stumbling as he left. (One site gives 22 October for the death; the battle and his death were on the 21st.)
**Source:** Mr. Lincoln's White House, "Edward D. Baker", citing Hay via Oates, and Pratt, *Concerning Mr. Lincoln*, 78–79 (https://www.mrlincolnswhitehouse.org/residents-visitors/visitors-from-congress/visitors-congress-edward-d-baker-1811-1861/index.html).
**Verdict:** partly. The "telegraph office" in K20 is better put as "McClellan's headquarters", where the telegraph was. Blair and Tarshis, Hunt's edition of the speeches, and the "California" regiment not checked.
**Draft change needed:** K20: "the news reaches Lincoln at McClellan's headquarters (Hist.: Hay's diary)". Drop "to verify".

## 13. Pyramid Lake and the Pony Express (K10)

**Finding:** First battle 12 May 1860 (Ormsby killed); second battle 2 June 1860. In November 1860 the Pony took six and a half days from Fort Kearny to Fort Churchill, and Sumter news took about ten days from St. Louis to Fort Churchill in April 1861. News of a battle near Pyramid Lake on the evening of 12 May could not reach Chicago by the ballots of 18 May.
**Source:** Nevada SHPO, "The Two Battles of Pyramid Lake" (https://shpo.nv.gov/nevadas-historical-markers/historical-markers/the-two-battles-of-pyramid-lake); Anne T. Kent California Room (above).
**Verdict:** agrees.
**Draft change needed:** K10: replace "early June" with "2 June" where the second battle is named; drop "Pony Express timing to verify". The Pony also ran eastbound more slowly in May 1860 because the war disrupted the Nevada stations (drafter's knowledge; to verify if used).

## 14. Lincoln's campaign silence in 1860 (K11)

**Finding:** To William S. **Draft change needed:** none. Cite the Speer letter (Basler vol. 4) at integration.

## 15. Lincoln and Plutarch (K27)

**Finding:** The biographer was **John Locke Scripps** of the *Chicago Press and Tribune*, not Howells. That Lincoln then read it rests on later recollection, not checked here.
**Source:** Scripps to Lincoln, 17 July 1860, Lincoln Papers, via House Divided (https://hd.housedivided.dickinson.edu/node/34470); Burlingame, "Writing Lincoln's Lives" (https://housedivided.dickinson.edu/essay/essay_burlingame_campaign.html).
**Verdict:** disagrees on the name; the letter itself confirmed.
**Draft change needed:** K27: "after Scripps's campaign biography said he had (Hist.: Scripps to Lincoln, 17 July 1860)". Research item 15's "by Howells" becomes "by Scripps". Whether he read it afterwards stays "to verify".

## 17. The inaugural and the 4 July message on revolution (G1)

**Finding:** The inaugural sentence is confirmed (item 1). "Morally justifiable cause" is Lincoln's: McPherson quotes him, dated to the summer of 1861, saying the right of revolution is never a legal right and at most a moral one, "when exercised for a morally justifiable cause". The published 4 July message has no such passage; its only use of "revolution" is that the states can leave only "against law and by revolution".
**Source:** McPherson, *Abraham Lincoln and the Second American Revolution* (1991), ch. 1, p. 28 as reproduced at West Valley College (https://instruct.westvalley.edu/kelly/History17a_on_campus/Readings/Lincon.htm); Teaching American History, 4 July message (https://teachingamericanhistory.org/document/message-to-congress-in-special-session-4/).
**Verdict:** partly. The phrase survives only in a draft (Basler prints draft passages in notes; page not confirmed).
**Draft change needed:** G1 may use the phrase only as an unpublished draft: Lincoln wrote it and struck it out, so in-world no one reads it in 1861. Either give it to the Chronicler (who has the papers) or keep Lincoln's public answer to the inaugural's sentence and the message's "against law and by revolution".

## 19. Hawaiian-language newspapers, 1861–63 (G5)

**Draft change needed:** G5 can name the *Kuokoa* as the paper that translates it (PROPOSED unless found). Next route: Papakilo Database or nupepa.org for *Kuokoa* issues of February–March 1863.

## 25. A loyal government made from the loyal (G7)

**Finding:** Nevada's and Colorado's enabling acts were both approved on 21 March 1864 (13 Stat. 30, 32). Colorado's first convention adopted a constitution on 11 July 1864, and the voters rejected it on 11 October 1864, 4,672 to 1,520, chiefly because it came with a slate of state officers chosen in advance. ## 30. Never styling Davis a president (open decision 5)

## 35. The Dakota war and Lincoln (G4)

**Finding:**

- Lincoln's message to the Senate of 11 December 1862: 39 ordered executed, from those proven to have taken part in massacres as distinct from battles. One was reprieved on 23 December, so 38 were hanged at Mankato on 26 December. The CR had "from 303 to 38".

## 37. Jefferson and the Pierce letter (G6)

**Finding:**
