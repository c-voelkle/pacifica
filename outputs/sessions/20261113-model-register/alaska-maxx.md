# Alaskamaxxing: how Pacifica makes Alaska pay sooner

*Investigation for the author, 14 November 2026. Nothing here is canon. Every idea is PROPOSED; every amount is a MODEL estimate. Historical claims are marked Hist. and sourced at the end.*

## 1. The problem in one paragraph

Package D strong spends $5.7m a year on Sitka (garrison, squadron and northern mail), 1867–1900. That costs about $9m of the 1932 Stress margin per $1m a year. The early years are the dearest, because their cost compounds longest. The author's taper (E218: the garrison at a share of itself from 1880) shows this. Half strength from 1880 recovers $6.1m of the 1932 margin and 30% recovers $8.5m (phase 6, `levers3.xlsx`). The rest of the $43m stays lost, because most of it was spent before 1880. So the target is the first fifteen years: spend less on soldiers and find the money sooner.

For scale: Japan's whole ten-year plan for Hokkaido (1872–81) was budgeted at 10 million yen, and outlays came to about 20 million (Hist.). The 1871 yen was roughly a gold dollar (Hist.; to verify). Sitka at $5.7m a year spends that decade's whole Hokkaido outlay in under four years, on a territory of about 33,000 people in 1880, most of them Native (Hist.). The money has to buy more than soldiers.

## 2. What the historical Union did slowly or not at all

| | What happened (Hist.) | Delay or loss |
|---|---|---|
| Government | Army rule 1867–77, then a customs collector and the navy; no civil government, courts or land and mining law until the Organic Act of 17 May 1884. Before 1884 nobody could get title to a mineral property; Juneau ran on a miners' code. | 17 years without mining title |
| Security | The army left Sitka on 15 June 1877. In 1879 the town called a British warship, HMS *Osprey*, for protection; the scare was exaggerated, and two Tlingit leaders, Katlian and Annaxóots, kept order. Army rule had included the shelling of Kake (1869) and Wrangell. | A costly presence that made enemies |
| Gold | Doroshin found placer gold on the Kenai in 1848–51; the Russian American Company judged it too thin. The telegraph expedition of 1865–67 noted gold traces on the Niukluk (Seward Peninsula) and near Fort Yukon. Juneau came in 1880, when the Auk leader Kowee answered a Sitka engineer's reward ("100 Hudson Bay blankets and work for the tribe") with ore and sent the prospectors back when they missed it. Treadwell, the world's largest hard-rock gold mine in its time, followed. Nome came in 1898–99, Kennecott's copper in 1899–1900 after Chief Nicolai showed the deposit. Alaska produced about $5m of gold in 1880–96 and over $32m in 1895–1901. | 30 years from the first traces to the main fields |
| Seals | The Alaska Commercial Company paid $317,500 a year (rent plus $2.625 a skin on 100,000 skins). A pelt sold for under $5 in the late 1860s and about $40 by 1900, and the lease left the rise with the lessee. Pelagic sealing grew from 1884; the quota fell from 100,000 to 21,000 in 1890 and 7,549 in 1892; by 1910 the herd was about a tenth of its 1867 size. The ban came only in 1911. | A renewable rent wasted |
| Salmon | The first canneries came in 1878 (8,159 cases). By about 1890 the pack was worth $3.4m; by 1900 it was 1.5m cases. Over 1880–1937 the value of canned salmon exceeded that of Alaska's minerals. San Francisco owned the canneries, and the territory got little from them. | A decade lost; no public share |
| Routes | The Chilkoot clans held the passes as a trade monopoly. In 1880 the navy opened the Chilkoot to prospectors under the threat of its Gatling gun. The Tlingit then built a packing business that the 1897–98 stampede overran. | The road was taken, not built with them |
| Coal | The Russian American Company mined coal at Port Graham from 1855 and lost money. British coal sold in San Francisco at $15 a ton, Australian at $8, Japanese at $5. | A warning, not an opportunity |
| Telegraph | Western Union spent $3m on a San Francisco–Bering–Siberia line in 1865–67 and abandoned it when the Atlantic cable worked. | Assets and surveys thrown away |

## 3. The playbook

Each idea gives the historical anchor, why Pacifica in particular can do it, how much sooner, and what it is worth: to the Treasury, which is what the 1932 margin needs, and to the economy (gold, growth, people).

### A. Govern from the first day: the Northern Commission (1867)

**Idea.** On the day the flag changes, Alaska gets the Mining Law of 1849, an assay office at Sitka, claims courts and a land office. A Northern Commission runs the territory as Kuroda's Kaitakushi runs Hokkaido. Canon already sends Pacifican advisers north to Kuroda. Here the traffic runs both ways, and Hokkaido and Alaska become one northern school: Sapporo's agronomists, Sitka's assayers, the same problems of cold, fish, coal and the peoples who were there first.

**Why Pacifica.** Elias's administrative framework for assays, claims, courts, safety, royalties and records exists from 1848–49 (canon, the Mining Law of 1849). The Union needed 17 years to give Alaska a mining title; Pacifica needs a proclamation.

**Effect.** It is the precondition for everything below. There is no direct money; it removes the 1867–84 delay.

### B. Buy the Russian archive with the territory

**Idea.** Pablo de la Guerra (CR-062 K3) asks for the Russian American Company's papers as a schedule to the treaty: Doroshin's Kenai prospecting notes and coal surveys, Tebenkov's coastal atlas, the Pribilof counts, the Creole pilots' knowledge. Doroshin published his account in the Russian *Mining Journal* in 1866. The Creoles of Sitka and Kodiak, Pacifican citizens under CR-062, staff the commission's survey; CR-045's Kashevarov family, Sitka Creoles, belongs here.

**Effect.** It saves the survey years and gives a story thread: Russian America's unfinished science is finished by Pacifica.

### C. The finder's compact (the biggest lever)

**Idea.** Pilz's reward, turned into law and treaty. Any nation that leads the commission to a workable deposit on its land receives, by compact, a share of the royalty and first rights to the work: packing, provisioning, wages. This is how the Me-Wuk lease, the White Bluffs compact and the Columbia compacts already work in canon. Kowee and Nicolai were not lucky accidents. They were people who knew where the gold and copper were and were waiting for a fair offer.

**Why Pacifica.** CR-062 leaves "the nations' lands and rights to their own arrangements" and defers the arrangements to a later CR; this is that CR's economic core. It also inverts the historical record honestly: the US shelled Kake; Pacifica pays Kowee.

**How much sooner.** Juneau, eight years sooner (1872 instead of 1880): the samples were there for anyone who asked. The Copper River's copper is known in the 1870s–80s, and is worked once a railway is affordable (1890s rather than 1911).

**Worth (MODEL).** The Treasury takes only the 5% royalty (Levers row 18), so the direct receipts are small: about $0.05m a year in the 1870s, rising to $0.1m. The gold itself raises output, money and migration, and those are what move 1932. The workbook already has Alaska gold from 1880 (Resources E, $0.1m then, $2m by 1895); this would shift that path eight years earlier.

### D. The telegraph survey's gold: the Seward Peninsula in the 1870s

**Idea.** In 1867 Pacifica buys Western Union's abandoned Russian-American line for a song: its stations, wire, stores and survey notes. In those notes David Libbey of the 1865 expedition recorded gold on the Niukluk (Hist.); he went back to prospect thirty years later. Pacifica sends a party at once. San Francisco's Arctic whaling fleet passes Norton Sound every summer (Hist.: San Francisco became the North Pacific's whaling port in the 1880s), so supply is a matter of a contract with the whalers.

**The line itself, a wild card.** The Polytechnic holds the telegraph (canon, CR-027). Finishing the Bering link joins San Francisco to Russia's line, which reached Vladivostok and the Nagasaki cable in 1871 (Hist.; to verify). That gives Pacifica a wire to Japan and to Europe that never touches a British cable. It would lose money for years, with Arctic maintenance and little traffic. Its value is strategic: the gold room's prices, the Navy's orders, Tokyo's news. Proposed only as an option.

**Worth (MODEL, speculative).** Placer gold on the Niukluk and at Nome from about 1876, $0.5–1.5m a year into the 1880s. Historically the Seward Peninsula yielded about 6 million ounces from 1897 (Hist.), so there is plenty in the ground; the question is only how early gold that far north pays.

### E. Seals: sell the skins, not the island

**Idea.** Three changes to E217:
1. **Tender, don't grant.** The lease goes to the best bidder per skin, re-tendered every ten years. Historically the 1890 bidder offered $60,000 rent and $9.625 a skin when the herd was already failing (Hist., *U.S. v. North American Commercial Co.*). Pacifica captures the price rise the 1870 lease gave away.
2. **Save the herd fifteen years early.** Pelagic sealing becomes visible in 1884. Pacifica proposes the conservation regime then, not in the 1890s; that date is PROPOSED in CR-058, the regime itself APPROVED. It has the standing: owner of the Pribilofs, Japan's ally, friend of Russia, Canada's diplomatic tutor. The quota of 100,000 holds through 1900.
3. **Dress and dye at home.** The skins went San Francisco–New York–London for dressing and dyeing, a London craft monopoly, and came back paying duty (Hist.). The Polytechnic's chemists crack the dye, and the fur auction moves to San Francisco. Most of that value goes to private industry, not the Treasury.

**Worth (MODEL).** Treasury about $0.32m a year in 1868–79 (as now), $0.6m in the 1880s, $1.0m in the 1890s (100,000 skins at the 1890 bid). That is about +$10m to 1900 over the historical lease, and the herd still exists in 1932. This is the only large early receipt.

### F. Salmon from 1870, with a conservation law and a case tax

**Idea.** Pacifica's Columbia canners (the river's first cannery 1866, Hist.; to verify) are Pacifican after 1867, so the capital and the skill go north a decade early. A conservation law, a tax per case, Native fisheries reserved by compact, and the Metlakatla model (a cannery partly financed by Tsimshian villagers, Hist.) for the nations that want their own.

**Worth (MODEL).** At a tax of about 4% of the pack's value: $0.05m a year in the 1870s, $0.15m in the 1880s, $0.3m in the 1890s. Small but early, rising every year, and it lasts to 1932.

### G. The Chilkoot compact and the road to the Yukon

**Idea.** In place of Beardslee's Gatling gun (1880), a toll compact with the Chilkoot and Chilkat clans: their packers and their trade keep the pass; Pacifica and the clans build a tramway and a wagon road together by the Fortymile strike (1886, Hist.). When Bonanza Creek comes in on 16 August 1896, the route already works. Canon already proposes the Skagway–Whitehorse railway, assay office and bank (storyline; workbook E61, citing §25, $1m a year 1899–1901). This makes them ready three years earlier and gives the clans their share. Canada's free river navigation (CR-058) is untouched: this is a land route on Pacifican soil, offered on fair terms.

**Worth (MODEL).** The Klondike produced $2.5m in 1897, $10m in 1898, $16m in 1899 and $22m in 1900 (Hist.). The public share comes from assay charges, the bank and freight, about $0.3–0.5m a year from 1897, and it brings the Canadian gold to San Francisco's reserve. Much of what the 1898 stampede spent on outfitting flows through Pacifican merchants anyway.

### H. Sitka as a station, not a fortress; the settler garrison

**Idea.** The Russians held Southeast Alaska by working with the Tlingit; the Union's army made enemies (Hist.). Pacifica's presence is the squadron, revenue cutters, the survey, the mail and the compacts, with a small garrison. The soldiers are Hokkaido-style settler soldiers (*tondenhei*): they serve five years and receive land, a fishing licence and a claim right. The garrison becomes the territory's first residents, and its cost falls as it settles. That is the author's taper with a story.

**Worth (MODEL).** The taper is built (cw_sitka_taper, E218). The settler garrison argues for starting it in the early 1870s, not 1880, because that is where the dear years are.

### I. Unalaska: the coaling station on the great circle

**Idea.** The purchase's own reason, the Aleutian route to Japan (canon §14). Steamers on the San Francisco–Yokohama great circle pass the Aleutians. Unalaska sells them Japanese coal from Takashima (canon: part of the 1870–71 alliance), not Alaskan coal, which lost money against $5 Japanese coal (Hist.). It doubles as the navy's northern station.

**Worth.** Small margins; mostly strategic. It also takes some of the squadron's cost out of Sitka.

### J. Smaller things worth a line each

- **Fox islands.** Blue-fox leases on the Aleutians for Unangan villages (Hist.: the Commerce Department later sold Pribilof fox skins).
- **Reindeer from Chukotka.** Brought over for the Iñupiat when whaling strips their food: a welfare measure (Hist. 1892; here the 1870s).
- **Inside Passage tourism.** Steamers to the glaciers after Muir (1879); prosperous California is the market.
- **Copper on the Copper River with Nicolai's people** (see C). The world's richest copper ore is found in 1900 (Hist.). In Pacifica it feeds the electric century of CR-052, after 1890.

### What not to do

- Mine Alaskan coal for export.
- Hunt the sea otter out. The Aleut hunters' living went with it (Hist.).
- Let San Francisco's canners and lessees take the north's rents with nothing left in the territory: the complaint of the 1890s (Hist.).
- Write the Treaty of Cession's "uncivilized tribes" clause (CR-062 already refuses it).

**The arithmetic.**
- The receipts alone come nowhere near $5.7m a year; nothing Alaska yields before the 1880s does.
- Making Sitka pay for itself early is mostly the cost side: the settler garrison, the station not the fortress, and a taper that starts in the 1870s.
- The receipts carry the 1880s and 1890s. The gold works through the economy, not the Treasury.
- Honest expectation: with the taper from 1873 at 40% and the receipts above, D strong should recover a large part of the $25m. It will not recover all of it unless the early garrison itself is smaller. Only a run can say.

## 6. Canon tensions

1. **§14:** "Northern gold income belongs to the later Juneau and Klondike developments; the northern administration must initially pay for harbors and transport without a ready gold windfall." Case A, adapted: no early gold; only C and D's later dates, seals, salmon and the taper. Case B, amended: Juneau about 1872 and the Niukluk about 1876, with the sentence rewritten. Recommendation: B for Juneau, which follows from the compacts; D as an author's choice.
2. **CR-058:** the conservation regime "in the 1890s" (the date is PROPOSED) becomes the mid-1880s. Recommendation: amend; the regime itself is unchanged.
3. **§25:** the Klondike route policy is unchanged in substance. The tramway and the clans' share are new.

Protected intent: no conflict. The compacts are the opposite of a campaign of extermination.

**The other figures:**

**Reading:**
- The taper is the large item, because settler soldiers replace a standing garrison in the dear early years.
- Seals and salmon are the receipts that matter.
- Early gold barely moves the Treasury: Alaska's output is small beside the economy, and the state takes 5%.
- The telegraph is the price of its strategic value.
