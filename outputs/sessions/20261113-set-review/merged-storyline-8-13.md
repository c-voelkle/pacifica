## 8. The constitutional test of public money and private force (1850–1861)

California's revenues invite domestic conflicts that Elias cannot solve with engineering alone. The Sacramento squatter crisis of 1850 and San Francisco's Committee of Vigilance in 1851 expose the republic's weak courts and the ability of wealthy or armed private associations to govern in place of elected institutions. President Vallejo hesitates to confront powerful merchants. Elias argues that the treasury and the law must be defended by public institutions rather than competing private armies.

In 1852 the assembly reorganizes government at Monterey, creating a Council of State and elevating Elias to **Superintendent of Mines and Public Revenue**. He controls the collection and investment of mineral royalties, the mint, public credit and important works contracts but does not become president. He administers a financial state in which taxes, railway subsidies, foreign loans and port revenues are contested by legislators who can still frustrate him.

A regular Californian army is organized in 1852, alongside an academy at Monterey. The state develops its Benicia arsenal, Mare Island naval facilities and the foundries on the Carquinez Strait. Skilled immigrants and officers from several military traditions join Californio lancers, Mexican and Indigenous scouts, seamen and engineers. Marcus Vance, a relative of Caleb who joins the republic in the aftermath of the rush, gradually develops a professional command and military training system. Elias continues to direct money more readily than he can command troops.

# PART II — The struggle for North America (1861–1867)

## 9. The republic faces the American Civil War (1861)

Fort Sumter's bombardment in April 1861 transforms the Confederation's strategic position. # PART III — Ocean partners, continental dependence and industrial limits (1865–1887)

This part follows the construction of the Pacific network and interleaves with the Hawaiian and family story in Part IV. Its major commitments are separated by consolidation. Pacifica seeks local naval superiority, paying and supplying partners who fight on land. Acquisitions must earn their cost within about ten years; there is one operation at a time, no populated mainland annexation outside North America, no Pacifican land war against Russia, and no war with Britain before 1900.

## 13. Winning the peace and binding the continental partner (1865–1875)

The prewar technical community turns wartime experience into expanding capacity. Donahue's workshops and Huntington's procurement, Ralston's credit and Crocker's construction teams, and Kelly's experiments, Holley's plant engineering and Hallidie's material handling contribute different parts of a cumulative industrial system. Outside processes, licensing, fuel, workers and capital still determine what can be built. Logan's Louisiana repair relationships offer an engineering base and commercial opportunity; no private fortune or monopoly follows automatically.

**[PROPOSED]** Demobilization brings land offers and paid contracts for engineers, naval officers and shipwrights from both American armies. Sierra railway crews remain employed on interior links. As France withdraws, a Baja purchase in 1867, paid over five years, a Guaymas railway concession and free port secure the southern flank. Mexico retains Sonora. Prices, garrisons and firms await audit.

The convention that sets up the post also ends the scalp bounties in Sonora and Chihuahua. Maximilian is shot at Querétaro on 19 June 1867, in the city where Elias and Alvarado signed the convention of 1847, and Mexico's liberals learn that Pacifica, not Washington, ended the French adventure. Monterey restrains its Confederate partner, whose exiles had courted Maximilian and settled in his empire. Juárez's words of 15 July 1867, that among individuals, as among nations, respect for the rights of others is peace, become the common language of the continent's neighbours.

The Confederate economy needs credit, markets and protection. A railway treaty in 1866–1867 gives Huntington's line a route across Texas to New Orleans. Johnston succeeds Davis in February 1868 after his 1867 election. Richmond's Mississippi tolls and fugitive raids into Union Tennessee give Grant a popular cause; elected in 1868, he attacks in July 1870 while Europe is occupied by the Franco-Prussian War. Pacifica sends a Gulf squadron, turret ships, mines under Logan, rifles and credit, and closes its Pacific ports to Union shipping. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** Hawaiian schooners raise the Confederation's flag over the remaining American guano bonds in the Phoenix group, at Kiritimati and on Swains, without firing a shot. It sends no field army and undertakes no Asian military commitment during this war.

Union attacks fail at New Orleans in autumn 1870 and stall against entrenched lines in Virginia. Among the Texan volunteers at New Orleans is Silas Pruett, nineteen, who sees Pacifican mines and credit at work. After the association treaties he takes a commission in the Atlantic Auxiliary. In spring 1871 Sherman negotiates with Grant, whose friendship now crosses national allegiances. Richmond restores free Mississippi navigation; Pacifica guarantees Confederate independence. <!-- CR-068: the river -->**[APPROVED — CR-068]** Free navigation of the Mississippi is Pacifica's condition for that guarantee. **[APPROVED — CR-029]** The Union quitclaims the Pacific islands Pacifica occupied during the war. <!-- CR-058: the neighbours in Grant's war -->
**[APPROVED — CR-058]** Grant's war is part of a plan. Having lost the West, the Union means to make up for it in the Caribbean, on the Mississippi and in the north, and each neighbour meets a different part of the plan. Canada meets it in Sumner's speech of 13 April 1869, which puts the Alabama damages at about $2.1 billion, swollen by the raiders that helped the South win, a sum read on both sides of the Atlantic as payable only in Canada; in the Fenian raids of May 1870, which Washington tolerates; and in the refusal that month to let the steamer *Chicora* through the Sault canal with troops and supplies for the Red River expedition, which shows Ottawa that the Union can cut the Dominion in two at the Soo. St Albans and the Confederate agents of Montreal are an account the Union means to settle, and Britain is taking its garrisons home. Montreal also earns from Richmond's tolls, which drive Midwest grain onto the St Lawrence canals. Mexico meets the plan in Grant's treaty to annex Santo Domingo, which the Senate rejects on 30 June 1870, and Juárez would rather have a weak Confederacy on the Rio Grande than a reunited giant. Pacifica coordinates nothing; it shows each capital the whole map. When Grant attacks, the Canadian militia still under arms after the May raids stays on the border and ties down Union troops, and Mexico closes its Gulf ports to Union coaling.

**[APPROVED — CR-058]** In the spring of 1871 two treaties are made. At Washington on 8 May Britain trades Canada's fisheries away for peace with the Union. In the same weeks Pacifica grants Canada, for nothing but the asking, the navigation of the Stikine, the Yukon and the Porcupine through Alaska, free forever. **[PROPOSED — CR-058]** The grant is a convention signed at Monterey with Britain, with a British Columbian delegate in the British party. **[APPROVED — CR-058]** British Columbia enters Confederation on 20 July. Pacifica has encouraged it, because a strong Canadian buffer is worth more to it than a province that would provoke Britain, and since 1866 it has let London know that a Union move on Rupert's Land or the coast would concern the Pacific. Its money goes quietly, through London houses and never into the charter, into the finance of the Canadian Pacific, whose western traffic and, from 1887, trans-Pacific steamers feed Pacifica's own shipping. The scandal of 1873 over the foreign money behind Allan's syndicate shows what visible money would cost. The river grant matters at once: the Cassiar rush of 1873–1874 goes up the Stikine through Wrangell.

The Union turns to an anti-slavery cold war. Greeley defeats Grant in 1872, then dies before the electors meet. <!-- CR-068: Lincoln's death -->**[APPROVED — CR-068]** Lincoln dies at Springfield in November 1872. Pacifica pays him no official honour. After a disputed count, Brown takes office in 1873 on condition that he sign the Freedom Act: refugees are free on arrival and citizens after a year; discriminatory duties and European campaigning penalize slave-grown cotton. The Panic of 1873 weakens cotton and slave prices while Pacifica recruits laid-off engineers and workers. A disputed Hayes election and the railway strike of 1877 deepen the Union's crisis without ending its anti-slavery policy.

Gordon succeeds Johnston in 1874 and works with Pacifican railways, coal and credit. Benjamin helps carry a free-womb amendment in 1874–1875 as refinancing pressure mounts. **[PROPOSED]** Benjamin first serves as Louisiana senator from 1868, then Gordon's Secretary of State from 1874. A convention establishes freedom for children born after 1 January 1875, while allowing masters their labor to twenty-one or state compensation. The constitutional route requires state consent; its economic effect and finance await audit. Black Union regulars and freedmen's homesteads along Tennessee sustain escape networks. Flight, free-labor markets and campaigning make the Confederacy poorer and more dependent on Pacifican credit.
