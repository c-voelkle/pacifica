### Findings

**1.1 BLOCKER — the forum and the day of the "year more" are not real. Small author decision, then mechanical.**
Canon §8: "renewal requires both member legislatures". Canon §12: "both member legislatures renew". CR-068's own heavy-beat table (l. 491) says of 1863 "The office lapses if Honolulu does not vote", and CT24's calendar has the renewal passed at Honolulu in the last week of May. G9a (l. 346) nevertheless says "most of the Council expects it, and Hawaii's delegation could hardly refuse it", and the APPROVED §12 text (l. 644) says "most of the Council expects Elias to ask for a year more". The Council and Hawaii's delegation at Monterey do not renew; Hawaii's legislature does, twelve days away.
Consequence for time: a request has to leave Monterey by about 18 May to be voted at Honolulu before the 31st, and Hawaii's legislature has to be sitting. The armistice is "mid-May". So the decision not to ask is made in the two or three days between the signature and the last steamer, not on the last day. B16a (l. 548) stages it as "The last day of May 1865: … everyone expects him to ask for a year more, and he lets the office expire". On that day nobody could expect it; the steamer left two weeks earlier. It also follows that if the armistice had slipped a week, there would have been "a day of war without a commander", which G9a says never happens: he must have let the Honolulu deadline go with the armistice still unsigned or just signed.
Fix: name the legislatures as the forum; move the moment to the sailing of the last steamer in mid-May (a good scene: the packet leaves without the message); say whether Hawaii's legislature was in session. The author should choose the day.

**1.2 SHOULD-FIX — Hawaii's ratification cannot be known at Monterey by 1 December. Mechanical.**
G9a (l. 357): "she carries the ratification about 20 November. The law is proclaimed in force on 1 December." Honolulu to Monterey is about twelve days (CT24's own calendar, l. 836–838): 20 November arrives about 2 December. Either the vote is about 15 November or the proclamation is in the first week of December. The wire to Washington "in the first days of December" and a signature in the third week still fit either way.

**1.3 SHOULD-FIX — the King's instruction on the term predates the proposal it answers. Mechanical, with a line for the author to approve.**
G9a (l. 354): Victoria "comes from Honolulu in June … with the King's instructions". But the life term is "his move in 1865" (G8.6, l. 328) and the heads are first laid in July (l. 353). Lot cannot have instructed for an office for life in an instruction that sailed in early June. The text needs a dated reference home: heads in early July, twelve days out, twelve back, the answer at Monterey in early August at the earliest, the vote late in August. That is feasible and is nowhere stated. It would also give the page what it lacks: Victoria writing to her brother against the term and being overruled by return steamer.

**1.4 SHOULD-FIX — how Hawaii's delegation votes is told two ways, and the count depends on it. Author decision.**
- November 1864 (G8.7–8, l. 329–330): the delegation "acts on the standing instructions of Hawaii's government" and still divides seven to two, "with the mission party the two".
- August 1865 (G9a, l. 356–357): "The King's instruction decides, and Hawaii's nine vote for the office"; "Hawaii's nine by instruction".
If instructions bind, the two of 1864 need a reason. If they do not, the mission party's two, who would not confirm a one-year Protector, are now voting for an office for life. The PROPOSED count (13–5) is fragile on exactly this: seven Hawaiian votes plus California's four is eleven, one short of two-thirds of eighteen.
- Victoria's seat: in 1864 she is at Honolulu and nine resident delegates vote. In 1865 she is "at the head of Hawaii's delegation" and "casts her vote with them". Is she one of the nine (who steps down?) or a tenth? Canon §8 has "equal member delegations" and "a resident Hawaiian delegation" and does not seat the Kuhina Nui.
- California's delegation votes four to five against the bill (in 1864 it was five to four for him), so the life office is carried by Hawaii's bloc on an absent king's instruction against the judgment of Hawaii's own co-ruler in the room. That may be intended; it should be said, because California's legislature then ratifies in October what most of its delegates refused in August.
- There is no vote on Sherman's ten-year term as such; the only division shown is on the whole bill.

**1.5 SHOULD-FIX — "the Council adopts" and "the chambers pass" may count the Council twice. Mechanical.**
Canon §12: "two-thirds of both confederal chambers". Canon §8 gives the Confederation a Council and an Assembly and no third body. G9a (l. 357): "The Council adopts the bill in August, thirteen to five … The confederal chambers pass it in September and October by two-thirds". If the Council is one of the two chambers, its August vote is its constitutional vote and only the Assembly remains (and one chamber does not need two months). The §12 text (l. 646) has the same ambiguity: "Hawaii's nine vote for the office. The chambers pass the law in September and October". Say which.

**1.6 SHOULD-FIX — the case for an office *for life* is still made in the room only by its holder. Author decision.**
The vote is now shown (13–5). The argument is not. "Why for life" (l. 355) is Elias's own two arguments. Lot's reasons (l. 356) are good and are his own, but Lot is at Honolulu and his voice in the room is an instruction carried by the delegate who opposes it. Marcus "speaks for a lawful and lasting executive" (l. 354, PROPOSED), which is canon's phrase and stops short of "for life"; B16a calls it "a lasting office". G9a itself says "The parties who asked who is bound did not ask for him" (l. 353). So no one present argues for life except the man who gets it. One of the creditors of the question (Young's man, Nesmith, a banker with seven years of instalments to collect, Richmond's envoy) or Marcus in terms would fix it. The §12 text states the case with no speaker ("the case for a life office is …").

**1.7 SHOULD-FIX — "asked of him" against "did not ask for him". Author decision (one sentence).**
G9a (l. 353): the parties "did not ask for him; he answers their question with himself". G10 (l. 366): "it was asked of him by the people to whom the Republic had given its word, Oregon's own compact among them". CR-059 V11 (l. 164) repeats it: "the office was asked of him by those the Republic had given its word to". If the printed answer of 1866 stretches the truth, the Chronicler, who "records that the answer concedes Lincoln's point", should also record the stretch; otherwise the two rows contradict.

**1.8 SHOULD-FIX — "pledged" against "expected to ask" is coherent in G9a and MD4 and not yet in G8 and CT17. Mechanical.**
G9a and MD4 (l. 164: "sooner than his pledge required") read the pledge as permitting a renewal until the peace and have him read it against himself. But G8.6 (l. 328) says: "'After the war' means what it says: the renewal of 1864 is the last", and CT17 (l. 781): "The renewal of 1864 is therefore the last". In November 1864 nobody at Monterey knows who has won the Union's election, and a re-elected Lincoln means war in May 1865 (the winter plan). The resolution cannot then have meant that 1864's renewal was the last. Those two sentences are left from the version before the author's "read against himself". Also X12 (l. 575) "on the condition that the Protectorship ends with the war" and Lincoln's "as he had promised" (G10) are fine as loose speech; the two above are narrator's statements.

**1.9 SHOULD-FIX — "Victoria's standing instructions" survives in six places. Mechanical; one point for the author.**
G8.6 now has "the standing instructions of Hawaii's government, which she drafted as Kuhina Nui and the King approved". Still personal:
- l. 433 (W1): "Hawaii's, instructed by his wife"
- l. 546 (B15): "Hawaii's delegation, on Victoria's standing instructions"
- l. 381 (G12) and l. 470 (TL6): "her standing instructions"
- l. 672 (character note): "by standing instructions to Hawaii's delegation"
- l. 946 (Open decisions, record of 11 November): "acts on Victoria's standing instructions in 1864 and in 1871–72"
- neighbouring: CR-061 C0a (l. 49) "Victoria's delegation votes for it"; CR-059 V1 (l. 154) "Victoria speaks through it".
For the author: the blind vote is called on the 9th for the 12th and Victoria is twelve days away. She cannot know the vote exists. G8.6 nevertheless gives the price situational reasons in her person ("a Protector confirmed by her delegation's votes owes her what no American treaty would"). Either the standing instructions already fix the price of the *next renewal* (May 1865) and the head of delegation applies it to a vote nobody foresaw, or the reasoning is the delegate's. Say which; at present she reasons about an event she has not heard of.

**1.10 NOTE — "reservations about its reach" is turned into something else. Mechanical.**
Canon: she retains "reservations about its reach" (the reach of cabinet legislation). G9a (l. 359): she "votes for the law without knowing which is true; that is her reservation about its reach". §12 text (l. 646): "retaining reservations about its reach: she has watched him lay the old office down, and does not know whether she saw a man keeping his word or winning his case." Doubt about his sincerity is not a reservation about reach. Her stated reservation in this draft is the life term and who is bound after it. The colon should lead to that.

**1.11 NOTE — full powers "sent by the wire" (CR-062 T1, l. 51). Author's call.**
In 1865 full powers were a sealed instrument examined and exchanged. A telegraphed full power from an office four days old, received over a line that crosses a gap ridden by couriers, is something Washington's Secretary of State would at least remark on. Easy ways out: the commissioners sign under the Council's commission and the Providentor's ratification follows; or sealed powers follow by steamer and the signature is "subject to exchange".

**1.12 NOTE — the six months' evidence is contradicted mildly by neighbouring drafts.**
G9a: "The interior has no lawful government" and the honest answer is "the compacts' own words and nothing behind them". CR-061 l. 120 treats New Mexico in December 1865–January 1866 as lawfully "held under the compact and the armistice until the exchange". CR-060 N6 has the Republic's commissioner conclude a new long agreement at Spanish Fork in the same months (and says so). An armistice convention is itself a title to hold until the treaty. The claim would be safer as "no law of the Confederation's own".

**1.13 NOTE — hindsight in the callers' mouths.** G9a (l. 351): the commissioners ask about "a treaty the Senate will not vote on before 1867"; "Until the accessions of 1867". In the summer of 1865 nobody knows either date.

**1.14 NOTE — the Kuhina Nui is out of the kingdom from June to late October.** Nothing says who acts for her at Honolulu. Canon has her kahuna die there in May 1865 and her illness there in February 1866; both fit.

**1.15 NOTE — CR-065 §12 text (l. 263): Stephens asks for an armistice "which McClellan will grant in a month".** From 3 February that is March; L6 (l. 107) and l. 223 say "within two months" and Richmond's convention is of April. Mechanical.

**1.16 NOTE — the Amity treaty's place and Richmond's signatory are not stated** (CR-062 A6; G9a). "His last act as Protector" requires him to sign or ratify at Monterey in the last week of May.

**1.17 NOTE — the blind vote's margin.** D4a closes the line west of Julesburg "to all but its own traffic", so the Union's military wire still runs toward South Pass. An operator's or trader's leak from the last military station could reach Fort Bridger a day or two sooner than Denver's riders. The 12th is still before the 15th; the text's "cannot" is nearer "is very unlikely to". Confidence: medium.

**Lot and Victoria.** Their positions are coherent with each other and with canon §12 (Lot "admires decisive government"; Victoria negotiates and keeps reservations). Victoria's stand in 1865 is the same sentence she is given in 1864 ("a guarantee written into the constitution does not depend on any one life"). Lot's reasoning from the end of parity is new and plausible. Its weak point, which the draft half-sees: the Enabling Law's danger to Hawaii is the cabinet's power, which is the guarantor's own, and not the new states' chambers. That is the irony of 1874 and can stay.

## 2. CR-064 A9–A13, Part V steps 11–16, the summary, the §11 text

**2.1 BLOCKER — McPherson both dies on 22 November 1864 and "lives through 1864". Mechanical.**
- C8 (l. 79): "**McPherson lives through 1864.** He historically died at the battle of 22 July 1864 (Hist.), which here is not fought. Nothing else is assumed".
- Registry table (l. 379): "JAMES-MCPHERSON … lives through 1864".
- A12 (l. 106), step 15 (l. 240), §11 text (l. 311), summary item 5 (l. 33): killed on 22 November 1864.
No other draft has him acting after that date (CR-063 uses him only in 1863).

**2.2 SHOULD-FIX — the third reason for the standstill contradicts A11–A12 and canon's own winter. Author decision (wording).**
A13 (l. 107): "after 8 November no officer orders an assault for a city the next President will not ask for, and Thomas says so in writing". But on 10 November Grant orders exactly that, with Lincoln's wish for "Atlanta before 4 March" as the motive (A11), and McPherson assaults on the 20th to 22nd. And canon §11 and CR-063 Part VI have Lincoln send Farragut and eight thousand men against Mobile in February and fight Hatcher's Run, for places the next President will not ask for either. The reason proves too much. It holds only as "after 22 November" and only as Thomas's and the army's temper, not as a rule of the service.

**2.3 SHOULD-FIX — why Grant leaves Thomas in place a second time. Author decision.**
Grant relieved Thomas on 10 November for not attacking. After the 22nd Thomas declines to attack and "says so in writing", on a political ground (what the next President will want). The Lincoln and Stanton of A11 would relieve a general for that sentence, and history's Grant sent Logan on 13 December 1864 after suspending the order of the 9th (high confidence). "Grant does not replace him again" (A12) needs its reason on the page: no general he trusts is left (Schofield? Logan? Howard?); the shipping and the fresh troops are promised to Mobile in December (CR-063); and the army has just buried the man Grant sent. As written, only the last is implied.

**2.4 SHOULD-FIX — A11 does not answer C7. Mechanical or one author sentence.**
C7 (l. 78) says that in March 1864 Lincoln and Stanton wanted Thomas, and that Grant judged "the Army of the Cumberland, three-fifths of the force, will not serve under a thirty-five-year-old engineer over its own commander". On 10 November Grant puts that engineer over that army with its commander still at its head, and the row does not mention the earlier judgment or how the Cumberland's corps behave on the 20th to 22nd (they are not in the battle at all: only the Army of the Tennessee moves). That silence could be made the point: McPherson attacks with his own army alone because he cannot move Thomas's.
Also unstated: who commands the Army of the Tennessee after McPherson rises (he "rides between two of his corps" as its commander still).

**2.5 SHOULD-FIX — the neighbouring drafts do not know the battle. Mechanical.**
- CR-065 summary item 5 (l. 34), L1–L8 (l. 102–109), Part text (l. 220–221) and §12 text (l. 263) list Lincoln's last efforts as Petersburg, Mobile, the amendment and Hampton Roads. A six-thousand-casualty battle and a dead army-group commander twelve days after the vote belongs there, and it is ordered on the day of the serenade (L2, 10 November) and the day before the memorandum is opened (L1). CR-065 l. 369 still lists "why the western armies stay still from November to March" as not done.
- CR-063 Part VI (l. 235): in December "the western army is stalled before Atlanta, with … Johnston on the Chattahoochee". Since September Johnston is south of the river (A9, A10), and the December decision for Mobile is taken a fortnight after McPherson's repulse, which is its strongest argument and is not mentioned.
- CR-068 B16 (l. 547) gives Lincoln's 10 November as "one evening: the serenade". B14 (l. 545) and G8 (l. 312) are consistent ("Thomas", September).
- Canon §11's "paired failures at Mobile and Petersburg close Lincoln's last winter offensive" becomes three failures; CR-064 has no canon tension for it.

**2.6 NOTE — stale counts and one muddled sentence. Mechanical.**
- Heading l. 91 "The Atlanta campaign stalls, May–November 1864", heading l. 207 "(A1–A10)", summary item 5 "(A1–A10; …)", CT2 "(A1–A10)": the rows now run to A13 and to March 1865.
- §11 text (l. 311): "On 22 November he attacks from Decatur toward the Macon road; Hardee marches round his flank in the night, the attack is stopped". A12 has the advance on the 20th–21st, the night march on the 21st–22nd and the blow on the 22nd. In A12 "the attack is held" is Hardee's; in the §11 text "the attack is stopped" reads as McPherson's.
- "from Decatur against the city's east front, toward the Macon road": the Macon and Western leaves Atlanta to the south; an advance from Decatur reaches it only by passing south of the city. "Toward the Macon road" is a direction, not an objective within reach in two days.

**2.7 NOTE — supply as a reason.** Johnsonville fed Nashville by one of three routes (the Louisville railroad and the Cumberland were the others); in history its loss on 4–5 November 1864 did not stop Thomas or Sherman. It also precedes McPherson's attack and did not stop that. Polk's second raid in December is the stronger reason and is MODEL. "Short rations behind 140 miles of single track" is fair for Chattanooga–Atlanta (138 miles).

**2.8 NOTE — the XIII Corps (A1, PROPOSED, drafter).** Thirteen thousand men who in history went to Banks are "counted inside the 99,000, in place of the garrisons Thomas leaves on a longer line". Thomas's line is not longer than Sherman's (he never passes Atlanta), and A6 says his rear garrisons equal Sherman's. As written the corps is absorbed by assertion. Either the total rises (and the stall has to carry it) or the offset is named.

**Would Grant and Lincoln act so?** The order of 10 November: yes. It matches Grant before Nashville and Lincoln's memorandum pledge "to save the Union between the election and the inauguration". The three months of acquiescence afterwards: not yet (2.2, 2.3).

**Thomas continuously in command elsewhere?** CR-065 l. 16 ("the stalled Atlanta campaign under Thomas"), l. 33, 85, 193, 261, 379 all concern September–October and are right. No passage has Thomas commanding continuously through November in so many words; CR-065 and CR-063 simply stop before the change.

**Historical anchors (from memory).**
- Hardee's night flank march and the battle of 22 July 1864; McPherson shot riding through woods into Cleburne's skirmishers between the XVI and XVII Corps: high confidence. Historical losses were roughly 3,700 Union and 5,500 or more Confederate (medium); A12's MODEL reverses the proportion and should say why (McPherson's own repulsed advance).
- Forrest at Johnsonville, 4–5 November 1864: high.
- Grant's suspended order of 9 December 1864 (naming Schofield) and Logan sent on the 13th: high.
- Western and Atlantic 138 miles; Dalton about 100 and Allatoona about 40 from Atlanta: high. Decatur six miles east of Atlanta: high. Blue Mountain to Kingston about 65–70 miles, three to four days' march: medium.
- McPherson senior to Thomas as regular brigadier (1 August against 27 October 1863): high. Thomas senior as major general of volunteers (April against October 1862): high, so "returns to Thomas as senior officer on the field" is right.
- Kilpatrick's raid of 18–22 August 1864, the road mended in days: high.

## 3. CR-059 I2, I3, W13, W14, W15, §10 text, notes at l. 620–625 and 985–990

The order of October 1861 as now written (I2 l. 139, W14 l. 132, §10 text l. 598) is consistent with a state of war (W15) and with regiments sent to the Sound: it withdraws only posts east of the Cascades that can no longer be supplied, and Casey is reinforced. Casey commands at Port Townsend throughout; Wright commands the Columbia and the interior until he marches east in July 1862 (I3; CR-067 l. 42 agrees).

**3.1 SHOULD-FIX — four passages still describe the general recall. Mechanical.**
- CR-060 l. 25: "the October 1861 recall takes the coast's regulars east (CR-059 I2)".
- CR-060 U2 (l. 46): Fort Churchill's "regulars obey the October 1861 recall (CR-059 I2)". I2's order now names Walla Walla, Colville and The Dalles. Fort Churchill is not east of the Cascades and not cut off from the sea; its regulars need an order of their own or a different reason. CR-060's §10 text (l. 265) avoids the claim; U2 does not.
- CR-060 l. 562 (self-review): "The October 1861 recall that drives CR-059 would otherwise empty the fort".
- CR-059 CT3 (l. 622): "Every regular was ordered east in October 1861." The paragraph means the historical record; the sentence reads as this timeline's fact. Add "historically".

**3.2 SHOULD-FIX — two dated notes now misdescribe the rows. Mechanical.**
- CR-059 l. 987 (repair pass, 8 November): "W14 and I2 state that Casey's command is the War Department's one exception to the recall of 1 October 1861 … the §10 text says so in a clause." Neither row nor the text says that now. Mark it superseded.
- CR-059 l. 774: "Not done, because they need the author: the recall order of 1 October 1861 against the state of war (I2, W15) … Wright at Walla Walla (I3)". Both are done.

**3.3 SHOULD-FIX — Wright after July 1862. Mechanical, plus an open item.**
CR-064 O1 (l. 123): "Casey stays on the Sound (CR-059 W14) and Wright on the Columbia, so neither is in this order of battle". Wright leaves the Columbia in July 1862 and reaches St Louis that autumn. From then he is a Union brigadier with no stated post in any draft. One line is enough; it should exist, since CR-064's claim is that no misplaced officer is in its order of battle.

**3.4 NOTE — the order of October cannot be obeyed before late June.** The text says so. It follows that for eight months Washington has told its interior force to leave while it plans the lower-Columbia attempt of spring–summer 1862, for which that force is the other jaw (canon: the interior concentration gives "later reinforcements somewhere to arrive"). The draft's sequence (attempt fails, then the convention lets them obey) hides the tension; a clause that the order was suspended for the attempt would remove it. Author's call.

**3.5 NOTE — Fort Benton in late August.** I3 has the column (strength not given) go down the Missouri in mackinaw boats after the steamers' season. A mackinaw carries a dozen or two; several hundred regulars need dozens of boats built at a trading post. MODEL; worth a number.

**3.6 NOTE — who commands the whole before July 1862.** Casey's volunteer commission (31 August 1861) predates Wright's (28 September 1861; medium confidence), so "Wright commands the Columbia and the interior, Casey the Sound" needs the War Department's assignment to say who is senior. W14 implies two separate commands and can stay so if stated.

**3.7 NOTE — the Official Records quotations.** They are used as evidence of conditions and labelled Hist., which the timeline can bear: the report of 28 January 1862 (the valley's emptiness), "There are no recruits here to send you" (2 June 1862), Halleck's refusal of coin (reassigned to Casey), the 68 per cent (reassigned to Port Townsend's paymaster). One phrase strains: "what Wright called a 'ruinous discount'" (I2), a remark of 1863 from San Francisco by a man who in this timeline is by then in the East. Drop the attribution or reassign it as the other two are. Page references (OR I.644, I.790, I.829–830, I.1117, II.303, II.310, II.356) I cannot confirm from memory; the events and dates are plausible (low to medium confidence on pages).

**3.8 NOTE — Mullan.** Cantonment Wright in the winter of 1861–62, Fort Benton in 1862, the road's 624 miles, his resignation in April 1863: consistent with what I know (medium to high).

## 4. CR-060 D5, D7, N1, N6, CT7, open decision 6, the ledger, the §10 Black Hawk paragraph; D4a, D8, D9, C5, C6

**Protected intent.** Kept. N6's orders (guard settlements and herds, no pursuit into the canyons without a confederal officer, no reprisal on bands at peace), the court for later raiders and "no Circleville" are the opposite of a campaign of extermination, and the Legion fights only in defence and under countersigned orders, which squares N1's "no volunteer companies" as D5 argues (a lawful militia named in a compact). The Legion's pay is out of the §10 text, as CT7 requires.

**The Legion's position** is stated the same way in D5, D7, CT7, the ledger (C-1862-DESERET), the §10 text (l. 265), the §12 text (l. 291, Defense Law at accession), CR-067 l. 73 and CR-068 l. 843: bound to common defence under its own officers; Defense Law only at accession; pay open and not integrated.

**4.1 SHOULD-FIX — what happens to the killings of spring 1865 is not said, and it decides whether Black Hawk comes in. Author decision.**
N6 (l. 135): "killings on either side go to the confederal court with Ute testimony (N1)". If that reaches the settlers killed in April and May 1865, Black Hawk and his men are invited under safe conduct to an agreement that sends them to trial, on the model of Box Elder, where the bands surrendered the killers "of any date" (D9) and the same three words hanged Lee. He would not come. If the agreement is an amnesty for acts of war before the council and the court takes killings after it, that is a departure from N1 and from Box Elder's rule and has to be stated as one, with its price: Deseret's southern settlers see the man who killed their neighbours ride their streets with the commissioner, keep the cattle (set against the first issue) and receive a yearly issue that Deseret partly pays. The §10 text (l. 275) carries the same ambiguity.

**4.2 SHOULD-FIX — plausibility that he comes in during his first, most successful season. Author's judgment; my assessment.**
Historically (high confidence) Black Hawk stayed away from the Spanish Fork council of June 1865, whose treaty asked removal to the Uintah and was never ratified; he made peace in 1867, wounded and ill, and raiding ran on to 1872. He died in September 1870 (high). The draft changes the three things that could bring him in: no removal, a food issue that answers the hunger, and Kanosh and Sowiette as sponsors. Against it: his following was built on the raids' success, much of it was Elk Mountain Ute, Paiute and Navajo and not his to bind, and the stock had a market. "The peace holds" is believable as the draft limits it (a season, dead in tens, other bands still raiding and answered by a court). It needs two things it lacks: who serves the court's warrants in the canyons if not the Legion (Hays's regulars are at Camp Floyd, eighty miles away), and a reason for Black Hawk personally (the historical one is illness; the draft could use the smallpox winter and his family).

**4.3 NOTE — first days of the war against the orders.** The Manti quarrel is 9 April; history's first Legion pursuit into Salina Canyon was three days later. Countersigned orders from the commissioner cannot be in Sanpete by then. "In the weeks that follow" covers it; the text should not imply the orders governed April.

**4.4 NOTE — wording. Mechanical.**
- "the common defence of the roads and the valley" (D5, D7, §10) against a muster in Sanpete and on the Sevier, which are neither. D5 elsewhere says "settlements and roads". Use one.
- N6: "no pursuit into the canyons without a confederal officer"; §10 text: "its orders forbid it to carry the war into the canyons". The second is absolute.

**4.5 NOTE — the agreement is made in the gap.** N6 says so. It is evidence against G9a's claim that the six months show a government unable to bind itself (see 1.12): the commissioner binds the Republic to a yearly issue with no Protector and no Enabling Law.

**The telegraph.** CR-060 D4a (l. 59), C5 (l. 77), the §10 text (l. 270) and CR-068 G8 (l. 322) agree: Union traffic through Salt Lake until April 1864; the line west of Julesburg closed to all but the War Department's traffic when Washoe is retaken; the Union's last public office at Denver; the Republic's wire from Fort Bridger; riders between in four or five days. The weekdays are right (8 November 1864 was a Tuesday, the 12th a Saturday, the 17th a Thursday; high confidence). Denver to Fort Bridger is about 450 miles; four or five days by relays is fair. See 1.17 for the military wire.

**D8, D9, C5, C6.** Consistent with each other and with CR-068 (the Compact six days before Box Elder; the mandate of September 1863; the three conditions granted and one refused in May 1864). No finding.

## 5. CR-061 C0a and the §10 text; CR-067 A1

**The resolution covers the campaign fought.** "The southern road" covers Tucson (C5) and the passage of Apache Pass (C6); "the valley of the Rio Grande, from Paso del Norte to its head" covers Mesilla, Fort Craig, Albuquerque, Santa Fe, Taos and, as the row says, the San Luis Valley and Fort Garland (F8). The front stops at Glorieta and the ranges (F9), and Fort Union stays the Union's until the exchange (P1), so nothing fought lies outside the words. "With the forces of Richmond the commander is to treat and not to fight" sits correctly with C5 (Tucson evacuated, no orders to fight) and makes C7 a breach by a subordinate, which is what canon's "a brief clash both governments prefer to forget" needs. CR-067 A1 cites it accurately.

**Timing with Glorieta.** Sound. Glorieta is 26–28 March (high confidence); the Texans leave Santa Fe about 8 April (high). A vote "in the first days of April" is taken while Sibley holds the capital and before the news can reach Monterey (by Denver and the wire, a fortnight or more). The column leaves on the 13th.

**5.1 NOTE — the Apache war is not within "against the armies of the United States".** C6: "Pacifica's Apache war begins on Pacifica's own road" and runs to 1865. Under CR-067 A1 "a change of war aim needs a new resolution". "To open … the southern road" can carry the fight for the springs; three years of war with the Chiricahua is arguably a new enemy. Sherman and Victoria made that complaint about the Salt Lake road. Author's call whether anyone makes it here.

**5.2 NOTE — "hold" a valley whose southern end a partner occupies.** In April 1862 Mesilla is the capital of Confederate Arizona and Richmond's claim stands until February 1863 (C8). The resolution orders the commander to hold it and not to fight for it. The row names the "unspoken reason"; the contradiction in the instrument is the cause of Mesilla and could be said in the §10 text in a clause.

**5.3 NOTE — mechanical.**
- "Victoria's delegation votes for it" (C0a): see 1.9.
- "Why the column marches when it does" (l. 165–172) gives three reasons for April and does not mention the vote; C1 says "Elias waits for the season". The column is staged at Fort Yuma before the vote (on Californian soil, so no breach).
- C0a: "leaves southern California on the 13th"; §10 text: "leaves Fort Yuma". Same event; one wording.
- l. 172 quotes Boatner for "Santa Fe taken 23 March"; the usual date for the Confederate entry is 10–13 March (medium confidence). It does not affect the row.
- CR-061 C12 (l. 61): "the cabinet governs for nearly six months without him"; CR-067 A1 (PROPOSED, drafter): "the Council's senior member presides at the capital, Sherman answers for the army … orders that cannot wait go out over both names". Two descriptions of the same absence; and before the Enabling Law there is a Chancellor and a Council, with no war minister (CR-067 A4), so "cabinet" needs care.
