**Findings**

- **F1.0 = B2. BLOCKER, author.** Who conceded the clause, under what authority, and why Elias was not party to it: no text answers this. CR-068 says only "the ministers" (l. 3, 378, 796). The candidate (363) does not say even that: the reader meets "the new treaties' rendition clause" with no one having agreed to it; Cand. 351 ("Union attacks fail at New Orleans"), which lists the association treaties' contents, does not mention it. Against it stand three statements: canon/Cand. 289 "Elias controls ministerial appointment and dismissal, sets the cabinet's legislative agenda"; Cand. 295, where a treaty is signed "under the full powers of the office"; and CR-061 S4, where the Californios make non-rendition "the one thing Elias may not trade". CR-068 l. 382 calls rendition Richmond's "price" for the treaties, so it was the headline term, not a clause that slipped through. Either Elias knew and let his ministers sign (then the refusal in the autumn is a reversal, and the round reads as the duplicity the author objected to on 10 November, CR-068 l. 16), or he did not know (hard to credit of this office). The author must choose and one sentence must go into §13: who signed, on whose instruction, and what Elias knew. CR-061 S4 and CT9 must then be brought into line (F1.2).
- **F1.1. SHOULD-FIX, author.** CR-061 S4 (l. 151): "the rule of the Mexican constitution of 1857 (Hist.; to verify), under which the Californios founded the Republic". The constitution of 1857 is ten years younger than the Republic (founded 1846–48); the Californios cannot have founded anything under it. The storyline's own wording ("the Mexican law the Republic was founded on") is vague enough to be safe; the decision row is not. Replace with pre-1846 law. See "Mexican law" below.
- **F1.2. SHOULD-FIX, mechanical once B2 is decided.** CR-061 S4 ("Elias pays in money instead") and CT9 l. 555 ("Elias refuses and the ransom article pays") skip the ministers' concession: read alone they say Richmond's demand of 1871 was refused outright. CR-068 says it was conceded and then not applied. The two drafts must tell one sequence.
- **F1.3. SHOULD-FIX, mechanical.** Cand. 363 / CR-068 l. 661, G12 l. 382, CT18 l. 796: "Over the winter of 1871–1872 the cabinet *sets* travel-paper controls that refuse entry at Texas, the railway and Gulf ports". The Texas line has been closed "on travel papers" since February 1863 (CR-061 S4), and CR-061 CT9's knock-on (l. 556) says the controls "date from 1863 on the Texas line and are extended to the railway and the Gulf ports in 1871–72". G12 contradicts itself: l. 378 has the petitioner get "through the controls at the Texas line" before l. 382 has the cabinet set them. Change "sets … at Texas, the railway and Gulf ports" to "extends the controls of the Texas line to the railway and the Gulf ports".
- **F1.4. SHOULD-FIX, mechanical (O5 and S2: author).** Stale statements of the old form:

X16 (l. 580), TL6 (l. 471, 482, 484) and the chain paragraph (l. 582) say "the continental controls of 1871–72 are the cabinet's": true only of the extension.
- **F1.5. SHOULD-FIX, mechanical.** "A ransom article *replaces* the rendition clause" (Cand. 363; CR-068 l. 661) against "the fugitive agreement *gains* an article" (G12 l. 381) and "adds"/"is added" (CT18 l. 796–797). - **F1.6. SHOULD-FIX, mechanical.** Lincoln's opinion. The storyline (Cand. 363) and PP14 rest it on three grounds: the prohibition, the San Antonio article of 1863, the terms of passage under the Peoria sentence. G12 "Lincoln's move" (l. 380) and CT18 "Amended" (l. 796) still give two (no article of 1863).
- **F1.7. SHOULD-FIX, author.** Does the opinion make sense on the new footing? Mostly. The prohibition is the right ground: a treaty clause signed by ministers cannot repeal the Republic's own law without the legislative act the ministers then prepare, which is exactly why an "override under the enabling law" is needed after the writ. But the article of 1863 is an article of an agreement with the same party, and the treaty of 1871 is later; a lawyer of Lincoln's quality would not plead an earlier treaty article against a later one. It can serve only as evidence that the rule is the Republic's standing law and that Richmond acknowledged it for eight years. The text should say "as evidence of" or drop the article as a ground. Third ground: see F4.2 (the Peoria sentence is no longer told where the terms of passage are told).
- **F1.8. NOTE, author.** Cand. 363 "Elias refuses the override his ministers have prepared under the enabling law, to which Sherman had objected": ambiguous whether Sherman objected to the override or to the law (he did both; Cand. 283).

The text holds only if the petitioner is the first person ever claimed; G12 says "the first such clause", not that he is the first claimed. Worth one word ("the first person claimed under it").
- **F1.10. NOTE, author.** "Refused at the crossings" and "one who reaches Pacifican soil is free": a man standing at a Pacifican post is on Pacifican soil. The article works only if the refusal is made at the line (ford, bridge, ferry), which CR-061 l. 442 implies ("turned back at the ford"). The storyline sentence could carry "at the fords".

**"Mexican law": consistency and plausibility**

- *Consistency:* the premise appears in CR-061 S4, CT9 and the San Antonio sentence, and nowhere in CR-068. In 1871 neither Lincoln's brief nor Elias's answer invokes it: the brief pleads "Pacifica's own prohibition", and Elias answers with "a rule older than either" (hospitality, G12 l. 381). The rule that S4 calls "the one thing Elias may not trade" is thus traded by his ministers in 1871 with no Californio heard. SHOULD-FIX, author (part of B2).
- *Plausibility:* sound in substance, wrong in the citation. History.com's account of the southern Underground Railroad dates Mexican abolition to 1829, when Texas was still Mexican, and describes Mexico as insisting that anyone enslaved became free on setting foot on its soil. Wikipedia's article on the constitution of 1857 puts that constitution's promulgation in February 1857 and traces its abolition article back to Guerrero's decree of 15 September 1829. So the tradition predates the Republic, and the 1857 text does not. From memory, unverified: the federal decree of 13 July 1824 (slaves brought in are free on touching the soil), the law of 5 April 1837, the Bases Orgánicas of 1843 (art. 9) and Mexico's refusal of a rendition article in treaty talks with the United States in the 1820s–30s are the pre-1846 anchors; the express bar on extradition treaties for slaves is art. 15 of 1857. **Confidence:** high that "free on Mexican soil, not given up" was Mexican law and practice before 1846; medium that a pre-1846 text states non-rendition in terms (it was policy and treaty practice more than statute); high that citing 1857 is an anachronism here.

**Verdict, point 1: not sound.** The storyline sentences agree with each other, but the 1871 sequence has no stated authority (B2), and five rows in three drafts still tell the old form.
