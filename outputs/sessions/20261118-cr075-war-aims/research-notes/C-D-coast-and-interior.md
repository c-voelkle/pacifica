# Pass 2 notes — C1, D2, D3: the Union on the coast and in the interior, 1861–62

## Hunt, *The Army of the Pacific*

### The department in 1861 (pp. 20–37)
- Spring 1861: Blair asked for men and guns to guard the Panama mail-and-gold steamers; Scott ordered the department to act with the Pacific squadron to keep secessionists out of Lower California; three warships patrolled the coasts of California and Mexico; 30,000 stands of arms went east from Benicia; any Confederate-flagged vessel in Pacific waters was to be taken, and sunk if it fled — HUNT 20–21. → C3: the Union's Pacific posture in 1861 was a gold-convoy and anti-privateer patrol. In Pacifica that patrol needs a base and stops ships on suspicion — the mechanism behind canon's packet boarding.
- Johnston resigned on 9 April; Sumner took over on 25 April 1861 — HUNT 21. Sumner was first ordered (August) to invade western Texas by sea through Mazatlán or Guaymas; **Beale told him the route was impracticable**, and the plan was dropped — HUNT 21–22. → P10, D-c: Beale's knowledge of the southwestern routes in our world told the Union's general what could not be done. In Pacifica it is the Republic's.
- The regulars were withdrawn east from September; the last left on 21 December 1861; the 9th Infantry and four artillery companies stayed at San Juan Island, Fort Vancouver and San Francisco — HUNT 26. → D2: in our world the Union stripped the coast of regulars within six months of Sumter. In Pacifica the Northwest garrisons of mid-1861 are regulars on the point of being recalled: thin, and few — what makes Marcus's July–September calendar credible.
- The coast was alarmed: the allied flotilla for Veracruz (late 1861), joint occupation of San Juan Island, Spain on the South American coast; **San Francisco harbour was the only fortified point on the whole Pacific coast** — HUNT 22–23.
- California's 1860 census population 379,994, of whom 169,975 of military age; California gave about 16,231 men without a draft; the Pacific department ran from the ocean to the Rockies — HUNT 24–27. Commanders: Johnston (Jan–Apr 1861), Sumner (Apr–Oct 1861), Wright (Oct 1861–Jul 1864), McDowell — HUNT 27–28. Wright refused volunteers who asked to go east: "needed far more here than in the East" — HUNT 32.

### The Pacific squadron (pp. 301–316)
- **1861: six sloops** (*Lancaster*, *Saranac*, *Wyoming*, *Narragansett*, *St. Marys*, *Cyane*), under 7,000 tons together, about 100 guns, under 1,000 men; half of them twelve to twenty-four years old; cruising from Panama to Alaska and Chile, **visiting Hawaii to guard the whaling fleet**, and to China and Japan — HUNT 301–302. → C1, C3, Hawaii's reason 2: "the squadron lives on this coast" can be specific: six wooden sloops whose stations, once California's ports are closed, are the Columbia, the Sound and Panama, and whose cruise includes Hawaiian waters.
- Mare Island and Benicia were stripped by shipments east; Sumner lent guns to the Pacific Mail steamers — HUNT 302. Montgomery sent three sloops to escort the mail and gold steamers along Mexico and California — HUNT 303.
- 1862: the *Wyoming* went to the East Indies; the concentration in the south **left the Columbia and the northern coast exposed**; the revenue cutter *Shubrick* seized the Port Townsend custom house from its own acting collector at gunpoint (August 1862) — HUNT 303–304. **"There was not one fortified point on the Columbia river"** in 1862; Alvord asked for an ironclad — HUNT 304.
- 1863: the *Saginaw*, engines by the Union Iron Works; the *Chapman* privateer plot foiled by the *Cyane* — HUNT 305–310. The monitor *Camanche*, built in New York, sent disassembled in the *Aquila*, sank at the wharf, raised; Donahue reassembled her; launched 14 November 1864 — HUNT 311–313. 1864: Hogg's plot to seize the *San Salvador* from Panama — HUNT 314–315. Beale's part in the Mexican republican arms affair (1864) — HUNT 315–316.
- The Russian squadron at San Francisco, autumn 1863 — HUNT 310. Shimonoseki: the *Wyoming* fought the Chōshū batteries, July 1863 — HUNT 310–311. → Canon checks: Japan in Pacifica (CR-0xx on the Japanese treaty) — whether Shimonoseki happens is a separate question.

### The Northwest (pp. 220–236)
- California volunteers reached Fort Vancouver on 21 October 1861 to relieve the regulars; by the end of 1861 three companies in Oregon and seven in Washington; posts: Colville, Walla Walla, The Dalles, Vancouver, Hoskins, Yamhill, Steilacoom — HUNT 220–226.
- **Recruiting in Oregon and Washington was nearly impossible**: private firms offered $40–50 a month in coin beside the army's notices; miners earned $5 a day; eight of the ten companies of the 1st Washington Infantry were raised in California — HUNT 227–228. → D3, reason 5: the Northwest's economy ran on Californian coin and wages; its men would not enlist for a distant government. Support for E3/D3 (the interior follows its supplies and its money).
- **No fortifications at all in Oregon or Washington at the start of the war**; every federal dollar had gone to San Francisco Bay; Fort Cape Disappointment begun only July 1863 — HUNT 234. → C1: the Columbia and the Sound in 1861 were stations without forts. Canon's July 1861 offensive meets anchorage and garrisons, not fortresses.
- **The Nez Perce:** 10,000–15,000 miners had entered their reservation in breach of the 1855 treaty; after seven years little of the treaty had been kept; Lawyer had received six months of his salary; few annuities reached the tribe — HUNT 232–233. → D3, P10 and CR-071's Nez Perce homeland (the 1855 line kept, Lewiston as leased ground): the Union's broken treaty is a fact the Republic's envoys to the nations can use honestly. Fits canon's homeland decision.
