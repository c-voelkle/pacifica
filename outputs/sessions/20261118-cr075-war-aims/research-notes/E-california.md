# Pass 2 notes — Part E: California's reasons (and C4, D4)

Format: claim (paraphrase) — source, page — what it means for Pacifica. Quotes under fifteen words, only where the wording matters. "Hist." = our world; Pacifica's California is independent, so each American grievance has to be translated into what an independent California in 1861 would fear or want.

## Ellison, *California and the Nation*, ch. IX–XI (pp. 178–230)

### The Pacific Republic idea (ch. IX)
- E1/E4. The idea was old; its friends grew loud "whenever California felt she had a grievance" against Washington; isolation bred a sense of a destiny of its own; the native Californians had little attachment to the Union — ELL 178. → In Pacifica the destiny exists; the grievance is the convention of 1848 and Washington's view of California as fruit to be picked (canon §4).
- Southern-born men (estimated, probably too high, at 30%) dominated state politics 1850–60 and wanted independence if war came, so as not to fight their own kin — ELL 179. → E4: the Southern men's motive in Pacifica is the same, and C5 ("no army east") is what lets them support the Republic's war.
- Governor Weller's last message (1860): California would join neither side but found "a mighty republic" on the Pacific — ELL 180. Gwin and Latham told the Senate the eastern boundary of such a republic would be the Sierra Madre and the Rocky Mountains — ELL 180. → **The Rockies line was a Californian idea in our world.** In Pacifica the same men (or their analogues) are the domestic party that wants C4 read broadly: an expansionist pressure Elias has to hold back, not invent.
- Congressman Burch's letter of 22 November 1860 proposed a "Republic of the Pacific" of California, Oregon, New Mexico, Washington and Utah — ELL 181. → This is very nearly Pacifica's outcome of 1867. In Pacifica, a Burch-type voice in 1861 asks for it outright; Elias's aims refuse to name it (C4: only by their own choice). Strong material for E4 and for showing Elias's restraint as a choice.
- Latham warned that the failure to build a Pacific railroad might cost the Union California's affection — ELL 181.
- C. L. Scott (21 December 1860): tied to the North, California would be taxed to pay for a war, perhaps through a seignorage on the miners; standing alone, North and South would compete to connect with her by rail; 100 million acres of public domain; position for the trade of China, Japan and the East Indies — ELL 182. → E3/E6 arguments an independent California would make for staying out of the Union's orbit: no American tax on its gold, both sections courting it.
- Alameda Gazette, January 1861: about 40,000 Southern-born and 40,000 Northern-born men in the state; neither would take up arms against his native section — ELL 183. → **E4's core fact.** In Pacifica the Republic can only fight a war that sends neither half east. C5 is California's condition before it is Elias's rule.
- Sonora Democrat: California as "an asylum of peace" that many thousands would flock to; refuse taxes for coercion — ELL 183. → Matches canon's westward refuge from the draft (CR-068 G5).
- Opponents: trade, people and ships all came from the North; 602,000 people could not defend 1,500 miles of coast; a navy and fortifications would cost millions; taxing the Mormons and northern Oregon would cost more than it raised; California would fall prey to Louis Napoleon — ELL 184. → These are the objections Pacifica's institutions have already answered by 1861 (the navy, the Defense Law of 1855, the turret ships), and the ones its Council would still raise against C4 (the interior's cost).
- Volney Howard: treasure passing through Southern waters would be liable to capture; neither section could build a railroad; the tariff and navigation acts forced California's commerce to the Atlantic; California paid gold for goods priced in Eastern paper; it could raise $8–9 million a year in duties, enough for a government — ELL 184–185. → D4 and E3: Pacifica already ships gold direct to Europe. The danger to treasure at sea is C3.
- The Assembly minority's resolution (February 1861): California would not pledge itself to "either a northern or a southern fragment", nor be "the ally of one section" against another — ELL 185. → Wording close to C5. A minority view in our world; in Pacifica it is the consensus that makes the war possible.
- The loyal side's argument: the federal government lost $931,000 a year on California's mails and spent $2 million on its defence — ELL 186. → In Pacifica California pays for its own defence; the Union's money was never a tie.
- Sumter turned opinion; the legislature pledged loyalty 24 to 5 in the Senate; Stanford's election in September 1861 ended the movement — ELL 186–188.

### Loyalty and disloyalty (ch. X)
- September 1861: Union parties 86,980, Breckinridge 32,751: over a third against the war; strongest in the southern counties — ELL 190–191. → E4, E7: about a third of the electorate in any California-like state would oppose a war on the South's side and another part a war on the North's. A war against the Union that does not help the South invade anything can hold both.
- Disloyal press, arrests at Alcatraz, the *J. M. Chapman* privateer plot (March 1863: a schooner fitted out at San Francisco with Confederate letters of marque to take the Pacific mail steamer; the plotters' sketch: raise a thousand men, take Benicia and the harbour forts) — ELL 197–199. → C3 and Hawaii: Confederate privateering from Pacific ports was a real fear; in Pacifica it is the Union's reason to search Hawaiian packets (canon's boarding), and the Republic's reason to want no privateering from its ports either.
- Knights of the Golden Circle and similar orders: claims of 16,000 to 50,000 members, probably exaggerated — ELL 201–202. Sumner believed the plan was a Pacific republic first, the Confederacy after — ELL 201. Wright thought the rumours "highly exaggerated" — ELL 201.
- San Francisco merchants petitioned (28 August 1861) against sending California's men east: they were needed at home — ELL 201, 205. The draft was never extended to California; over 16,000 volunteers out of about 400,000 people — ELL 204.

### The coin (ch. XI)
- California's constitution forbade paper money; specie was the only currency, and Californians credited it with their prosperity — ELL 209–210. Chief Justice Field held (Perry v. Washburn) that legal tender did not apply to state taxes — ELL 211. The state treasurer's conversion of the direct-tax gold into notes was condemned as petty speculation — ELL 212–214.
- **The merchants' agreement of November 1862:** San Francisco's leading merchants agreed by circular to take notes only at market value against gold, with a "black book" for anyone who refused — ELL 215. Then the Specific Contract Act (1863) — ELL 216–217. → **The historical analogue of canon's Steamer Day Agreement (CR-069): a private compact of the houses, kept by publicity, in place of a law.** It supports Ralston's method over Elias's decree (D4, P5) as realistic.
- Wage-earners refused notes; half a firm's men would not work unless paid in gold — ELL 214. → The coin's popularity reaches below the merchants: D4 has a popular base.

## Waite, *West of Slavery* (pp. 150–207, the secession winter in the Far West)
- Every member of California's congressional delegation supported a western separation at one point or another in 1860–61 — WAITE 166, 189. → E1: in our world the idea of a Pacific state had the whole political class; in Pacifica it is the state.
- The separatists knew their republic depended on the Confederacy's success; Gwin stayed on the sidelines for that reason — WAITE 166, 169. → **C5 and reason 7 have a historical basis**: the Pacific separatists' own logic was that the West's independence lived or died with the South's. In Pacifica that logic belongs to California's Southern men before Elias states it.
- Hamilton's *Los Angeles Star*, January 1861: Los Angeles lies on the road to the South, its people are from the South; why join the South's enemies "to invade, impoverish, and despoil them?" — WAITE 188. → E4: the southern counties' voice; a refusal of any war that sends them east.
- Brigham Young, secession winter: "What will King Abraham do? … God will accomplish his own purposes" (Young's words as quoted) — WAITE 174. Mormon leaders expected the nation to bleed itself and the Saints to rebuild it; Utah gave the Union fewer resources than any state or territory (95 volunteers to guard the mails) — WAITE 174. → D3, P10: Young's own reasons for leaning to Pacifica are millennial and independent; he waits on events. Fits canon (CR-060: Young will not be commanded).
- Many Southern-born leaders (Gwin, J. L. Brent) left for the Confederacy early; the rest kept the Department of the Pacific in a siege mood — WAITE 189.
- After the *Trent*, fear of a European attack on the Pacific coast was general — WAITE 190.
- Gwin's later plan (1864): a Sonora colony under Napoleon III as part of a Pacific republic that would take northern Mexico — WAITE 205. → Not for 1861; an example of the expansionist current (cf. CR-058, Juárez).
- Sources for the Oregon gap (in Waite's notes): Hull, "Movement in Oregon"; J. Ellison, "Designs for a Pacific Republic" (*Oregon Historical Quarterly*) — WAITE 289 n.67. To ask the author for.

## Lavender, *Nothing Seemed Impossible* (pp. 148–158, 1860–63)
- In November 1860 California's Unionist majority was divided between Republicans and Douglas Democrats; the Southern Democrats were a minority but united and well led, with many industrialists and civic leaders; between them stood isolationists for a Pacific Republic — LAV 150. Some Southerners backed the Pacific Republic only to keep California from aiding the North — LAV 150. → E4: the same three-way split in Pacifica, but the "isolationists" are the government.
- Ralston: New Orleans years gave him sympathy with the South; voted the Chivalry ticket; his political ally Latham talked of a Pacific Republic; emotionally an Ohioan — LAV 150–151. → P5: his divided position in 1861 makes his oath of July 1861 (canon) a real choice.
- The "prophets of doom" of 1860: the southern stage route cut, harbour blockades, Southern privateers after the gold bullion, guerrilla war if plotters struck; a Pacific Republic would upset commerce and finance — LAV 151. → C3, E3: the fears of the San Francisco houses in 1861. In Pacifica, the privateer threat to treasure at sea is a reason for C3.
- Ralston's bank: Donohoe, Ralston & Co., with Eugene Kelly & Co. as its New York house; announced on the last working day of 1860 — LAV 149–151. → P5: canon's "partner Eugene Kelly in New York" is historical.
- After Sumter, Ralston came out for the Union, organized the mass meeting of 11 May 1861 (25,000 people), chaired the Committee of Thirty-Four against treason, and built a home guard on the model of the vigilance army of 1856 — LAV 152–154. → P5 in Pacifica: a man who acts on his own when the city is afraid; the 1856 model is awkward in Pacifica, where Elias broke the committee (canon §8).
- Ralston to Latham, August 1862: "I am in favor of the powers that be" — LAV 156. → P5's political temperament: he backs the government in a war.
- The *Chapman* plot (Harpending, Greathouse, Rubery) and the fear of bombardment; San Francisco asked for two ships to guard the Gate and was refused, because the monitor *Camanche* was under contract (Ryan, Secor and Peter Donahue), its parts fabricated in New Jersey and shipped in sections round the Horn — LAV 156–158. → **Historical parallel for canon's second turret ship, shipped to Honolulu in sections and assembled on a new slip (CR-068 G2)**, and for Donahue's part (P4).

## Ellison, earlier chapters: California's grievances against Washington (E6)
- Congress left California's public lands unsurveyed and unlegislated; the Land Act of 1851 turned the old grants into years of litigation; settlers were ejected without compensation for improvements; California was refused the 5% land fund other new states got ("unfair discrimination") — ELL 4–35. Governors Bigler and others blamed federal neglect for the frontier's Indian wars, while the Union collected large taxes on the coast ("all these things … rankle") — ELL 82–91. (The frontier "Indian war" demands are the ugly side; in Pacifica CR-033's treaties of 1851–52 replace them and the Republic pays no volunteer expeditions — canon §5.)
→ **E6 reframed.** These grievances do not exist in Pacifica, because California governs its own land, gold and frontier. They are what a Union that took the coast back would bring: federal land courts over the register titles, a tax on the miners (Scott's "seignorage", ELL 182), garrisons stationed for Washington's purposes. That is the concrete content of E1 ("the lock is gone") for a Californian voter, and the best answer to the question what California fights for: its own land titles, its own royalty, its own frontier policy.
