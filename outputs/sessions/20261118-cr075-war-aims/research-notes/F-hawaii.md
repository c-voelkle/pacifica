# Pass 2 notes — Part F (Hawaii), C3, CT2

## Kuykendall, *The Hawaiian Kingdom* II
### Neutrality and the law of the sea (pp. 55–66)
- Wyllie's long project (1857–63): a "general political treaty" by which the maritime powers would guarantee Hawaii's independence and neutrality, with Bowring as roving envoy; Wyllie thought the American war proved the need; Bowring thought it an "invincible impediment" — KUYK 55–57. → P9: Wyllie's instinct is a guarantee of neutrality by many powers; in Pacifica Hawaii chose instead a confederation with a common navy (1856). The 1861 boarding tests that choice (reason 3).
- Crimean War precedent: Hawaii's neutrality proclamation of 16 May 1854 (captures in the king's waters unlawful; asylum to all belligerents' commissioned ships; **privateers barred from Hawaiian ports** except in distress) — KUYK 57–58.
- Hawaii acceded in 1855 to the Russo-American principles (free ships make free goods; neutral goods in enemy ships free) — one of only five states to do so; did not accede to the Declaration of Paris in 1856 because the United States asked it to wait for Marcy's amendment; gave the United States assurances about American privateers — KUYK 63–65; acceded in effect only in 1867, by an article of the Italian treaty — KUYK 64n. → Canon check (CR-074): the Confederation's note of 1856 keeps Paris's rules on goods and reserves letters of marque. That is Hawaii's own historical position of 1855–56. Consistent.
- **June–August 1861:** a report of a Confederate privateer in the Pacific; Wyllie wanted belligerent rights given to the Confederacy and believed it would win its independence; the king refused anything that looked like recognition, calling Wyllie "an old woman" for it in a private letter of 1 July 1861 (the king may have misunderstood Wyllie); Gregg backed the king; the American commissioner Dryer, on Seward's instructions, asked what Hawaii would do about Confederate vessels in its ports; Wyllie answered that the king had no force to make his neutrality respected and that Honolulu, with millions in American whaling property, needed an American warship present; British and French neutrality decided it; neutrality proclaimed 26 August 1861 — KUYK 65–66. → **F and D-c:** Hawaii's real dilemma of 1861 was a small neutral without a navy, asked by Washington to police the South's privateers and dependent on an American warship to do it. In Pacifica the dilemma is the same but inverted: the common navy exists, and the American warship that would "protect" Honolulu is the one that boarded its packet. Wyllie's belief that the South would win is a source for D1/D5 (P9). The king's caution about recognition matches canon's later recognition of Richmond only in May 1865.
- The American position on privateering reversed in 1861: a strong navy now wanted privateering suppressed — KUYK 66n. → E2's device works: in 1861 Washington wants neutrals to treat the South's privateers as pirates without admitting a war.
- March 1860: the first Japanese embassy to the United States put in at Honolulu in the *Powhatan*; Wyllie proposed a treaty — KUYK 67. → Canon check: in Pacifica the Japanese connection runs through Monterey (§7); the *Kanrin Maru* visit of 1860 is canon (unresolved 14).

### The war's economic effect on Hawaii (pp. 138–146)
- The Civil War cut the American whaling fleet in half; Confederate cruisers (*Alabama*, *Shenandoah*) destroyed forty-six whalers; petroleum was already undercutting whale oil — KUYK 138. The Honolulu-owned fleet (mostly American capital) peaked in 1858 — KUYK 139.
- **Sugar expanded fastest in 1861–66 because of the war:** prices rose from June 1861; Hawaiian sugar sold in San Francisco at up to 13½ cents a pound in 1864–65; American war tariffs partly offset it; San Francisco was the main market; by 1865 sugar had replaced whaling — KUYK 140–146. → **F-1, reason for Hawaii:** in Pacifica, Hawaii's sugar enters California inside the Confederation's customs union, with no American war tariff; the war is Hawaii's sugar boom. Canon's fixed-price sugar contract of 1864 and Lincoln's 1864 offer of duty-free sugar (CR-068) both turn on this.

### Victoria and the court (pp. 36, 121–126)
- Kamehameha IV made Victoria Kamāmalu kuhina nui in 1855 and Lot commander of the forces and a member of every council — KUYK 36. Wyllie's constitutional amendments of 1860–62 aimed to reduce the kuhina nui's power — KUYK 121–123. On Kamehameha IV's death (30 November 1863) some American missionaries argued for a regency under Victoria as kuhina nui until the legislature chose a king; the privy council had her proclaim Lot instead — KUYK 124–125. Lot replaced her as kuhina nui with their father Kekūanaōʻa — KUYK 126. → Canon differs by design (CR-014, CR-027: she keeps the office under Lot). The missionary party's wish to use her office is a historical seed for her standing in 1861.

## Jones, *Blue and Gray Diplomacy* — belligerency, blockade, search (ch. 1–4)
- Seward cultivated the image of a man ready for a foreign war to stop recognition, and on 1 April 1861 proposed one to Lincoln (demands on France and Spain over Mexico and Santo Domingo) — JONES ch.1 (s10.15–18). → Canon (CR-068): Lincoln refused it, with Monterey among its objects. Consistent.
- **Port closure versus blockade.** In March 1861 Seward told the diplomatic corps that foreign ships leaving Southern ports without federal papers would be seized and confiscated, as a matter of municipal law; Lyons called it a paper blockade that left foreign powers "in the Dilemma of recognizing the Southern Confederation or of submitting" — JONES ch.1 (s10.17–18). Lincoln announced a blockade, which under international law made both sides belligerents; he hoped to delay that by calling the problem domestic — JONES ch.1 (s10.30–31). Britain read the proclamation as a blockade in force and declared neutrality (May 1861), recognizing both as belligerents — JONES ch.2 (s11.2–5).
- **"The best of both worlds":** the Union denied that a war existed and called Southerners traitors, yet claimed the belligerent's rights — blockade and search — JONES ch.2 (s11.7–8). Closing only Southern ports also raised a constitutional problem (no preference among ports) — JONES ch.2 (s11.11).
- **Search is a belligerent right.** Neutrality granted the parent government a right to search neutral merchantmen for contraband and to take them before prize courts — JONES ch.2 (s11.7). The *Trent* turned on that right (Moir: "I deny your right of search") — JONES ch.3 (s12.5, s12.26). In peacetime a right of search existed only by treaty, as in the Seward–Lyons slave-trade treaty of 1862 (mutual search in African and Cuban waters) — JONES ch.4 (s13.10).

**What this means for CR-075.**
- **E2 is sound, with one caution.** Washington's doctrine in 1861 is exactly the bind E2 needs: it cannot invoke an article about "the enemies of the United States in its wars" without admitting a war, and it will not admit one. But the device cuts one way only: if Washington did admit a war, the article of 1848 would bind California to close its ports to Confederate ships. Elias should say so to Baker — he loses nothing (Pacifica bars privateers anyway, as Hawaii did in 1854) and the honesty is in character (B3: he does not rely on the other side's honesty, but he does not need to hide his own).
- **CT2 resolves toward Case A.** The reply's first demand is a wartime demand: it asks the Union to give up a belligerent's right while it blockades, which it cannot (canon G2 already says so). Peace ends the belligerent right of search by itself; after 1867 the Union also has no Pacific station. A treaty article is not needed. If Hawaii wants the principle in writing, the model is the Russo-American convention of 1854 that Hawaii acceded to in 1855 (KUYK 63) — a principle, not a ban on search.
