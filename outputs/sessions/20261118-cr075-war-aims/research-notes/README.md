## What the reading changes in the outline (proposals for the author, not yet written in)

1. **California's reasons have a historical template (E1, E4).** In 1860–61 every member of California's congressional delegation supported a separate Pacific republic at some point (WAITE 166, 189); its friends drew its eastern line at the Sierra Madre and the Rockies (Gwin, Latham: ELL 180) and its members as California, Oregon, New Mexico, Washington and Utah (Burch: ELL 181) — very nearly Pacifica's outcome of 1867. In Pacifica this current exists as a **domestic expansionist party** that wants C4 read broadly, and Elias's aims refuse to name what it wants. That gives Elias restraint as a choice, and his Council a real argument.
2. **C5 is California's condition before it is Elias's rule.** The state had about as many Southern-born as Northern-born men, and neither would fight his native section (ELL 183); the Assembly minority of 1861 would not be "the ally of one section" against another (ELL 185); the separatists knew their republic depended on the South's survival (WAITE 166, 169). A war that sends no army east and lets no American people be conquered is the only war both halves of California can support.
3. **E2 holds, with a caution.** Washington in 1861 denied a war while claiming a belligerent's rights (JONES ch. 2); it cannot invoke the article of 1848 on "the enemies of the United States in its wars" without admitting a war. But if it did admit one, the article would bind California to close its ports to Confederate ships; Elias should say so to Baker. Hawaii's own 1854 rule already barred privateers from its ports (KUYK 58).
4. **CT2 resolves toward Case A.** Search of neutral merchantmen was a belligerent right that ends with the war; in peace a right of search exists only by treaty (JONES ch. 2, 4). The reply's first demand was a wartime demand; the peace meets it.
5. **The Union's Pacific strength in 1861 can be specific (C1, D2):** six wooden sloops, under 7,000 tons and about 100 guns, guarding the gold steamers and the whalers at Hawaii; no fortifications at all in Oregon or Washington; regulars being withdrawn east by December 1861; the Northwest unable to recruit because Californian coin paid better (HUNT 26, 227–228, 234, 301–304).
6. **Hawaii's reasons (F):** a neutral without a navy asked by Washington in 1861 to police the South's privateers and depending on an American warship to do it (KUYK 65–66); in Pacifica the warship is the one that boarded its packet. And the war was Hawaii's sugar boom (KUYK 140–146) — in Pacifica inside the Confederation's customs union.
7. **The coin (D4, P5):** San Francisco's merchants in November 1862 agreed among themselves, by circular and a "black book", to treat paper only at its gold value (ELL 215) — a historical analogue of canon's Steamer Day Agreement, supporting Ralston's method over Elias's decree.
8. **Players (D-b):**
   - Sherman's real forecasts of 1861 (a long, huge war; volunteers unfit for invasion; the revenue logic of separation; "we cannot have two kings") make him the respondent who argues that the aims must be written down because the war will not stay small (SHER 106–133).
   - Beale's real roles (telling the Union's general a route was impossible; sounding the Picos on the Union versus a Pacific republic and printing their answer) map onto P10 (HUNT 22; THOM 130–131).
   - Wyllie believed the South would win (KUYK 65): a source for D1 and D5.
   - Vallejo's own grievance in our world was the loss of Californio land titles through American courts (ROS 200, 219): in Pacifica, that is what a Union return would threaten.
9. **E6 reframed.** California's grievances against Washington in our world (land courts, a miners' tax, neglect: ELL 4–35, 82–91, 182) are, in Pacifica, what a Union that took the coast back would bring. That is what California fights for in concrete terms.

## Canon checks raised (for pass 3)
- Sherman at Monterey in 1847–49 (SHER 36–48): does CR-028's Cahuenga allow it?
- The second turret ship shipped in sections (CR-068 G2) has a historical parallel in the *Camanche* (HUNT 311–313; LAV 158).
- The Confederation's 1856 note on the Declaration of Paris (CR-074) matches Hawaii's historical position of 1855–56 (KUYK 63–65).
- Shimonoseki 1863 and the Russian squadron at San Francisco 1863 (HUNT 310–311): do they happen in Pacifica?
- Edward Baker was elected senator from Oregon in 1860 in our world (THOM 128; HUNT 300); canon has him a San Francisco lawyer who never took Californian citizenship, sent as minister in 1861 (CR-068). Consistent, but his Oregon seat is gone in Pacifica and may need a line.
