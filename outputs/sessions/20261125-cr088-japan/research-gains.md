## Part 2. What the ledger says

The answer to "why do Japanese of the early twentieth century accept Pacifica?" can be put in five sentences, each resting on canon:

1. **Japan wins its wars with Pacifica and gets paid for the ones Pacifica stops** (1894, then 1898–99; 1905). In our timeline it won its wars and was robbed of their fruit by Europe (1895) and by its own exhausted treasury (1905).
2. **Japan's territory grows without colonies of rice-growers to feed it** (Enkaishū, Urajio, Karafuto, Kamchatka; Korea and Formosa left as partners). Its farmers are not broken by colonial rice, which in our timeline fed the countryside's despair.
3. **Japan's standing rises in every treaty**: equal in 1870–71, its subjects equal in the Realm from 1907, its clause in the peace of 1919, a co-equal party in 1927, the Navy seat by law in 1932. The Atlantic powers hold it at arm's length until the 1890s and longer.
4. **Japan's money is rescued and never conquered**: credit in 1870, 1873, 1897, 1923 and 1927, always lent, never a customs administration or a debt commission.
5. **Japan keeps what it cares most about**: the Emperor in Japan, the home army, the *koseki*, its schools and its language (`A4.3`). It refuses Pacifica's constitution in 1871 and its home-army request in 1932, and both refusals stand.

The partnership is therefore a bargain that a Japanese statesman can defend in the Diet on Japanese grounds. The grievances of step 8 are the price, and each has its holders.

**The thread of Japanese argument for `A2`**, one voice per era: Nariakira (1850s), Kido and Itō (1871), Saionji (1907 and 1927), Takahashi (1927 and 1932). All four are already in canon. A historical figure who could give the argument a public, non-official voice is **Nitobe Inazō**, who in our timeline told his examiners in 1883 that he wished to be "a bridge across the Pacific", became the League's Under-Secretary-General and is remembered for that phrase (*Hist.*). He is optional, and the cast rule of D-17 would have him as background unless the author wants a scene.
