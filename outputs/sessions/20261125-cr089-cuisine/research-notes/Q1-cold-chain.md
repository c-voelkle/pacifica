# Pass 2 notes — Q1: the cold chain

Source: Rees, *Refrigeration Nation* (REES, cited by EPUB chapter segment: ch10 = book ch. 2, ch12 = ch. 4, ch13 = ch. 5, ch15 = ch. 7, ch16 = ch. 8). Paraphrase throughout; *Hist.* = our timeline. "→" marks what it could mean for Pacifica (proposals, not canon).

## Mechanical refrigeration (REES ch10)

- Carré's aqua-ammonia absorption machine (1859) was the world's most used until 1875 and drove the first meat exports from Australia and South America; Linde, a Munich professor working with brewers, made anhydrous ammonia compression viable from 1877 and licensed his designs rather than building them (REES ch10.7, 10.9).
- Ammonia compression won the American market: 74% of ice plants in 1899, 86% in 1909, because ammonia was cheap and efficient. Carbon dioxide machines (cheap refrigerant from breweries, non-toxic, copper pipe) held only ships, hotels and restaurants, where safety counted (REES ch10.8–10.10).
- Ammonia's danger shaped public opinion for decades: the fire at the Columbian Exposition's ice plant (1893, seventeen dead) was blamed on an ammonia explosion, plants leaked badly (an estimate of 1919 put avoidable losses at 65% of the charge), and fire insurance for mechanically refrigerated warehouses cost two to three times more (REES ch10.1–10.2, 10.10; ch13.4).
- What shrank machines was not new science but systematic practice, automatic control after 1890 and above all the switch from steam to outside electric power; small hotel, butcher and dairy machines spread around 1900 (REES ch10.17–10.18). A trade journal from 1891 spread best practice until by about 1908 every make gave much the same results (REES ch10.18).
- → **The Realm's fit is unusually good.** It makes its own ammonia on a large scale from the 1910s (`CR-054`, `CR-082`: the Columbia's ammonia) and is the most electrified country on earth (`L81`: electric cooking in nearly every home by 1929). Electric-driven refrigeration in Pacifica can come a decade before the Union's, and the Polytechnic's habit of publishing a method (the Linde model: design and license, never manufacture) suits the open-licence rule of `CR-057`. A refrigeration engineering school at Carquinez, and a published safety code for ammonia plants after a Pacifican disaster, are natural (PROPOSED).

## Refrigerated transport (REES ch12)

- California is the American case par excellence. Fruit cars (ventilated, no ice) ran east from the 1860s; the Santa Fe sent ten or eleven a day in the 1886 season; iced refrigerator cars replaced them only slowly, into the 1890s (REES ch12.18–12.19).
- The pomologist G. Harold Powell invented precooling in 1904 (cool the fruit or the car before loading), which cut ice use and spoilage (REES ch12.19). [He is a different Powell from canon's John Wesley Powell.]
- The packers (the Beef Trust) controlled produce cars until the railroads' Pacific Fruit Express (1906: 6,000 cars; 40,000 carloads in 1908) took the trade; PFE ran the largest ice plants on the coast by 1924. Imperial Valley cantaloupes: 237 carloads in 1905, 21,500 in 1931, a week to Chicago (REES ch12.19–12.20).
- Three-quarters of the cars went back to California empty (REES ch12.18). Mechanical refrigeration on rail came only in the 1950s; ice stayed too cheap (REES ch12.21).
- Fruit shipped green never tasted as it should: "New York … never has known how good California fruit ought rightfully to be" (*Ice*, 1909; REES ch12.21).
- Oysters: seed oysters from New York reached San Francisco Bay by rail from 1869–70 and had to be renewed because they would not spawn there, until Japanese oysters came later (REES ch12.16). This confirms `CR-089` A3's correction on the Pacific oyster.
- Before portable ice machines (about 1920) a fishing boat's range was the life of its ice (REES ch12.16).
- → **In Pacifica the fruit is the Realm's own, and it goes west as well as east.** The Realm needs no Beef Trust stage: the state-owned trunk rights of way (`CR-057`) and a common-carrier car pool can be in place from the 1890s. Its markets are its own cities, the Union by treaty, and across the Pacific to Japan, which makes refrigerated shipping the decisive link. The 1909 complaint about green fruit is the opening for a Pacifican pride: fruit eaten ripe in the Realm, where it grows, is one of the things visitors come for (Q8).

## Cold storage and trust (REES ch13)

- Wiley (Bureau of Chemistry) praised cold storage but attacked its abuse: holding food back to raise prices, and storing what should be eaten fresh (butter, milk, eggs, poultry, fish). His tests of 1910 cleared stored food, but the equipment varied widely before 1914 (REES ch13.1–13.2).
- The press campaign of about 1911 ("Cold Storage Evils") and the attempts at federal regulation failed; the states passed labelling laws instead (5 states in 1911, 11 in 1915, about 15 by 1920, after the Uniform Cold Storage Law of 1914), and the industry came to like them because they hit only the dishonest (REES ch13.11–13.12).
- Hoover's Food Administration licensed the warehouses and took reports of all stored food during the war; the war made cold storage "our best friend" when stored produce broke winter prices in 1918 (REES ch13.11). [Canon's Hoover is Pacifica's food administrator (`CR-051`).]
- The rest of the world lagged; English plum growers let fruit rot in gluts because they had no cold stores (REES ch13.13).
- Capacity: 620 warehouses in 1904, about 1,000 in 1916, 1,710 in 1925 (REES ch13.12).
- → **This is where Pacifica's transparency becomes a food technology.** A cold-store date label is exactly the label "that says only what is true" of `CR-076`; the Realm would have the labelling rule from the start, and the Office would print the stocks held in store each month, as Hoover's administration required in the war. Published stocks make speculation by storage unprofitable, which was Wiley's real complaint. PROPOSED: the stores' monthly returns as one of the Office's county tables (`CR-077`: monthly from 1931; earlier quarterly).

## The household refrigerator (REES ch14–16)

- Electric household refrigerators were unreliable and dear in the early 1920s (a General Electric study of 1923 advised against entering the market); average price $600 in 1920, $275 in 1930, $152 in 1940 (REES ch15.1–15.3, 15.9).
- Leo Szilard and Albert Einstein applied for 29 German refrigerator patents between 1924 and 1934 (REES ch15.2). [Canon: Szilárd is inside the Works from about 1924, `CR-050`.]
- Adoption: 1.1% of wired homes had one in 1925, 9.4% in 1930, 17.1% in 1932, 56% in 1940; refrigerator sales passed iceboxes in 1930 (REES ch16.2).
- Quick freezing: Birdseye's insight in Labrador (small crystals from fast freezing keep taste and texture) and his multiplate patent of 1927; General Foods bought him in 1929; frozen food needed its own cold chain, stores' cases and home freezers, and was not profitable until the 1950s (REES ch16.9–16.10). Locker plants from the mid-1930s (REES ch16.11).
- → **The Realm's electrified homes make the refrigerator a 1920s household good, not a 1940s one.** With the Union's 1930 share as a floor, the Realm could plausibly have a refrigerator in a third to a half of its wired homes by 1932 (PROPOSED; not modelled). Szilárd's refrigerator, which in our timeline went nowhere for noise, is a tempting small detail (PROPOSED, background). Quick-frozen fish fits a fishing coast and an ammonia industry: frozen salmon, halibut and tuna for the interior and for Japan in the late 1920s (PROPOSED). The dairy growth that `CR-089` A1 now relies on rests partly on this: fresh milk, butter and ice cream need the household cold chain.
