## What the books give

- **Yuan Mei's *Recipes from the Garden of Contentment* (1792)** (DUB pp. 144–147): local produce, season, the cook's knowledge; a list of the cook's sins (artifice and showmanship, carelessness, following fashion, overcooking, everything in one pot), and no drinking until the dishes are tasted. Its recipes were copied freely from older books (p. 146–147).
- **Sun Yat-sen:** "China lags behind in all areas of modern civilization, but in the realm of cuisine we have no peer" (DUB p. 169). Qin Meng's complaint twenty years later that Western food is simple and raw (pp. 169–170).
- **Macao's cuisine of substitution** (DUB pp. 162–163): turmeric for saffron, coconut milk for dairy, chouriço for Chinese sausage; steamed egg-and-milk desserts.
- **Condensed milk and ice cream in China** (DUB pp. 165–166, 169): 34,000 tins a year into Canton by 1880; ice cream a craze in 1920s Shanghai, with the city closing insanitary makers.
- **Harbin as "China's Chicago"** (DUB p. 180): chilled and frozen meat by rail from the 1900s.
- **Chop suey's 1920s craze** (DUB pp. 173–174). (The Li Hongzhang story of 1896 is not in DuBois; general knowledge, a legend.)
- **Shanghai's fancaiguan** (SWIS pp. 97–99, 108, 112): Chinese-owned Western restaurants run mostly by Cantonese entrepreneurs, with chefs from Hong Kong, dominant at the high end in the 1890s; Fourth Avenue had as many as all regional Chinese restaurants together. The Fourth Avenue fire of 12 June 1899 and the tabloid's "Fire God eats Western food": Jiangsu and Zhejiang elites' resentment of Cantonese money. Courtesans as the craze's patrons.
- **The regional hierarchy and its losers** (SWIS pp. 146–167): the 1912 guide lists ten regional kinds of restaurant; Sichuan's Zuiou the most exclusive in 1912–13; by the late 1920s Cantonese houses the most expensive and fashionable, "the Revolutionary Army of the restaurant industry" (Wang Dingjiu); a team of Japanese chefs judges Cantonese cooking the best in the world; Fujian houses called complacent, "fallen behind in the ranks"; Fujian's sailors' dockside restaurants with chefs who had cooked for admirals (p. 167). Ningbo and Cantonese cliques (*bang*).
