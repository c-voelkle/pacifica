# Pass 2 notes — Q2: cooking as a profession

Sources: Trubek, *Haute Cuisine* (TRUB, printed pages; the OCR is noisy, quotations checked against the text), and Barr (RITZ). Paraphrase; "→" marks proposals for Pacifica.

## Who the French cooks were (TRUB ch. 5, pp.67–86)

- French chefs exported their cuisine as cultural nationalism ("France is the mother land of the art of eating", Philéas Gilbert, 1902) and were the most employable artisans in the world: whole careers abroad in British, Irish, Russian and American houses and hotels (TRUB pp.67, 77, 85–86).
- They came from the provinces, apprenticed at thirteen to sixteen, unpaid or paying, moved from kitchen to kitchen and city to city; Joseph Favre (1849–1903) took science and nutrition courses at Geneva, wrote a universal dictionary of cooking (1894) and founded the Union Universelle pour le Progrès de l'Art Culinaire (1879) (TRUB pp.69–70, 94).
- At home their status was low: a *métier manuel*, classed with workers, not with pharmacists. Escoffier recalled that from 1860 to 1895 the cook "was not well regarded" though cooking is "a science and an art" (TRUB pp.68, 92). Orwell found cooks the least servile hotel workers, "un ouvrier", proud of their skill (TRUB p.76).
- No food school entered France's new technical system (Écoles des Mines, Centrale, Ponts et Chaussées): cooking did not fit a curriculum of abstract science (TRUB p.77).

## The attempt to make a profession (TRUB ch. 6, pp.87–109)

- **Journals:** *L'Étoile* (1868, mutual aid), *L'Art Culinaire* (1883 to the 1920s; the Société des Cuisiniers Français' organ, subscribers in America and Algeria; Escoffier on its committee), *Le Progrès Gastronomique* (1884, the union chamber's) (TRUB pp.85, 94, 99–101).
- **Associations:** the mutual aid society of 1868; the Société des Cuisiniers Français (April 1882), whose two aims were a professional school and culinary exhibitions to fund it; the union chambers after the law of 1884, a cooks' congress in 1887 and a national federation (TRUB pp.94, 99–101).
- **The school:** the École Professionnelle de Cuisine et des Sciences Alimentaires (Rue Bonaparte) had an elementary course with a certificate, a superior course with diplomas, a public course for girls and housewives, scholarships, a model kitchen, library and museum, and an endowment. It admitted only French citizens aged fourteen to twenty. The state gave only "moral" patronage; the Paris gas company gave an oven. It closed within two years (1893–94) with money still in the bank, because apprenticeship went on alongside it and owners did not want to lose unpaid apprentices (TRUB pp.104–107).
- England had started a school for boy cooks in the 1870s precisely to break the French hold on its kitchens (TRUB p.104).
- **Exhibitions:** annual in Paris from the 1880s, London from 1885 (Willis's Rooms, Édouard Pouard), Tours 1892; long tables of set pieces with trade stalls (Bovril, Shredded Wheat) round the edges; gold, silver and bronze medals from the minister of commerce, apprentices' classes. They were judged on display, not taste: a temple of royal icing on a wooden frame lost its prize because the frame was not edible; nobody tasted the aspic (TRUB pp.98, 113–114, 121–122).

## → What Pacifica takes and what it refuses

1. **The cooks' school inside the technical system.** What the French cooks begged for and never had, the Realm would have: food science and cookery as a branch of the Polytechnic's examined schools, beside assaying and agriculture (PROPOSED; e.g. a School of Food at Carquinez or Davis, with Hilgard's soils and the brewing and cold-storage engineers). It must not be a state plan of diet (author, 06:58): the school teaches method and tests skill; it does not tell anyone what to eat.
2. **Apprenticeship paid, not bought.** The French school died because owners wanted free apprentices. The Realm's labour article (`CR-071`: the same wage for the same work, school to fourteen) already rules out unpaid apprentices, so the school does not compete with free labour (PROPOSED).
3. **Open to all, by examination.** The French school took French citizens only, to protect a closed market. The Realm's rule is the examination, never origin (`CR-037`), so the school admits the Cantonese cook's son, the Basque herder's daughter and the Japanese navy cook's apprentice alike.
4. **Judged by taste, blind.** The French exhibitions judged what could be seen; the Realm's contests (`CR-089` A4) are tasted blind and scored, with the scores published. This is the sharpest single difference between Paris's model and Pacifica's, and it follows from the Realm's habit of measuring (PROPOSED: a Pacifican exhibition of the 1880s that copies Paris and is laughed at for judging sugar temples, after which the guilds switch to blind tasting).
5. **Women in the profession.** Paris kept haute cuisine male and gave women the *cordon bleu* courses for the home (TRUB pp.85, 105). The Realm's examined women (`CR-049`, `CR-077`: the operators and assayers) suggest women master cooks from the start (PROPOSED).
6. **A Pacifican trade journal** on *L'Art Culinaire*'s model, read on the coast and in Tokyo (PROPOSED).
