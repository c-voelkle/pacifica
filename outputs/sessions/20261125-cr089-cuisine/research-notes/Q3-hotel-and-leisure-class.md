# Pass 2 notes — Q3: the grand hotel and the leisure class

Source: Barr, *Ritz and Escoffier* (RITZ, cited by EPUB chapter segment: ch5 = book ch. 1 … ch22 = ch. 18, ch23 = afterword). Paraphrase; "→" marks proposals for Pacifica.

## How the elite moved (RITZ ch5, ch7)

- The leisure class was "an international tribe", following seasons: London's social season spring to summer; Baden-Baden and the Swiss Alps in summer; Cannes, Nice and Monte Carlo in winter and early spring. Ritz could run hotels in all of them because the seasons did not overlap (RITZ ch5.2, ch7.4).
- Railways made the seasons: the Calais–Mediterranean Express (1886, with restaurant car and lavatories) and the Orient Express brought "throngs" to the Riviera (RITZ ch5.1–5.2).
- Ritz's capital was a clientele: the Prince of Wales's set, the Continent's royal families, opera and theatre stars, and the American and European financiers ("the Vanderbilts and Morgans … the Rothschilds"). "Where the prince went, others invariably followed" (RITZ ch5.4).
- The American heiresses of the 1890s (Consuelo Vanderbilt, Mary Leiter) brought new money into the season and believed "in the value of advertisement" (RITZ ch15.2).
- → **A Pacifican season has to fit the calendar.** The Riviera owns the European winter; the Alps and Baden-Baden own the summer. California and Hawaii are winter places too, but a month's voyage from London. The Realm's natural season is therefore the northern winter for Americans of the Union and for Japan's court and financiers, and a once-in-a-lifetime tour (a winter or a year) for Europeans, with the transcontinental lines, the Pacific liners and the southern wire making it possible. It needs its own "prince": a royal patron whose stays others follow (Q8).

## What the grand hotel invented (RITZ ch5–ch9, ch19)

- **Technology as luxury, and teaching the guest to trust it.** The Savoy (opened 6 August 1889): electric light in every room day and night, switched from the bed; six American lifts, so the top floor cost the same as the first; speaking tubes from every floor to the kitchen; 67 bathrooms when the Hotel Victoria had four for 500 guests (RITZ ch5.6). D'Oyly Carte had smashed a lit bulb under muslin on his theatre's stage to prove electric light safe (RITZ ch5.7).
- **The model was San Francisco.** On his American tours D'Oyly Carte was most impressed by the Palace Hotel in San Francisco: five lifts and more than four hundred bathtubs, built in 1875 (RITZ ch5.6). [In our timeline the Palace was Ralston's project and opened after his death; in canon Ralston lives and thrives (`CR-073`).]
- **No extra charges, no tips.** D'Oyly Carte forbade tips at his theatre, gave programmes and coat check free, served undiluted whisky and real coffee; the hotel advertised "No charge for Baths, Lights, or Attendance" against the old English hotel's fee for everything (RITZ ch5.7).
- **The kitchen moved upstairs and became a profession.** Escoffier's kitchen at the Savoy was on the ground floor, sunlit, electrically lit, new; no shouting (the *aboyeur* became the *annonceur*), no drink, no smoking, clean whites, street clothes outside (RITZ ch8.1–8.3).
- **The brigade:** six *parties* (saucier, rôtisseur, poissonnier, entremétier, garde-manger, pâtissier) under a sous-chef; an order divided at once among stations; order to table fell from fifteen minutes to five; dishes simpler ("faites simple") and served hot, for à la carte service à la russe (RITZ ch8.5–8.7).
- **The menu problem.** English diners could not read French menus or compose a meal, so Escoffier built a personal seven-course prix fixe for parties of four or more, and kept files on each regular's tastes so as never to repeat a dish unasked (RITZ ch8.7–8.8). A cheaper set-price *salle à manger* opened beside the grand room (RITZ ch8.8).
- **Women in public.** Women did not eat in restaurants; Ritz set out to end the club's monopoly. Lady de Grey's all-women dinner at the Savoy (1890), first behind screens, then without them, made mixed dining fashionable; "it was women who would make the restaurant fashionable" (RITZ ch9.13). At the Ritz in Paris (1898) every detail was designed for women: indirect light, apricot-lined shades tested on Marie Ritz, hooks for handbags, wardrobes and drawers for dresses and false hair (RITZ ch19.1–19.3).
- **Music and Sunday.** The Sunday dinner became the Savoy's showpiece; Johann Strauss's orchestra was hired to make diners linger (RITZ ch11.6).
- **Celebrity dishes.** Pêche Melba for Nellie Melba; frogs' legs as "Nymphes à l'Aurore" for the Prince of Wales (RITZ ch11.6, ch16.1).
- **The press.** Newnham-Davis's restaurant column in the *Pall Mall Gazette* reviewed the Savoy (RITZ ch16.3); the telephone booked tables.
- **Codification.** Escoffier co-founded *L'Art Culinaire* in 1883, wanted a pocket dictionary so kitchen and waiters agreed on what a dish was, and wrote "L'École des Menus" (RITZ ch16.1); the *Guide culinaire* came out of this (RITZ ch18).

## The fall of 1897: commissions (RITZ ch15.5–ch16.3)

- Gross kitchen profit at the Savoy fell from about 35% to 24.5% while receipts rose. Deloitte's auditors and private detectives found that Escoffier took a 5% commission on suppliers' orders ("common knowledge in the shop"), resold wine and cigars through a middleman, and that the grocer covered the kickbacks by delivering 30–40% short (450–500 eggs for 700 ordered, hams underweight); waiters charged for flowers not ordered, headwaiters took bribes for tables. Ritz, Escoffier and Echenard were dismissed (RITZ ch15.5–16.3).
- → **This is the counter-image Pacifica's kitchens define themselves against.** The commission was "how restaurants operated". A Realm that prints its accounts and weighs everything (`CR-073`'s Assay Office, `CR-089` A4's weights and measures) would treat the short delivery as fraud by statute and the kitchen's purchasing as an audited trust. PROPOSED: the guild's master cooks swear, as a condition of the title, never to take a supplier's commission; the hotels' and halls' purchase books are open to the guild's inspectors. A Pacifican cook trained at the Savoy in the 1890s would carry home both the brigade and the memory of the scandal.

## → Pacifica proposals from this book (for the synthesis)

1. **San Francisco's Palace as the world's first modern luxury hotel** (1875, Ralston's), a model D'Oyly Carte copied: the Realm starts in front, not behind.
2. **The no-tip, no-extra-charge rule** is D'Oyly Carte's in our timeline; in the Realm it is the norm (fits benchmarked pay, `CR-038`; see Q4 on tipping).
3. **The brigade arrives with the guild fellows of the 1890s** (`CR-089` A4), and the Realm's kitchens are electric from the start (`L81`), so the cool, clean, sunlit kitchen is the ordinary Pacifican kitchen rather than a showpiece.
4. **Women dine in public in the Realm earlier than in London**, because the Realm's examined women (operators, assayers, the Kaʻahumanu Society) already have public lives; a Pacifican Lady de Grey moment is unnecessary, or happens in the 1870s (PROPOSED).
5. **A Pacifican restaurant press** on the Newnham-Davis model, printed by the press estate (`CR-079`), rating by neighbourhood (`CR-089` A3).
