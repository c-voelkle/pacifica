# Pass 2 notes — Q4: the restaurant and the middle class

Source: Haley, *Turning the Tables* (HAL, printed pages). Paraphrase; "→" marks proposals for Pacifica.

## The argument (HAL pp.1–3, 222–226)

- In the Union of 1880 the best restaurants and hotels served French food cooked by French chefs, as the upper class's claim to aristocracy; between 1880 and 1920 the new urban middle class (clerks, managers, professionals) challenged it and won: menus in English, many cuisines, women served freely, less formality, attention to health and comfort. Delmonico's closed in 1923; a Chinese tearoom opened in the McAlpin, then the world's largest hotel, in 1919 (HAL pp.1–3, 192–193, 222–223).
- The cosmopolitan restaurant had no benchmark; the middle class answered with restaurant reviewing, Progressive "expertise and investigative journalism" applied to eating (HAL pp.223–224, 228).

## San Francisco, the cosmopolitan capital (HAL pp.89, 96–101, 113–114, 150, 228)

- In the 1850s ethnic restaurants already fed the Gold Rush's bachelors (Chinese, German, Italian); in 1868 Noah Brooks found no "distinctive local dish" but a fare that was "cosmopolitan", and noted that single men kept the restaurants alive (HAL pp.96, 113, 148).
- Charles Greene (*Overland Monthly*, 1892): the French table d'hôte gave soup, salad, fish, entrée, roast, fruit, cheese, coffee and half a bottle each, cheaply; "one may obtain all the benefits of the grand tour without leaving San Francisco" (German-Jewish, Mexican, Italian kitchens) (HAL pp.100–101, 113).
- Roland Whittle (1903): the city's fame rests on its humbler restaurants, "the pride of the city and the wonder of tourists"; Clarence Edwords, *Bohemian San Francisco* (1914): the city is "the Mecca for lovers of gustatory delights", a walk from Japan to China to Mexico to France to Italy in an afternoon (HAL pp.89, 113–114, 228).
- A federal study of the 1920s: in San Francisco "it is possible to eat in any language" (HAL p.114).
- Women: the "Home" Coffee and Lunch Rooms advertised in 1888 as the only quiet downtown restaurant for ladies and gentlemen, with ice cream as the sign of respectability (no alcohol) (HAL p.150).
- → **The book confirms `CR-089` A3's premise from our own timeline:** San Francisco was already the Union's most cosmopolitan table, famous for cheap, good, many-tongued eating rather than for one great restaurant. In the Realm, with no Exclusion and a third of the population of Asian origin by 1932, that reputation is larger and earlier, and it is the city's draw for visitors (Q8).

## The menu's language (HAL ch. 8, pp.192–221)

- The French menu was a barrier of class: those who could not read it could not take part, and the middle class saw it as a "conspiratorial effort to exclude" them; *The Cook* (1885) demanded "short, sensible, scientifically arranged and English worded" bills of fare; by 1920 the English menu had largely won (HAL pp.193–195).
- → **The Realm prints menus in the language of its diners**: English with the dish's own name beside it, and the county's language where the county has one (Spanish in Pima and Baja, Hawaiian in the Hawaiian counties; `CR-071`). A French name is the dish's name, not a disguise. PROPOSED: the press estate's and the guilds' rule that a menu states what is in the dish and its price, the same "label that says only what is true" (`CR-076`).

## Tipping (HAL ch. 7, pp.171–191)

- Before the Civil War Americans did not tip routinely; rich travellers brought the European habit home after it. Tipping bought the rich better service, better cuts and the waiter's discretion; Veblen's "unproductive consumption of service". *The Cook* (1885) called it an "evil system", worst in "first-class" places (HAL pp.172–174).
- Middle-class campaigns against tipping failed because restaurateurs profited: tips hid the real price of a dinner. The middle class turned to fantasies of technology: Daschner's "Automatic Table Service Apparatus" (patent 1917), the waiterless restaurant, ordering on an electric tablet and paying in silence (HAL pp.171, 188, 190–191).
- → **In the Realm tipping never takes root** (PROPOSED). The arguments are all Pacifican ones: a tip hides the price, buys unequal service and bribes discretion, and the Realm's waiters have benchmarked pay (`CR-038`). D'Oyly Carte's "no charge for attendance" (Q3) is the Realm's ordinary practice. The waiterless restaurant is a natural Calculation Office curiosity (an order board wired to the kitchen, the bill printed at the table; `CR-077`'s exchanges from 1893), but as a novelty, not a replacement for service (PROPOSED, background).

## Women and the restaurant (HAL ch. 6, pp.145–170)

- Restaurants were male preserves for most of the nineteenth century; women dining alone were suspect; bans on women smoking fell about 1919 (HAL pp.148, 169).
- → See Q3: the Realm's examined women lead the change earlier.
