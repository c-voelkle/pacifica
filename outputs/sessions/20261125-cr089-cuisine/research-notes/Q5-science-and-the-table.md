# Pass 2 notes — Q5: science and the table (the Union's diet, 1880–1930)

Source: Levenstein, *Revolution at the Table* (LEV, printed pages). Paraphrase; "→" marks proposals for Pacifica.

## Prices and processors (LEV ch. 3, pp.30–43)

- Railways and refrigerated cars remade the Union's diet: Swift's Chicago dressed beef from 1879, corn-finished beef (the "Golden Age of American Beef" of the mid-1880s), California's first carload of fresh fruit east in 1869, iceberg lettuce in 1903 (LEV pp.31–32).
- Prices fell: one dollar bought 15 lb of flour in 1872 and 34 lb in 1897; between 1872 and 1898 a dollar bought 43% more rice, 51% more coffee, 114% more sugar, 60% more lard and butter (LEV p.32). [This is the falling-price world `CR-057` gives the Realm on purpose.]
- Trusts followed (the Sugar Trust), and the processors learned that once people have enough calories, every change in diet is a substitution of one food for another; advertising fights for shares of a fixed stomach (LEV pp.32–33, 152).
- Breakfast cereal came out of the Battle Creek sanatorium, "a thriving health resort" for the rich and famous (LEV p.33).
- → **Two lessons for `CR-089` A1.** First, corn-fed beef and lard are the Union's diet because of the corn belt's prices; the Realm, with no corn belt, never gets that table cheap (confirms A1's cause 1). Second, the substitution rule is exactly how the Realm's diet shifts without a plan: a fixed stomach, and the cheapest good thing wins.

## The New Nutrition (LEV chs. 4–7, pp.44–97)

- Atwater (Wesleyan; federal experiment stations from 1887) built the calorimeter of the 1890s and the calorie-per-dollar arithmetic; the New England Kitchen (Boston, from 1890, Ellen Richards) tried to teach the poor cheap scientific food and failed because immigrants would not eat it (LEV pp.44–59, 73–74).
- Cooperative kitchens (Bellamy, 1889; Melusina Fay Peirce in Cambridge) were tried and failed as "too individualistic" for American families (LEV pp.65–67). [Melusina Fay Peirce was C. S. Peirce's first wife; canon's Peirce is in Pacifica from his youth, `CR-049`.]
- → **The Realm measures without preaching.** The Office's household survey (`CR-089` A1, 1925–27) is the Realm's equivalent of Atwater's dietary studies, but the Office publishes and does not teach a diet. The New England Kitchen's failure is the warning: a state that tells immigrants what to eat is ignored.

## Hoover's Food Administration (LEV ch. 11, pp.137–146)

- In 1917–18 the Food Administration used advertising methods (slogans, free articles, half a million volunteers, pledge cards in windows), home economists in every state, "wheatless" and "meatless" days, and appeals to patriotism rather than health; Hoover feared food faddists (LEV pp.139–141).
- It failed among immigrants and the poor: they already ate little meat, ethnic restaurants ignored the meatless days (of 18,900 eating places in New York only 8,000 were English-speaking), a Yiddish pledge card asked Jews to eat shellfish (LEV pp.142–143).
- The wartime survey suggested the middle class ate more fruit, vegetables and milk (LEV p.141).
- [Canon: Hoover is Pacifica's food administrator in the war (`CR-051`), and there was no Pacifican relief of Belgium.] → In the Realm Hoover's administration works through contracts and published stocks (Q1), not pledge cards; the Realm's mixed table already eats little beef and pork, and a campaign telling Chinese, Japanese and Mexican Pacificans to save meat would be absurd (PROPOSED: Hoover learns this in 1917 and drops the pledge idea after one try).

## The Newer Nutrition and milk (LEV ch. 12, pp.147–160)

- After 1914 the vitamins: McCollum's "protective foods" (milk and leafy vegetables, 1918), vitamin A in milk; mothers came to see milk as "a magic potion", reinforced by clean-milk campaigns and the Children's Bureau (1914); deficiency diseases themselves were rare among the middle class, but vitamins promised "positive health", growth and long life (LEV pp.149–150). Pellagra's cause was known by the mid-1920s but not officially admitted until the 1930s, for political reasons in the cotton South (LEV p.149).
- Growers' associations advertised: Sunkist oranges, Sun-Maid raisins ("Had Your Iron Today?"), California walnuts, the Canners League of California made asparagus known in the 1920s; Fleischmann's yeast as a vitamin cure; health claims shading into falsehood (LEV pp.151–153).
- → **This is the evidence for `CR-089` A1's dairy line.** The 1920s rise in milk, butter and cream that keeps the Office on its first column is the Newer Nutrition's rise in our timeline, plus the Realm's cold chain (Q1). In the Realm the claims are tested: the Assay Office and the Polytechnic's food chemists test health claims on complaint, and a false label is fraud (`CR-089` A6). PROPOSED: the Realm's growers' cooperatives exist (Sunkist is a California cooperative of 1893 in our timeline) and advertise, but under the marks law their claims must be true.

## Restaurants in the 1920s (LEV ch. 15, pp.183–193)

- Prohibition broke the Union's high French restaurants (they lived on wine), though "islands of Francophilia" survived in San Francisco and New Orleans; restaurants tripled in number after 1919, with cafeterias (the name from Chicago in the 1890s), luncheonettes and soda fountains in old saloons, and tea rooms that men began to use (LEV pp.185–187).
- [Canon: the Union never adopts national Prohibition (`CR-076`), so in the Union the high French restaurant survives longer than in our timeline; the shift to cheap, mixed, cosmopolitan restaurants comes through the middle class (Q4), not Prohibition.]
