# Pass 2 notes — Q6: Japan's modern kitchen

Source: Cwiertka, *Modern Japanese Cuisine* (CWI, printed pages). Paraphrase; "→" marks proposals for Pacifica.

## Western food as state ceremony (CWI ch. 1, pp.13–34)

- From 1869 the Emperor's birthday was marked by a French banquet for diplomats; the Tsukiji Hotel (1868, French chef) and then the Tsukiji Seiyōken (1872, owned by Iwakura Tomomi's former steward) supplied imperial banquets until the Imperial Hotel (1890); Western banquets were "officially designated for state ceremonies" in 1873, to impress foreigners and to borrow the West's authority at home (CWI pp.13–15).
- → **Hayama's dinner (CR-089 C7) has its precedent:** the court's state dinners were French by policy for sixty years. In the Realm's story the question is whose produce and whose wine stand on that French menu. After the Iwakura mission's weeks in Pacifica (`CR-071` D), a Pacifican wine at the Emperor's birthday banquet in the 1870s is plausible (PROPOSED).

## Hotels, treaty ports and tourists (CWI ch. 2, pp.35–55)

- Western residents and travellers distrusted Japanese food (Isabella Bird, Knollys, Townsend Harris) and lived on tins; the treaty-port hotels (Yokohama from 1860, the Grand Hotel 1873; Nagasaki's Commercial House run by Caroline Wicks of San Francisco) fed them, and a hotel's name depended on its dining room (CWI pp.36–40).
- Japanese cooks trained in foreign households and hotels became the founders of Western gastronomy in Japan; Edo already had a dense, guide-book-rated restaurant culture (CWI pp.41–42).
- From the late 1870s inns at resorts and spas served foreigners; the Fujiya Hotel at Miyanoshita (1878) in Hakone, then Nikkō, Kamakura, Kyoto (CWI p.43).
- Department stores opened dining rooms (Shirokiya 1904, Mitsukoshi 1907); by the 1920s every store had two restaurants and a tea shop, Western in decoration and run for hygiene and speed (CWI pp.50–52). *Yōshoku* restaurants created a new "culinary language" with Western vocabulary and Japanese syntax (CWI p.50).
- → **Japan's resort hotels are the Realm's model and its rival for the same travellers.** The round-the-world traveller of 1880–1930 who came for Hakone and Nikkō came through San Francisco and Honolulu. A Pacific circuit (San Francisco, Monterey, Honolulu, Yokohama, Hakone, Kyoto) is the natural route of the prestige tour (Q8).

## The military kitchen and beriberi (CWI ch. 3, pp.56–86)

- Canning came to Japan through state laboratories and through Hokkaido: salmon canneries at Ishikari (1877) and Bekkai (1878) under Ulysses S. Treat of Eastport, Maine, a member of Horace Capron's Kaitakushi mission; from 1880 they trained canners from all over Japan (CWI p.62).
- The army's ration of 1916 rested on canned beef (*yamatoni*, beef simmered in soy, ginger and sugar), ship's biscuit, soy extract and pickles; 99.98% of the canned beef bought in the war of 1894–95 was *yamatoni*, three-quarters made at home (CWI p.64).
- Conscripts were small: 16.7% fell below the 151.5 cm minimum in 1900 (CWI pp.63–64).
- Beriberi: in 1883, 120 of every 1,000 sailors had it; the navy's Beriberi Research Committee; Takaki (Takagi) Kanehiro, trained at St Thomas's in London, put the *Tsukuba* on a British-style diet in 1884 and **the ship reached Hawaiʻi with no case on board**; the navy then changed its whole ration (CWI pp.68–69).
- The army reduced beriberi cheaply by mixing barley with rice (Osaka division 1884, widespread by 1891); Mori Rintarō (Ōgai), believing it infectious, ended the barley ration on becoming Surgeon General in 1907; Suzuki Umetarō showed the vitamin link in 1910; rice and barley 7:3 became the standard only after 1925 (CWI p.69).
- → **Correction for `CR-089` A7 and set piece C1:** the *Tsukuba*'s proof ended at Hawaiʻi. The Realm's surgeons need not go to Tokyo in 1885; the ship's arrival at Honolulu in 1884 with a clean sick list is where they see it (PROPOSED; to integrate in revision 4). The Japanese army's twenty years of refusal (1907–1925 at its worst) is a real conflict the Realm watches; Mori Ōgai stays background.
- → **The Pacifican canner in Hokkaido.** Treat's role in our timeline invites a Pacifican one: Columbia or Alaska salmon canners at Ishikari and Bekkai in the late 1870s, under the Hokkaido exchange of 1893's forerunners (PROPOSED; check against `CR-085`'s Hokkaido exchange).

## Japanese food abroad (CWI postscript, pp.181–199)

- Emigration began with Hawaiʻi's sugar plantations; California's Japanese community carried the skills on which Japanese restaurants abroad were built; Europe had only 3,696 Japanese in 1930 (CWI pp.182–183, 190).
- → In the Realm, with about 5.8 million Japanese-origin residents by 1932 (MODEL), Japanese cooking is not a foreign cuisine but one of the Realm's own; sushi, tempura and sukiyaki houses are ordinary in the coast's cities long before 1932 (PROPOSED).
