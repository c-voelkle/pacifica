# Pass 2 notes — Q7: fermentation, koji and flavour

Sources: Fruin, *Kikkoman* (KIK, printed pages); Lee, *The Arts of the Microbial World* (LEE, EPUB chapter segment: ch8 = book ch. 1 "Sake and Shōyu", ch9 = ch. 2 "Nutrition", ch11 = ch. 4 "Alcohol", ch13 = ch. 6 "Flavor"). Paraphrase; "→" marks proposals for Pacifica.

## The Noda soy brewers (KIK chs. 1–3)

- Kikkoman won medals at home (1881) and a gold medal at Amsterdam (1883), and **was registered as a brand in California in 1879, six years before Japan had trademark protection** (KIK p.60).
- The Noda cartel's brewers built small laboratories; from 1904 they jointly funded one of Japan's first industrial research laboratories (a college-trained brewing chemist, an engineer and three assistants by 1907), which analysed the brands chemically, distributed a pure koji mould of uniform quality to every member, taught the brewers, and by 1921 published the results of 53 tests. Noda's output rose 80% between 1904 and 1913 (KIK pp.72–74).
- Suzuki's quick-brewed soy sauce was test-marketed in Hawaiʻi in 1906 before his company of 1907 opened two plants; a government food inspection in November 1910 found saccharin far beyond brewed sauce ("EVIDENCE OF FRAUDULENT SHOYU"), he dumped his stock in Osaka Bay and was bankrupt within a year (KIK pp.71–72).
- The Noda Shoyu Company (1917, merged 1925) made shōyu, mirin and miso in Japan and Korea, with its own bank, railway and freight lines (KIK pp.123, 126). Before the war about a tenth of output was exported, a fifth of that each to Hawaiʻi and to North America, for the Japanese communities; 99% of shōyu exported to the United States in 1939 carried the Kikkoman mark (KIK pp.274, 286).
- → **For the Realm:** (1) Hawaiʻi and the coast are Japan's soy-sauce market from the 1870s; with Japanese-origin Pacificans counted in millions, brewing moves to the Realm, the first breweries perhaps in Hawaiʻi and the Sacramento Valley in the 1880s–90s (PROPOSED), with Noda's houses as partners or rivals under the Concord's industrial annexes. (2) Kikkoman's California mark of 1879 fits `CR-089` A6 exactly: a Realm that registers foreign marks honestly attracts the world's food brands to register there first. (3) Suzuki's saccharin is the Realm's textbook case for the Assay Office's tests of foods on complaint (PROPOSED: the case is read in the Polytechnic's food course).

## Brewing science in Japan (LEE ch8)

- The national Brewing Experiment Station (decided 1902, opened in Tokyo in 1904 under the Ministry of Finance, because sake was the largest tax) ran a network of regional stations, surveyed breweries, trained *tōji* (master brewers) and promoted pure-cultured yeasts distributed by the Brewing Society; Yabe Kikuji had isolated the first sake yeasts in 1893 (LEE ch8.9, 8.15). Its advocates (Kozai Yoshinao) set science-based training against the *tōji*'s unwritten craft (LEE ch8.13).
- → A Pacifican parallel: brewing and fermentation as a branch of the Realm's agricultural colleges (Davis, Hilgard's station) with a station for wine, beer, sake and shōyu cultures. Under `CR-076`'s duty the Realm also has a fiscal reason, as Japan did (PROPOSED).

## Takamine and koji in America (LEE ch11.5–11.6)

- Takamine Jōkichi (Imperial College of Engineering; later the discoverer of adrenaline) invented Takadiastase in 1894 by growing koji on waste wheat bran; it out-converted malt, and he tried to replace malt with koji in American whiskey distilling (LEE ch11.5–11.6). The Japanese army's Uji plant then used bran koji for industrial alcohol from sweet potatoes in the war of 1904–05 (LEE ch11.5).
- → **Takamine is the obvious Pacifican scientist of fermentation** (born 1854; in our timeline in the United States from 1890). In the Realm he works in San Francisco, and his koji process for industrial alcohol and starch conversion is licensed openly (`CR-057`) (PROPOSED; candidate for registration, author's decision).

## Nutrition and synthetic foods (LEE ch9)

- After the rice riots of 1918 Japanese scientists treated microbes as a food resource: synthetic sake (Suzuki Umetarō's Rikenshu at Riken) to save rice, chemical soy sauce, yeast preparations, synthetic vitamins; Suzuki had found the anti-beriberi factor ("oryzanin") in 1910 (LEE ch9.1, 9.3).
- [Canon: Japan's rice never falls short of its need (`CR-085`), and there are no rice riots in 1918.] → The interwar push to save rice by chemistry is weaker in canon's Japan; Suzuki's vitamin work stands.

## Flavour (LEE ch13)

- Ajinomoto made MSG by extraction from hydrolysed vegetable protein for half a century; in 1956 Kyōwa Hakkō found a microbe that ferments glutamic acid (Udaka Shigezō, Kinoshita Shukuo), which made it far cheaper and was licensed to Merck in 1958 (LEE ch13.1, 13.7).
- → **An option for the Realm's technology level** (`CR-077`: the world of about 1951 by 1932): glutamate by fermentation in the late 1920s at a Pacifican–Japanese laboratory would be the food-science counterpart of the Calculation Office's lead. It is a large divergence and should be the author's call (PROPOSED as an option, not recommended by default).
