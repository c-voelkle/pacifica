Generated by `population/flows.py`. Each step below is a full cohort run with the same engine and the same vital rates (case `canon`), so each step is exactly the people it adds, with their descendants. At 1846 every step is zero. By 1932, migrants arriving after 1846 and their descendants are **34,252,255** of the model's 35,245,920 people (97%). Territories joined in 1856–1867 and their descendants are 806,620; the 1846 Californians and theirs are 187,045.

Extra early arrivals: the sheet's 1848–1860 arrivals from the Americas, Europe and China × 1.00, the multiplier that reaches DEM-01's 1.2m in 1861 (`[dem01]` in assumptions.toml). Model in 1865: 1,814,445 (DEM-01 tests about 1.5m). Sheet − model is what the sheet has that the model does not: the sheet's flat growth rate against age structure, minus the extra early arrivals the sheet lacks.

Our timeline on the same territory: each territory from its canon join year, growing at its real census growth (partial areas — Montana camps, Western Slope, SW Wyoming, Baja — at the whole state's growth). Censuses before 1890 missed most Native people. It counts our timeline's own migration, so the ratio compares the sheet with what really happened, not with a closed population.

## Reading this

- The chain separates the sheet's growth into its sources; each column is the people that source accounts for.
- The US comparison shows the scale of the claim, not its impossibility: the canon diverts part of the US flow (the Labour sheet's diverted immigration), and some streams, such as Chinese family migration, have no US counterpart because the US restricted them. Whether each stream's size fits its canon cause is the author's call.
- Natural increase uses the rates case named at the top; canon rates are benchmarked on the best real populations of the period (New Zealand mortality, Canadian fertility).
